# Learning Path — A Hybrid Spiral Through Western Philosophy

This is the route through the library. It replaces the old era-by-era march (Stages 0–5 in about a year) with a design based on how introductory curricula actually sequence this material (MIT OCW 24.01, Oxford Prelims, Yale's paired Ancient/Modern sequences) and on what the learning-science literature says actually sticks (Dunlosky et al. 2013 — see `sources.md`).

Read the time budget honestly before you start: **this is a 15–19 calendar-month plan, not a one-year plan**, unless you take the trimmed version at the end. That is not padding. It is what ~30 authors in primary text, read slowly enough to reconstruct their arguments, actually costs alongside a job.

---

## 1. How the plan works

### 1.1 A chronological spine, five threads on a spiral

**Spine:** you move forward in time, era by era, because later authors are literally replying to earlier ones. Hume is hard to understand without Locke and Descartes; Kant is an answer to Hume; Nietzsche presupposes Schopenhauer and Kant; Foucault presupposes Nietzsche. Chronology is not tidiness — it is what makes the arguments intelligible.

**Spiral:** at every era you revisit the same five problems, so each new author is also a new answer to a question you have already met:

1. **Knowledge** — What can we know? What defeats scepticism? Is induction justified?
2. **Mind** — What is a mind/self, and how does it relate to body and world?
3. **God** — Does God exist? What follows either way for meaning, morality, death?
4. **Ethics** — How should I live? Virtue, duty, consequences, freedom, authenticity?
5. **Politics** — How should we live together? Authority, justice, liberty, power?

These five are the working subset of the nine recurring questions mapped in [themes.md](themes.md) (which adds metaphysics, logic & language, aesthetics and science). Each stage below names its thread focus, and each stage ends with a short **spiral return** (30–45 minutes): re-answer one earlier thread question using the new era's authors. That return is not optional garnish — interleaving old and new material is one of the few study techniques with real evidence behind it, and it is what stops Stage 1 evaporating by Stage 3.

### 1.2 Two tiers: core and map-level

The library covers 98 figures. **You will not read 98 figures in depth, and the plan does not pretend otherwise.** University semesters don't either — MIT's covers about ten figures in primary text.

- **Core (~30 figures):** read in primary text, in the editions named below, slowly, with a written argument reconstruction for each. These are the load-bearing authors: skip one and later stages wobble.
- **Map-level (everyone else):** cover through their entry in this repo + the paired Adamson episode or Kenny section + **one checkpoint question** you can answer in your own words (≈30–45 minutes per figure). Map-level is a deliberate design choice, not a failure. It gives you the shape of the whole tradition — who argued with whom, about what — which is exactly what makes the core reading intelligible.

Core figures are marked ●, map-level figures ○, and map-level figures with an optional short primary excerpt worth doing if the stage is going well are marked ◐.

### 1.3 The three non-negotiables

1. **Never meet Kant, Hegel, Heidegger or late Wittgenstein before their named predecessors.** Kant after Hume. Hegel after Kant (and Fichte at map level). Heidegger after Husserl at map level. *Philosophical Investigations* after *Tractatus* excerpts and the positivists it argues against. Starting with the famous hard book is the single most common way self-taught learners quit.
2. **Every core author = one argument reconstruction in your own words, before any opinion.** Premises → conclusion, book closed, then one paragraph of critique — critique always second, never first.
3. **Do not study by rereading and highlighting.** Dunlosky et al. (2013) rated rereading and highlighting *low utility*, summarising alone likewise. The two techniques rated *high utility* — practice testing and distributed (spaced) practice — are built into the weekly rhythm below. If your review session is re-reading marked pages, you are doing the thing the evidence says doesn't work.

### 1.4 Weekly rhythm (4–5 hours)

| Session | Time | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| **A — Primary** | ~2 hrs | Primary text only, slow, with reconstruction notes. No lectures, no videos, no podcast in this slot — ever. |
| **B — Support** | ~1.5 hrs | Secondary support for this week's author (see §3 for timing), finish the reconstruction, write the argument cards. |
| **C — Retrieval** | ~1 hr (weekend is fine) | Spaced review of *previous* authors' cards, the spiral return, and the weekly checkpoint (3 prompts, book closed). Session C is always about earlier stages, never the current page. |

Adamson episodes on walks or commutes count as Session B support. They never count as a primary-reading session.

**Stage discipline:** you may not extend a stage by more than 2 weeks. If a gate isn't passed, use a buffer week (below) — do not quietly live in one era for six months because it's comfortable. Era tunnel vision is a failure mode, not a personality trait.

## 2. The retention system (30 minutes/week of overhead, no teacher needed)

This system is part of the plan, not an accessory. It is what the checkpoints in §4–§9 assume you are doing.

1. **Argument cards, not fact cards.** One deck (Anki or paper) for the whole plan, cumulative across all stages. Front: a *do*-prompt — “Hume — induction: state the problem in 3 sentences.” Back: numbered premises + conclusion + **one objection + one reply**. About 5 cards per core author, ~150 cards total. Review each author's cards at **+2 days, +1 week, +3 weeks, and stage end**. A card that asks “when was Kant born?” is a wasted card; dates live in `index.md`.
2. **Blank-page reconstruction after every core text.** 200–400 words, book closed, the argument rebuilt in your own words and in a clearer order if you can — explaining it to someone who hasn't read the passage, not paraphrasing sentence by sentence. Then, and only then, a second paragraph critiquing it. (This is Jim Pryor's standard undergraduate exercise, and his reading rule goes with it: read three times — skim for conclusion and structure, read carefully, then evaluate.)
3. **Weekly book-closed checkpoint.** In Session C, answer 3 prompts from your current stage's checkpoint list (§4–§9), aloud or in writing, book closed. Score honestly against four levels: *can state / can reconstruct / can compare / can object*. Only the last three count as passing. “I recognise it when I see it” is *can state* at best, and usually not even that.
4. **One gate essay per stage: 600–800 words.** A modest thesis, defended clearly. A strong argument for a weaker thesis beats a weak argument for a grand one. **No gate essay = no advancing to the next stage**, however much you have "read." Prompts are suggested at each stage; any question from that stage's checkpoints, argued properly, also works.
5. **Discussion substitutes, in order of value.** Solo study's weak point is that no one pushes back. Climb this ladder as needed: (a) record a 5-minute phone voice memo teaching the week's argument aloud — if you can't teach it, you don't have it yet; (b) steelman exchange — write the strongest objection to your gate essay *as the opposing author*, then reply; (c) post the gate essay or a reconstruction to a philosophy forum, reading group, or a scheduled AI-critique session with the instruction "find the weakest premise; do not summarise me back" — and treat anything it says as a discussion partner's claim to be verified against SEP or the primary text, never as authority; (d) Magee's interview series (*Men of Ideas*, *The Great Philosophers*) after each era block, as a way of hearing a second voice before you write.

## 3. Secondary support — and when to use it

Timing matters. Read a full SEP entry *before* a hard primary text and its interpretation hardens into prejudice: you will find in Nietzsche exactly the nihilist every introduction told you to find. The rule:

- **Before (20–40 min only):** orientation — who, when, what question, key terms. Stop there.
- **Alongside:** the companion paired with that week's reading.
- **After:** the interpretive and debate layer, once you have a reconstruction of your own.

| Resource | When | How to use it |
|---|---|---|
| **Adamson, *History of Philosophy Without Any Gaps*** (podcast, free; also OUP books) | **Alongside, every figure, all stages** | ~20 min per figure, chronological, and the best match for this library's scope — it covers the lesser-known, Islamic, Jewish and women philosophers other histories skip. This is your main map-level engine. Breadth companion, not an argument tutor: you still owe the reconstruction. |
| **Kenny, *A New History of Western Philosophy*** (4 vols / 1-vol ed.) | **Alongside, one volume per era** | The default history spine for this plan (Vol. 1 with Stage 1, Vol. 2 with Stage 2, Vol. 3 with Stages 3–4, Vol. 4 with Stage 5). Its structure — narrative, then thematic chapters on knowledge, mind, ethics, politics, God — *is* the spiral in print. Prefer it to Russell as your spine. Caveat: the Islamic world appears mainly where it feeds the Latin tradition; Adamson fills that gap. |
| **SEP (Stanford Encyclopedia)** | **Before (§1/intro only) and After (full entry)** | Peer-reviewed and expert, but long and pitched at advanced readers — it is rarely assigned whole to introductory students, and you shouldn't assign it whole to yourself either. Protocol: §1 before the primary text, stop, read the primary text, then the rest. |
| **IEP (Internet Encyclopedia)** | **Before, preferentially** | Shorter and written at undergraduate level; the better first pass for beginners. Use IEP first for Spinoza, Kierkegaard, Hegel, Heidegger; graduate to SEP after. |
| **Russell, *A History of Western Philosophy* (1945)** | **After primary reading only, Stages 3–5, selectively** | Brilliant and openly biased — empiricist favouritism, dismissive on Aquinas and on figures he disliked. Read him as a philosopher to argue with, never as your sole authority on an author, and never as a Stage 0 textbook. (His *Problems of Philosophy*, 1912, is a different book and an excellent Stage 0 text.) |
| **Copleston, *A History of Philosophy*** | **After / reference only** | The tie-breaker: when Kenny and SEP seem to disagree on a figure, look up Copleston. Thorough and famously fair even to positions he rejects. Do not attempt it cover-to-cover inside this plan. |
| **Magee interviews** | **After each era block** | Consolidation and a discussion substitute (§2.5d). |
| **Per-author companions** | **Alongside, only where flagged below** | Feser on Aquinas (Stage 2); Scruton's *Kant: A Very Short Introduction* (Stage 3); Blackburn on the *Republic* (Stage 1, after Book VII); Leiter or Magee/Stern on Nietzsche (Stage 4, after a first pass at the *Genealogy*); Sandel's *Justice* lectures alongside the Stage 5 capstone. |

**Two-interpreter rule for contested figures** (Nietzsche, Marx, Aquinas, Heidegger, Beauvoir): before you form a view, have two independent interpreters — Kenny/Copleston plus SEP/IEP, or Russell plus his critic. One verdict, quoted twice, is still one verdict.

## 4. Editions — buy by translator, not by cover

Cheap reprints of public-domain Victorian translations are the stealth failure mode of self-study: the book is "the same text" and quietly unreadable, or quietly wrong. Rules:

- **Translator + series must be named before you buy.** If the title page names no modern translator and editor, don't buy it.
- Default series: **Hackett** (cheap, student notes, standard on US syllabi) and **Cambridge Texts in the History of Philosophy** (translations commissioned for students, with introduction and glossary). Penguin Classics and Oxford World's Classics are good *when the translator is modern and named*.
- Per-author editions are given in each stage. The consolidated avoid-list:

> **Avoid / flagged:** Jowett's Plato (1871–92 — Victorian, flowery, often lacks Stephanus numbers in reprints); Allan Bloom's *Republic* except comparatively; Haldane & Ross for Descartes (1911 — renders *cogitatio* inconsistently and obscures the argument; the Cambridge edition exists to replace it); Selby-Bigge/Nidditch for Hume's *Enquiry* (superseded by Beauchamp's Clarendon edition); Kemp Smith (1929) or Meiklejohn for Kant's *Critique*, and any unlabelled Gutenberg *Critique*; Thomas Common's Nietzsche in cheap reprints; **Parshley's *Second Sex* (1953 — abridged by ~15% and mistranslates key terms; use Borde & Malovany-Chevallier, 2010/2011)**; the full *Summa Theologiae* in the old Dominican Fathers translation as a *first* read (it's a reference set, not a course text); Ross's Aristotle translations as a first read (accurate but terse and unhelped); the public-domain Marriott Machiavelli; Barnes's *Being and Nothingness* for technical terms without checking Richmond (2021). And one non-edition warning that belongs here: ***The Will to Power* is not a book by Nietzsche.** It is a posthumous editorial construction from his notebooks, assembled by his sister's circle. Read it, if at all, as notebooks — never cite it as his considered view.

## Stage 0 — Tools & Problems (3 weeks · 12 hrs)

**Purpose:** learn what an argument *is* before meeting 2,500 years of them, and meet all five threads in miniature so the spiral has hooks to hang on.

**Study order**

1. **One intro book, whole, and only one:** Thomas Nagel, *What Does It All Mean?* (1987, ~100 pp) — or Simon Blackburn, *Think* (1999), chs. 1–3 — or Russell, *The Problems of Philosophy* (1912). ● (as a tool, not a figure)
   Library orientation alongside: [Russell](eras/05-20th-century/bertrand-russell.md).
2. **The vocabulary of arguments:** valid vs. sound, premise vs. conclusion, *a priori*, necessary vs. contingent, charity (state an opponent's view so they'd accept your statement of it before you attack it). Jim Pryor's guidelines on reading and writing philosophy (free online, under an hour each) — read both now.
3. **A problem sampler, one per thread:** Knowledge — a Gettier-style case (justified true belief that isn't knowledge). Mind — Nagel's "what is it like to be a bat?" in miniature. God — the problem of evil as a triad. Ethics — the trolley problem and Nozick's experience machine. Politics — who should rule, and what would you accept behind a veil of ignorance? No reading list for these yet; the point is to have *your* first answer on record. You will re-answer all five in the capstone.
4. **Set up the system (§2):** card deck, commonplace/reconstruction file, and the rule that Session A is primary-only.

**Secondary support:** none beyond the intro book. Do not "prepare" by watching ten hours of overview videos; that is failure mode 1 (§11) arriving early.

**Exit checkpoints — you can:**
- □ State the difference between validity and soundness, and construct a valid-but-unsound argument of your own.
- □ Reconstruct one short argument premise-by-premise (Nagel's bat point works) in ~200 words, book closed.
- □ Given a fresh paragraph of philosophy, mark its premises and its conclusion correctly.
- □ Define *valid, sound, a priori, necessary* without notes.

**Gate essay (600–800 words):** your current answer to one of the five sampler problems, argued — not surveyed. Keep it. The capstone grades your progress against it.

## Stage 1 — Ancient Foundations (13 weeks · 60 hrs)

**Threads in focus:** Ethics (virtue as knowledge vs. virtue as habit) and Metaphysics/Knowledge (Forms vs. Aristotle on substance); the God and Mind threads start quietly (soul, death). **Spiral return at stage end:** the death question twice — *Phaedo* vs. Lucretius.

**Secondary support & timing:** Adamson alongside, episode per figure (his ancient sequence is the natural companion). Kenny Vol. 1 alongside. Blackburn's *Plato's Republic* commentary **after** Book VII, not before. Kagan's Open Yale *Death* lectures pair with the *Phaedo*/Lucretius block if you want a lecture voice — Session B only.

**Exit checkpoints — you can:**
- □ State the Euthyphro Dilemma and why it still matters for divine-command theory.
- □ Reconstruct the Allegory of the Cave *as an argument* (not a story summary), plus one objection to it.
- □ Contrast Plato vs. Aristotle on forms/universals in five sentences.
- □ State Aristotle's function argument (*NE* I.7) and the doctrine of the mean (II.6), one example each.
- □ State Epicurus' "death is nothing to us" and Lucretius' symmetry argument, plus one reply to each.
- □ Explain Epictetus' dichotomy of control with a modern application — and one criticism of it.

**Gate essay:** "Is virtue knowledge, habit, or neither?" — Plato/Socrates vs. Aristotle, with the Stoics or Epicurus as your third voice.

**Known stall point:** the *Republic*'s length. The fix is built in: the MIT selective-books route above. Do not "push through" all ten books and arrive at Aristotle exhausted.

## Stage 2 — Medieval & Renaissance Bridge (8 weeks · 36 hrs · compressed)

**Do not skip this stage, and do not expand it.** Skip it and Descartes' God-proofs, the scholastic vocabulary in every early-modern text, and Locke/Kant on substance become unintelligible. Expand it and you'll still be in the 14th century in month ten. Eight weeks, held firmly.

**Threads in focus:** God (ontological vs. cosmological arguments; faith/reason) and Knowledge (what reason can prove unaided). **Spiral return:** the problem of evil, from Stage 0's triad to Augustine's answer.

- □ State Anselm's ontological argument premise-by-premise, Gaunilo's lost-island parody, and Anselm's reply.
- □ Reconstruct Aquinas' First and Third Ways, and state the objection Hume and Kant later press against cosmological arguments.
- □ Explain Augustine on time (*Confessions* XI) in five sentences.
- □ State the problem of evil as a triad and give one Augustinian response (evil as privation) — and say what it doesn't solve.

**Gate essay:** "What can reason prove about God unaided — everything, something, or nothing?" Anselm vs. Aquinas vs. one of the bridge figures.

**Known stall point:** Aquinas' form. Fix is above: excerpts in a modern student edition + Feser alongside, Q2 first. If you are reading the *Summa* cover-to-cover, you have left the plan.

## Stage 3 — Early Modern to Kant (16 weeks · 72 hrs · the load-bearing stage)

The longest stage, and deliberately so. **Do not compress Stage 3** (§10's trim cuts everywhere else first). Every later stage spends the capital earned here: Nietzsche assumes your Kant, analytic philosophy assumes your Hume, political philosophy assumes your contract theorists.

**Threads in focus:** Knowledge (rationalism/empiricism as *one conversation*, not two teams; induction), Mind (dualism and its discontents), Politics (the social contract, three ways). **Spiral returns:** scepticism, now with Descartes answering Montaigne's Pyrrhonism; the God thread, as the ontological and cosmological arguments get their early-modern rerun and Hume's audit.

| 6 | [Locke](eras/03-early-modern/john-locke.md) | ● | *Essay* Book II (experience, ideas, primary/secondary qualities), II.xxi, II.xxvii (personal identity) → *Second Treatise* chs. 2, 5, 7–9. | 9 | [Hume](eras/03-early-modern/david-hume.md) | ● | In this order: (1) *Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding* **whole** — §§1–3, 4–5 (induction), 7 (causation), 10 (miracles), 12 (scepticism) are the core; (2) *Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion* sel./whole; (3) *Enquiry Concerning the Principles of Morals* sel.; (4) *Treatise* I.iv selections **only after** the *Enquiry*. | 10 | [Rousseau](eras/03-early-modern/jean-jacques-rousseau.md) | ● | *Discourse on Inequality* Part II → *Social Contract* I–II. Edition: Cress (Hackett, combined volume) or Gourevitch (Cambridge Texts). |
| 11 | [Adam Smith](eras/03-early-modern/adam-smith.md), [du Châtelet](eras/03-early-modern/emilie-du-chatelet.md), [Wollstonecraft](eras/03-early-modern/mary-wollstonecraft.md) | ○ | Map-level. Smith's moral philosophy (sympathy, the impartial spectator) is the Politics/Ethics thread's quiet fourth voice — entry + Adamson + checkpoint; a *Theory of Moral Sentiments* excerpt is the best buffer-week spend in this stage. |
| 12 | [Kant](eras/03-early-modern/immanuel-kant.md) | ● | **Sequence is the whole game:** (1) "What is Enlightenment?" (a few pages); (2) *Groundwork* §§1–2, then §3, **whole**; (3) *Prolegomena* Preface + selections; (4) *Critique of Pure Reason* **selections only**: Prefaces, Introduction, Transcendental Aesthetic, the Analytic's core concepts, with Paralogisms/Antinomies taken in summary; (5) *Critique of Practical Reason* sel. on a later pass, optional. Editions: *Groundwork*, Gregor rev. Timmermann with Korsgaard intro (Cambridge Texts) or Wood (Yale) — MIT's Ellington (Hackett) is older but serviceable; *Critique*, Guyer & Wood (Cambridge). **Not Kemp Smith, not Meiklejohn, not Gutenberg.** Study guide: [*Groundwork*](study/kant-groundwork.md). |

**Secondary support & timing:** Kenny Vol. 3 alongside. IEP **before** for Spinoza and Kant (the two places beginners most need a plain-language map first); SEP §1 before, full entry after, per §3. Scruton's *Kant: A Very Short Introduction* alongside the Kant block, and Guyer on Kant in selection if the *Prolegomena* stalls. Russell's *History* may now be read **after** the primary texts, on Descartes-to-Hume, as an opponent — this is the first stage where §3's "Russell after" rule activates.

**Exit checkpoints — the hardest gate; a repeat week is allowed and expected by many learners:**
- □ Reconstruct Descartes' cogito, and state why the "Cartesian circle" is charged against him.
- □ State Locke on primary/secondary qualities and on personal identity, and Berkeley's "master argument" reply to material substance.
- □ **State Hume's problem of induction and one reply** (Kant's, Popper's, or a Humean naturalist reply). This checkpoint is the centre of the stage — if you can only pass one, pass this one.
- □ State Hume on causation as constant conjunction, and on miracles (the maxim, quoted or closely paraphrased).
- □ State Kant's categorical imperative in two formulations, apply both to a lying-promise case, and contrast the result with Mill (whom you'll meet properly next stage — the contrast is the spiral warming up).
- □ Explain *synthetic a priori*, and "thoughts without content are empty, intuitions without concepts are blind," in plain English, with Descartes and Hume as the foils Kant is answering.

**Gate essay:** "What, if anything, justifies our expectation that the future will resemble the past?" Hume's problem, and at least two replies, one of them Kant's.

## Stage 4 — Nineteenth Century: System, Revolt, Society (11 weeks · 48 hrs)

**Threads in focus:** Ethics and Politics fused (freedom, history, utility vs. duty revisited with real opponents), God as *critique* (religion explained, not just argued). **Spiral returns:** Kant's ethics, now facing Mill and Nietzsche; the contract tradition, now facing Marx and Tocqueville.

- □ Reconstruct the master–slave dialectic in ~300 words, and say what Marx takes from Hegel and what he inverts.
- □ State Mill's harm principle and one case where it is genuinely hard to apply.
- □ Reconstruct Marx on alienated labour and on commodity fetishism, with one objection to each.
- □ State Kierkegaard's "teleological suspension of the ethical" (Abraham) and why it scandalises Kant.
- □ Reconstruct *Genealogy* Essay I (master/slave morality) and Essay II (guilt, bad conscience) *as arguments*, and distinguish Nietzsche's critique from the claim "morality is relative."

**Gate essay:** "Is morality a discovery, an invention, or a symptom?" Mill, Marx and Nietzsche give three different answers; defend one against the other two.

## Stage 5 — Twentieth Century to Present: Two Traditions + Capstone (13 weeks · 60 hrs)

**Split the stage; do not intermix randomly.** Analytic and continental philosophy after 1900 are two conversations with different founding documents and different styles; alternating them weekly produces two half-conversations. Do 5A, then 5B, then the capstone that forces them back into one room.

**Threads:** all five, at full speed — which is the point. The capstone (§9.3) is a spiral return to Stage 0.

### 9.3 Capstone (final 2 weeks, inside Stage 5's 60 hours)

**The capstone essay: ~2,000 words.** Take **one** of Stage 0's five problems — knowledge, mind, God, ethics, politics — and answer it again, citing **at least 6 primary authors across at least 3 eras**, including at least one author you initially disagreed with, treated charitably. Mark it against three criteria: (1) reconstruction accuracy — would each author accept your statement of their view? (2) one genuine objection to your own position, considered, not waved at; (3) charity to opponents throughout.

Then put it next to your Stage 0 gate essay on the same problem. The distance between the two documents is the actual product of this plan; the reading was the method.

Submit it somewhere it can be answered: a forum, a reading group, a meetup, or the AI-critique protocol in §2.5(c) ("find the weakest premise; do not summarise me back" — then verify every factual claim it makes). An essay no other mind has pushed against is a draft, whatever its word count.

**Stage 5 exit checkpoints — you can:**
- □ Contrast Frege and Russell on definite descriptions, with one example sentence analysed both ways.
- □ State the *Tractatus* picture theory and the *Investigations'* "meaning as use" reply, with the builders example or the beetle in the box.
- □ State Quine's attack on the analytic/synthetic distinction and why it threatens positivism from inside.
- □ State Sartre's "existence precedes essence" and Beauvoir's application *and* critique of it in the *Second Sex* introduction.
- □ Set up Rawls' original position, derive the two principles, state Nozick's Wilt Chamberlain objection, and state Singer's contrasting pond-principle argument.
- □ Capstone submitted to at least one external reader (human or §2.5(c) protocol).

## 10. Buffer weeks, the 12-month trim, and the comfortable version

**Buffer weeks (+8, already in the calendar total):** for illness, holidays, and exactly two sanctioned academic uses — re-reading Hume's *Enquiry* or a second Kant pass. A buffer week spent starting a new author is not a buffer week; it's stage extension, and §1.4's 2-week extension cap applies.

**12-month trim (≈250 hrs / 50 weeks at a firm 5 hrs/week, no buffer).** If a year is a hard constraint:
- Stage 2 → 24 hrs: Augustine + Anselm + Aquinas in primary text; *everyone* else in the stage, Machiavelli and Montaigne included, drops to map-level.
- *Republic* → Books I–II, VI–VII only (−~6 hrs from Stage 1).
- Stage 5B: Heidegger and Foucault → summary-only (map-level, no excerpt pass) (−~8 hrs).
- **Do not trim Stage 3.** Not the Kant sequence, not Hume whole, not the *Meditations* in order. A 12-month plan that cuts Stage 3 produces a learner who has "done" philosophy and cannot follow an argument in Nietzsche or Rawls — the trim exists to protect the load-bearing stage, not to distribute pain evenly.

**18-month comfortable version (+40–60 hrs):** add the whole *Phaedo*; *Nicomachean Ethics* VIII–X whole (friendship and contemplation — MIT's Ancient course assigns them, and they're the Ethics thread's ancient high point); the *Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion* whole; an Avicenna/Averroes/Maimonides excerpt pass in Stage 2 (the compressed stage's acknowledged debt); a Plotinus excerpt pass (*Enneads* I.6, V.1) at the end of Stage 1; and a second, slower Hegel or Sartre pass. This is also the version where post-plan projects belong: Kuhn whole, Parfit's *Reasons and Persons* in selection, *Capital* I beyond ch. 1 with a companion.

## 11. Failure modes — check monthly

Any "yes" triggers the fix *before* new reading. These are ordered roughly by how often they end self-study plans.

| # | Failure mode | Diagnostic (yes = problem) | Fix built into this plan |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Secondary-only study** | No whole short primary work finished in the last 3 weeks | Gates require primary-text reconstructions; secondary is capped at Session B, never Session A (§1.4) |
| 2 | **Opinion-first notes** | Your notes are quotes and highlights, with no premises → conclusion in your words | Blank-page reconstruction per core author (§2.2); critique paragraph always second |
| 3 | **Rereading & highlighting as review** | "Review" means re-reading marked pages | Dunlosky et al.: low utility. Replace with book-closed recall + spaced argument cards at +2d/+1wk/+3wk (§2.1, §2.3) |
| 4 | **Starting too hard** | You've read <50% of a book in >4 weeks and can't state its thesis | Named predecessor gates (§1.3); Kant/Hegel/Heidegger/Wittgenstein sequencing rules in Stages 3–5; "selections only" is a design feature, not a compromise |
| 5 | **Wrong or hostile edition** | Your copy's translator died before ~1950 and no modern editor is named | §4's buy-by-translator rule and avoid-list (Jowett, Haldane & Ross, Parshley, Common, Kemp Smith, unlabelled Gutenberg) |
| 6 | **Era tunnel vision** | Current stage extended >2 weeks; your last five authors are all one century or school | Hard stage calendar (§1.5); the 2-week extension cap (§1.4); weekly spiral returns force cross-era comparison |
| 7 | **No writing** | No gate essay for the last completed stage | No-essay-no-advance (§2.4). The capstone is an essay, not a reading list |

## 12. Do-not-start-here list (unchanged, and worth restating)

"Advanced" means *needs the stages above plus a companion*, not "needs more intelligence":

- Kant, *Critique of Pure Reason* — full text, before the Stage 3 sequence
- Hegel, *Phenomenology* whole; *Science of Logic* at all, solo
- Spinoza, *Ethics* Part I unaided
- Heidegger, *Being and Time* unaided (and see Stage 5B's honest fallback)
- Aquinas, *Summa* beyond the selected questions; Scotus in primary text
- Nietzsche, *Zarathustra* as a first Nietzsche; *The Will to Power* as a Nietzsche "book" at all
- Hume, *Treatise* before the *Enquiry*; Plato, *Republic* before the early dialogues; Wittgenstein, *Investigations* before the *Tractatus*
- Derrida's 1967 books unaided
- Marx, *Capital* Vol. I beyond ch. 1 without a companion or reading group
