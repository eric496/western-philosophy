# Quotes in Context

Famous philosophical lines get quoted as free-floating slogans, but almost every one of them is doing a
specific job in a specific argument, often in someone else's mouth. This list gives each line its work,
location, speaker, and the sentences around it, plus a handful of lines that are not in the text at all.
Method: every "Exact wording" below was read in a full public-domain or publisher-preview text at the
cited location; where that was not possible, the entry says "wording not verified" and paraphrases
instead of quoting.

## Ancient

### "You cannot step twice into the same river"
- **Source:** Heraclitus, fragment 41–42 in Bywater's numbering (as reproduced in John Burnet's translation, *Early Greek Philosophy*, 1912; Diels–Kranz B12), 6th–5th c. BCE, as collected on Wikisource's "Fragments of Heraclitus"
- **Speaker:** Heraclitus, in his own voice (we have only the fragment, quoted by later authors — here via Diogenes Laertius / the doxographic tradition)
- **Exact wording:** "You cannot step twice into the same rivers; for fresh waters are ever flowing in upon you."
- **Context:** This is not a lament about change erasing identity, and not a mystical "nothing is real" slogan. Heraclitus's point is that stability and change are not opposites: the river keeps its name and course (the *logos* that structures it) precisely because its water is always different. A companion fragment (81) sharpens it: "We step and do not step into the same rivers; we are and are not" — same river, different water, both true at once.
- **Entry:** [Heraclitus](eras/01-ancient/heraclitus-of-ephesus.md)

### "Man is the measure of all things"
- **Source:** Protagoras, as quoted by Plato, *Theaetetus* 152a (written c.369 BCE; Protagoras himself wrote c.5th c. BCE), tr. Harold North Fowler (Loeb/Perseus Digital Library)
- **Speaker:** Socrates, summarizing a line of Protagoras, in conversation with Theaetetus
- **Exact wording:** "man is 'the measure of all things, of the existence of the things that are and the non-existence of the things that are not.'"
- **Context:** Socrates offers this as another way of stating Theaetetus's own definition of knowledge as perception: things are, for each person, as they appear to that person. Socrates goes on to test this claim relentlessly — including the famous objection that it makes Protagoras's own view no truer than its denial — so the line is the opening move of a critique, not Plato's endorsement of "everyone's truth is equally valid."
- **Entry:** [Protagoras (with Gorgias)](eras/01-ancient/protagoras-and-the-sophists.md)

### "The unexamined life is not worth living"
- **Source:** Plato, *Apology* 38a (written c.390s–380s BCE, dramatic date 399 BCE), tr. Benjamin Jowett (Project Gutenberg #1656)
- **Speaker:** Socrates, addressing the jury after his conviction, while proposing his own penalty
- **Exact wording:** "daily to discourse about virtue, and of those other things about which you hear me examining myself and others, is the greatest good of man, and... the unexamined life is not worth living"
- **Context:** Socrates says this while explaining why he will not propose exile as his penalty: he cannot stop his examining, because it is the one thing that makes a human life worth living, and the jury is "still less likely to believe" him. It is a courtroom argument for why death is preferable to silence, not a free-floating motivational slogan about self-reflection.
- **Entry:** [Socrates](eras/01-ancient/socrates.md)

### "I know that I know nothing" (misquotation)
- **Source:** Not an exact quotation. The actual passage is Plato, *Apology* 21b–d, tr. Benjamin Jowett (Project Gutenberg #1656)
- **Speaker:** Socrates, recounting his visit to the Delphic oracle and his investigation of the "wiser" men of Athens
- **Exact wording:** "I neither know nor think that I know... In this latter particular, then, I seem to have slightly the advantage of him."
- **Context:** The pithy "I know that I know nothing" is a modern condensation; Socrates's actual claim is narrower and stranger — he is no wiser than the politicians and poets he examines, except that, unlike them, he does not mistake his ignorance for knowledge. That small, specific edge is what "Socratic wisdom" means, not a claim to total ignorance.
- **Entry:** [Socrates](eras/01-ancient/socrates.md)

### "Until philosophers are kings... cities will never have rest from their evils"
- **Source:** Plato, *Republic* 473c–d (written c.375 BCE), tr. Benjamin Jowett (Project Gutenberg #1497)
- **Speaker:** Socrates, to Glaucon, introducing what he calls "the third wave" of a hard argument
- **Exact wording:** "Until philosophers are kings, or the kings and princes of this world have the spirit and power of philosophy, and political greatness and wisdom meet in one, and those commoner natures who pursue either to the exclusion of the other are compelled to stand aside, cities will never have rest from their evils... nor the human race."
- **Context:** Socrates immediately concedes that the thought "seemed too extravagant" and that being convinced of it "is indeed a hard thing," and spends the following pages defining exactly what he means by "philosopher" — a lover of the unchanging Forms, not a mere intellectual or technocrat — because he expects (and gets) the objection that this is absurd. The line is a conclusion that needs the definition, not a free-standing manifesto.
- **Entry:** [Plato](eras/01-ancient/plato.md)

### "Man is naturally a political animal"
- **Source:** Aristotle, *Politics*, Book I, ch. 2, 1253a (written c.330s BCE), tr. William Ellis (Project Gutenberg #6762)
- **Speaker:** Aristotle, in his own voice
- **Exact wording:** "it is evident that a city is a natural production, and that man is naturally a political animal"
- **Context:** The claim is specifically that the city (*polis*) is the natural completion of the household and the village, needed for a fully human life, and that a human lacking the capacity for this kind of community is "either inferior or superior to man." It is an argument about what humans need in order to flourish, not a claim that humans are inherently partisan, scheming, or drawn to electoral politics.
- **Entry:** [Aristotle](eras/01-ancient/aristotle.md)

### "We are what we repeatedly do. Excellence, then, is not an act, but a habit." (misattribution)
- **Source:** Not Aristotle. The sentence was written by the historian Will Durant in *The Story of Philosophy* (1926), summarizing (not quoting) Aristotle's *Nicomachean Ethics*, Book II, ch. 4 and Book I, ch. 7
- **Speaker:** Will Durant, paraphrasing Aristotle
- **Exact wording:** "not in the text" — the sentence is Durant's own prose in the Aristotle chapter of *The Story of Philosophy* (1926): "we are what we repeatedly do. Excellence, then, is not an act but a habit" (note: no comma before "but"; the popular version adds one). Durant quotes Aristotle in the same breath, but separately and in quotation marks: "these virtues are formed in man by his doing the actions" (*NE* II.1)
- **Context:** Aristotle does argue that virtues are acquired by habituation, not by a single act (NE II.1, II.4) — Durant's line is a fair gloss of that idea — but the crisp, quotable sentence itself is Durant's, not Aristotle's. It is one of the most common fake-Aristotle quotes in circulation.
- **Entry:** [Aristotle](eras/01-ancient/aristotle.md)

### "Death is nothing to us"
- **Source:** Epicurus, *Letter to Menoeceus* (written c.early 3rd c. BCE), tr. Robert Drew Hicks, hosted at Wikisource and the Internet Classics Archive
- **Speaker:** Epicurus, writing in his own voice to his student Menoeceus
- **Exact wording:** "Death, therefore, the most awful of evils, is nothing to us, seeing that, when we are, death is not come, and, when death is come, we are not."
- **Context:** This sits inside Epicurus's larger argument that the good life comes from removing groundless fears (of the gods, of death) and choosing pleasures prudently, not indulgently — the same letter says "the wise seek to enjoy the time which is most pleasant and not merely that which is longest." The popular sense of "epicurean" as lavish indulgence is close to the opposite of his teaching: overindulgence causes pain, so the safest pleasures are modest, secured above all by friendship rather than luxury.
- **Entry:** [Epicurus](eras/01-ancient/epicurus.md)

### "If cattle or lions had hands... they would paint their gods"
- **Source:** Xenophanes, fragment DK B15 (6th–5th c. BCE), preserved by Clement of Alexandria; tr. Arthur Fairbanks, *The First Philosophers of Greece* (1898), p. 67 (his fragment 6)
- **Speaker:** Xenophanes, in his own voice
- **Exact wording:** "But if cattle or lions had hands, so as to paint with their hands and produce works of art as men do, they would paint their gods and give them bodies in form like their own—horses like horses, cattle like cattle."
- **Context:** Xenophanes is not mocking religion as such. The fragment belongs to a sustained critique of Homer and Hesiod's gods, who steal and deceive, and it clears the ground for his own positive claim that there is "one god greatest among gods and men, not at all like mortals in body or in thought" (B23). It is a theological reform, not an early atheism.
- **Entry:** [Xenophanes and Anaxagoras](eras/01-ancient/presocratic-pluralists.md)

### "Whether genera and species subsist or are in mere conceptions only"
- **Source:** Porphyry, *Isagoge* (c. 270 CE), ch. I; tr. Octavius Freire Owen, *The Organon* (London: Bohn, 1853), vol. II, pp. 609–610
- **Speaker:** Porphyry, in his own voice
- **Exact wording:** "I shall omit to speak about genera and species, as to whether they subsist (in the nature of things) or in mere conceptions only; whether also if subsistent, they are bodies or incorporeal, and whether they are separate from, or in, sensibles, and subsist about these"
- **Context:** Porphyry raises the three questions only to set them aside: the *Isagoge* is a beginner's introduction to Aristotle's logic, and he says the matter needs a deeper inquiry. Because Boethius translated and commented on the book, these three unanswered questions became the medieval problem of universals, argued over by Abelard, Aquinas, Scotus and Ockham.
- **Entry:** [Porphyry, Iamblichus, Proclus and Hypatia](eras/01-ancient/late-neoplatonists.md)

## Medieval

### "Give me chastity and continency, only not yet"
- **Source:** Augustine, *Confessions*, Book VIII, ch. 7 (written c.397–400 CE), tr. Edward Bouverie Pusey (Project Gutenberg #3296)
- **Speaker:** Augustine, addressing God, recalling his state of mind as a young man before his conversion
- **Exact wording:** "I wretched, most wretched, in the very commencement of my early youth, had begged chastity of Thee, and said, 'Give me chastity and continency, only not yet.'"
- **Context:** Augustine quotes his own earlier, divided prayer to diagnose it: he wanted his disordered desire "satisfied, rather than extinguished," and the passage that follows traces the "contention" between old habit and his will to change, resolved only when he stops bargaining for delay. The line captures the problem Augustine is about to solve, not his settled teaching on chastity.
- **Entry:** [Augustine](eras/02-medieval/augustine-of-hippo.md)

### "Faith seeking understanding"
- **Source:** Anselm, *Proslogion*, Prologue (written 1077–78); the Latin phrase *Fides quaerens intellectum* was Anselm's own original title for the work, per the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy and Britannica
- **Speaker:** Anselm, describing his own method
- **Exact wording:** "Fides quaerens intellectum" ("faith seeking understanding"); Anselm states the point directly in the *Proslogion*'s first chapter: "I do not seek to understand that I may believe, but I believe in order to understand" (tr. S. N. Deane) ("Neque enim quaero intelligere ut credam, sed credo ut intelligam")
- **Context:** As SEP notes, the motto invites two opposite misreadings: that Anselm hopes to replace faith with proof once reason does its work, or that he thinks reason is irrelevant to faith. Both are wrong — for Anselm, faith is the starting point that makes the understanding possible at all, not a stage to be outgrown, and not a wall that reason may not approach. The ontological argument of *Proslogion* ch. 2–4 is offered as understanding sought *from inside* faith, not as a religiously neutral proof meant to produce belief in someone who lacks it.
- **Entry:** [Anselm](eras/02-medieval/anselm-of-canterbury.md)

### "As for fire, which is inanimate, it has no action"
- **Source:** al-Ghazali, *The Incoherence of the Philosophers* (*Tahāfut al-falāsifa*, 1095), Discussion 17, tr. Michael E. Marmura (2nd ed., Brigham Young University Press, 2000), p. 167
- **Speaker:** al-Ghazali, in his own voice
- **Exact wording:** "As for fire, which is inanimate, it has no action."
- **Context:** This is the cotton-and-fire example. Al-Ghazali argues that we observe the cotton burning when it touches fire, but never observe that the fire *necessitates* the burning: "The connection between what is habitually believed to be a cause and what is habitually believed to be an effect is not necessary, according to us" (p. 166). He does not deny a regular natural order; he denies that it is necessary, and grounds it in God's decree. Whether he was a full occasionalist is disputed.
- **Entry:** [Al-Ghazali](eras/02-medieval/al-ghazali.md)

## Early Modern

### "It is much safer to be feared than loved"
- **Source:** Machiavelli, *The Prince*, ch. 17 (written 1513), tr. W. K. Marriott (Project Gutenberg #1232)
- **Speaker:** Machiavelli, addressing the prince directly
- **Exact wording:** "it may be answered that one should wish to be both, but, because it is difficult to unite them in one person, it is much safer to be feared than loved, when, of the two, either must be dispensed with"
- **Context:** Machiavelli's actual claim is conditional and narrower than the slogan: a prince should want both love and fear, and should specifically avoid being hated even while being feared — he immediately adds that fear must stop short of hatred, secured by never seizing subjects' property or women. This is advice about necessity under new and dangerous rule, from a chapter that elsewhere condemns pointless cruelty (ch. 8's judgment on Agathocles), not a general claim that "the ends justify the means," a sentence Machiavelli never wrote.
- **Entry:** [Machiavelli](eras/03-early-modern/niccolo-machiavelli.md)

### "Que sais-je?" ("What do I know?")
- **Source:** Montaigne's personal motto, adopted c.1576 and inscribed on a medal bearing a pair of scales; developed at length in the *Essays*, Book II, ch. 12, "An Apology for Raymond Sebond" (written c.1575–80)
- **Speaker:** Montaigne, in his own voice
- **Exact wording:** The motto itself, "Que sçay-je?," appears in Montaigne's own words in the chapter cited — "Cette fantasie est plus seurement conceuë par interrogation : Que sçay-je ? comme je la porte à la devise d'une balance" (*Essais* II.12) — and the chapter it summarizes argues at length against dogmatic certainty specifically, not against commitment of any kind
- **Context:** The motto is read as a shrug — "who can say, so anything goes" — but it names a disciplined Pyrrhonian method: set argument against argument, let them balance, suspend judgment about *knowledge claims* rather than about everything. Montaigne holds firm, stated loyalties (to tolerance; against cruelty, the vice he says he detests most) and treats custom as a powerful fact to reckon with, not a truth to celebrate — his skepticism targets certainty, including the atheist's, which is why every side of the wars of religion tried to claim him.
- **Entry:** [Michel de Montaigne](eras/03-early-modern/michel-de-montaigne.md)

### "Even if we should concede... that there is no God" (*etiamsi daremus*)
- **Source:** Hugo Grotius, *On the Law of War and Peace* (*De iure belli ac pacis*, 1625), Prolegomena §11; tr. F. W. Kelsey (Oxford, 1925), p. 13
- **Speaker:** Grotius, in his own voice
- **Exact wording:** "What we have been saying would have a degree of validity even if we should concede that which cannot be conceded without the utmost wickedness, that there is no God, or that the affairs of men are of no concern to Him."
- **Context:** Often read as the moment natural law was secularized. Grotius himself calls the concession wicked and goes straight on to argue that we must obey God as our creator, and the hypothesis was not new: Suárez, thirteen years earlier, had reported a version of it from the fourteenth-century Gregory of Rimini and rejected it. How far it marks a break with the scholastics is still debated.
- **Entry:** [Suárez and Grotius](eras/03-early-modern/suarez-and-grotius.md)

### "The life of man, solitary, poore, nasty, brutish, and short"
- **Source:** Hobbes, *Leviathan*, ch. 13 (1651), original-spelling first-edition text (Project Gutenberg #3207)
- **Speaker:** Hobbes, in his own voice
- **Exact wording:** "continuall feare, and danger of violent death; And the life of man, solitary, poore, nasty, brutish, and short."
- **Context:** This describes the state of nature — life with no common power to keep people "in awe" — not Hobbes's view of human nature or society as such. Hobbes is explicit that people have strong, rational reasons to escape this condition by contract, the whole point of the rest of *Leviathan*: the line is the diagnosis the social contract cures, not a verdict on humanity as permanently savage.
- **Entry:** [Hobbes](eras/03-early-modern/thomas-hobbes.md)

### "I think, therefore I am"
- **Source:** Descartes, *Discourse on the Method*, Part IV (1637), tr. John Veitch (Project Gutenberg #59)
- **Speaker:** Descartes, narrating his own method
- **Exact wording:** "this truth, I think, therefore I am (COGITO ERGO SUM), was so certain and of such evidence that no ground of doubt, however extravagant, could be alleged by the sceptics capable of shaking it"
- **Context:** The cogito is reached only after Descartes has resolved to doubt everything that admits the slightest uncertainty — senses, mathematics, even the external world — and finds that the very act of doubting still requires a doubter to exist. It is a minimal certainty about his own existence as a thinking thing, used as the first foothold for rebuilding knowledge — not a claim that thinking makes a person special, and not, as pop usage sometimes has it, that consciousness creates reality. The precise slogan is not in the *Meditations* either, where it appears instead as "I am, I exist," with its status as inference or intuition a live scholarly question.
- **Entry:** [Descartes](eras/03-early-modern/rene-descartes.md)

### "White paper, void of all characters" (tabula rasa)
- **Source:** Locke, *An Essay Concerning Human Understanding*, Book II, ch. 1, §2 (1689), original 1690 first-edition text (Project Gutenberg #10615)
- **Speaker:** Locke, in his own voice
- **Exact wording:** "Let us then suppose the mind to be, as we say, white paper, void of all characters, without any ideas:—How comes it to be furnished?"
- **Context:** The image answers a specific target — the claim, attacked throughout Book I, that some principles and ideas are innate, stamped on the mind from birth. Locke's positive claim is that all ideas derive from experience, sorted into sensation and reflection; the "white paper" is a thesis about the *origin* of ideas, not a claim that a mind is passive or that people are infinitely malleable by upbringing alone.
- **Entry:** [Locke](eras/03-early-modern/john-locke.md)

### "God, or Nature" (Deus sive Natura)
- **Source:** Spinoza, *Ethics*, Part IV, Preface (written by 1675, published 1677), tr. R. H. M. Elwes (Project Gutenberg #971)
- **Speaker:** Spinoza, in his own voice
- **Exact wording:** "the eternal and infinite Being, which we call God or Nature, acts by the same necessity as that whereby it exists"
- **Context:** The phrase is Spinoza's identification of God with the single infinite substance of which everything else is a mode — not a claim that "nature" in the everyday, physical sense simply is God, nor a soft, poetic pantheism. The surrounding argument is doing technical work: because God/Nature acts from the necessity of its own existence rather than for the sake of an end, Spinoza argues, the common idea of natural "perfection" and "imperfection" is really just a comparison among things relative to a human-made mental template, not a fact about nature's purposes.
- **Entry:** [Spinoza](eras/03-early-modern/baruch-spinoza.md)

### "The heart has its reasons, which reason does not know"
- **Source:** Pascal, *Pensées*, §§277–278 (Brunschvicg numbering; composed c.1657–62, published posthumously 1670), tr. W. F. Trotter (Project Gutenberg #18269)
- **Speaker:** Pascal, in his own voice, in a fragment on faith and reason
- **Exact wording:** "The heart has its reasons, which reason does not know. We feel it in a thousand things... It is the heart which experiences God, and not the reason. This, then, is faith: God felt by the heart, not by the reason."
- **Context:** "Heart" here is not a synonym for emotion or sentiment against fact; in the surrounding fragments Pascal treats the heart as a cognitive faculty that grasps first principles — space, time, number, motion — which reasoning itself must rely on but cannot supply or prove. This is the same Pascal who was a working mathematician and experimental physicist; the line is about the limits of discursive reason, not an endorsement of feeling over evidence.
- **Entry:** [Blaise Pascal](eras/03-early-modern/blaise-pascal.md)

### "Reason is, and ought only to be the slave of the passions"
- **Source:** Hume, *A Treatise of Human Nature*, Book II, Part III, §3 (1739–40), original-spelling text at davidhume.org (T 2.3.3); Project Gutenberg #4705 modernizes "'Tis" to "It is"
- **Speaker:** Hume, in his own voice
- **Exact wording:** "Reason is, and ought only to be the slave of the passions, and can never pretend to any other office than to serve and obey them."
- **Context:** Hume's point is structural, not a celebration of impulsiveness: reason alone, he argues, can never originate an action or oppose a passion, because reason only discovers relations of ideas and matters of fact, while it is desire that moves us to act. The same section contains Hume's famous deliberate shock line, worth pairing with this one: "'Tis not contrary to reason to prefer the destruction of the whole world to the scratching of my finger" — not a preference Hume is recommending, but his argument that reason alone has no resources to call such a preference unreasonable; that work falls to better-informed passions, not to reason's say-so.
- **Entry:** [Hume](eras/03-early-modern/david-hume.md)

### "When the legislative and executive powers are united in the same person... there can be no liberty"
- **Source:** Montesquieu, *The Spirit of the Laws*, Book XI, ch. 6 (1748), Thomas Nugent's 18th-century translation, as reproduced in the Hanover College history sourcebook
- **Speaker:** Montesquieu, in his own voice
- **Exact wording:** "When the legislative and executive powers are united in the same person, or in the same body of magistrates, there can be no liberty; because apprehensions may arise, lest the same monarch or senate should enact tyrannical laws, to execute them in a tyrannical manner."
- **Context:** Civics courses extract this one chapter from a 31-book comparative study as a blueprint for hermetically sealed branches — but Montesquieu's own praised example, the English constitution, in fact mixes and overlaps the powers rather than walling them apart. His real claim is that liberty, defined as the tranquillity of mind that comes from not fearing arbitrary power, requires that the powers not be *concentrated* in one person or body. Most of the book concerns something else entirely: how law should fit a nation's "general spirit" — its climate, customs, religion, and commerce.
- **Entry:** [Montesquieu](eras/03-early-modern/montesquieu.md)

### "We must cultivate our garden"
- **Source:** Voltaire, *Candide* (1759), ch. XXX, Candide's last words (New York: Boni and Liveright, 1918)
- **Speaker:** Candide, the title character, not Voltaire in his own voice
- **Exact wording:** "we must cultivate our garden."
- **Context:** The answer Candide gives to Pangloss, who is still insisting that all events are linked for the best. It closes a satire on Leibnizian optimism written after the 1755 Lisbon earthquake. Readers disagree whether it counsels quiet withdrawal from public life or modest, practical work in place of metaphysics; Voltaire himself spent his last decades campaigning for the Calas family and for toleration.
- **Entry:** [Bayle and Voltaire](eras/03-early-modern/bayle-and-voltaire.md)

### "Man is born free; and everywhere he is in chains"
- **Source:** Rousseau, *The Social Contract*, Book I, ch. 1 (1762), tr. G. D. H. Cole (Project Gutenberg #46333)
- **Speaker:** Rousseau, in his own voice, opening the book
- **Exact wording:** "Man is born free; and everywhere he is in chains. One thinks himself the master of others, and still remains a greater slave than they."
- **Context:** As Cole's own introduction to this edition points out, the sentence is not a historical claim that a "golden age" of freedom literally preceded society — Rousseau means something closer to "man is born for freedom," using "is born free" to state a normative claim as if it were a fact. The chapter immediately turns to its real question: not whether this happened, but what could make political authority *legitimate* given that people now live under it.
- **Entry:** [Rousseau](eras/03-early-modern/jean-jacques-rousseau.md)

### "Sapere aude" ("Dare to know")
- **Source:** Kant, "An Answer to the Question: What is Enlightenment?" (1784), hosted in full English translation at marxists.org
- **Speaker:** Kant, in his own voice
- **Exact wording:** "Sapere aude! [dare to be wise] Have courage to make use of your own understanding! is thus the motto of enlightenment."
- **Context:** Kant borrows the Latin tag from Horace and makes it the motto for a specific, narrow failure he is diagnosing: most people stay immature not from lack of understanding but from "laziness and cowardice," letting a book, a pastor, or a doctor think for them. The essay's larger argument distinguishes the "private" use of reason (where obedience, e.g. as an officer or clergyman, may be required) from its "public" use as a scholar addressing the reading public, which Kant insists must always be free — the motto is not a blanket license to defy any authority in any role.
- **Entry:** [Kant](eras/03-early-modern/immanuel-kant.md)

### "The starry heavens above me and the moral law within me"
- **Source:** Kant, *Critique of Practical Reason*, Conclusion (1788); tr. Mary Gregor (Cambridge, 1997); T. K. Abbott's public-domain translation renders the same opening of the Conclusion "Two things fill the mind with ever new and increasing admiration and awe, the oftener and the more steadily we reflect on them: the starry heavens above and the moral law within"
- **Speaker:** Kant, in his own voice, in the book's closing words
- **Exact wording:** "Two things fill the mind with ever new and increasing admiration and reverence, the more often and more steadily one reflects on them: the starry heavens above me and the moral law within me."
- **Context:** The two objects are paired on purpose: the starry sky reduces Kant, a mere speck, to insignificance in the physical universe, while the moral law, precisely because it is not spatial or physical, reveals him as a rational agent of infinite worth regardless of his place in nature. It is a statement about human dignity grounded in autonomy, not nature poetry or an argument from design.
- **Entry:** [Kant](eras/03-early-modern/immanuel-kant.md)

### "Every thing is what it is, and not another thing"
- **Source:** Joseph Butler, *Fifteen Sermons Preached at the Rolls Chapel*, Preface to the second edition (1729); new ed., London: Tegg, 1841, p. xxii
- **Speaker:** Butler, in his own voice
- **Exact wording:** "Every thing is what it is, and not another thing."
- **Context:** Butler's target is the reduction of benevolence to self-love: an act done from love of a neighbor is not secretly selfish just because the agent takes pleasure in it. G. E. Moore later put the sentence, spelled "Everything," on the title page of *Principia Ethica* (1903) as the motto of his own anti-reductionism about goodness.
- **Entry:** [Shaftesbury, Hutcheson and Butler](eras/03-early-modern/moral-sense-school.md)

## 19th Century

### "The owl of Minerva begins its flight only with the onset of dusk"
- **Source:** Hegel, *Elements of the Philosophy of Right*, Preface (1820), in S. W. Dyde's public-domain translation (1896, as on marxists.org); the popular "begins its flight only with the onset of dusk" is H. B. Nisbet's wording (Cambridge, 1991)
- **Speaker:** Hegel, in his own voice
- **Exact wording:** "When philosophy paints its grey in grey, one form of life has become old, and by means of grey it cannot be rejuvenated, but only known. The owl of Minerva, takes its flight only when the shades of night are gathering." (Dyde)
- **Context:** This follows Hegel's more provocative, equally misread line from the same Preface — "What is rational is actual, and what is actual is rational" (the standard modern rendering of *wirklich*; Dyde's older translation has "real") — which sounds like a blank endorsement of the Prussian status quo but is really his technical point that only what truly embodies reason counts as fully "actual" (mere existence is not yet actuality). The owl explains why: philosophy understands a form of life only after it has matured and is passing away — a claim about philosophy's retrospective task, not a slogan that wisdom always arrives in time for a crisis.
- **Entry:** [Hegel](eras/04-19th-century/g-w-f-hegel.md)

### "Soft despotism"
- **Source:** Tocqueville, *Democracy in America*, Vol. II, Part IV, ch. 6 (1840); Henry Reeve's 19th-century translation (hosted at marxists.org) renders the passage as "more extensive and more mild; it would degrade men without tormenting them"; the now-standard English label "soft despotism" comes from later translations (e.g. Mansfield and Winthrop) of Tocqueville's *despotisme doux*
- **Speaker:** Tocqueville, in his own voice
- **Exact wording:** (Reeve translation) "if despotism were to be established amongst the democratic nations of our days, it might assume a different character... it would be more extensive and more mild; it would degrade men without tormenting them."
- **Context:** Tocqueville is explicit that this new despotism would not look like ancient tyranny; it would be a servitude "of the regular, quiet, and gentle kind," governing through "a net-work of small complicated rules, minute and uniform" that infantilize citizens rather than terrorize them, while leaving the outward forms of liberty intact. He presents it as a tendency equality makes possible, not a prophecy — and he did not think resisting democracy itself was wise; his question was how liberty survives inside it.
- **Entry:** [Alexis de Tocqueville](eras/04-19th-century/alexis-de-tocqueville.md)

### "Better to be Socrates dissatisfied [than a fool satisfied]"
- **Source:** Mill, *Utilitarianism*, ch. 2 (1863); wording cross-checked against multiple independent public-domain transcriptions
- **Speaker:** Mill, in his own voice
- **Exact wording:** "It is better to be a human being dissatisfied than a pig satisfied; better to be Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied."
- **Context:** Mill is answering the standard objection that utilitarianism is a "doctrine worthy only of swine" by introducing *qualitative* distinctions among pleasures — some kinds of pleasure (the intellectual, the moral) are higher than others regardless of quantity, and someone who has experienced both kinds will not trade the higher for any amount of the lower. The line is the center of a specific fix to a specific objection, not a throwaway crack about pigs.
- **Entry:** [Mill](eras/04-19th-century/john-stuart-mill.md)

### "It is the opium of the people" (religion)
- **Source:** Marx, "Contribution to the Critique of Hegel's Philosophy of Right: Introduction" (written 1843, published 1844), hosted in full at marxists.org
- **Speaker:** Marx, in his own voice
- **Exact wording:** "Religious suffering is, at one and the same time, the expression of real suffering and a protest against real suffering. Religion is the sigh of the oppressed creature, the heart of a heartless world, and the soul of soulless conditions. It is the opium of the people."
- **Context:** The sentence right before the famous one is usually dropped: religion is, for Marx, both an expression of real distress *and* a protest against it — not simply a con or a tool of the ruling class, though it also serves that function. His conclusion is not "abolish religion by force" but that criticizing religion is only the first step; the real task is to abolish the conditions that require that kind of "illusory happiness" in the first place.
- **Entry:** [Marx](eras/04-19th-century/karl-marx.md)

### "The philosophers have only interpreted the world... the point is to change it"
- **Source:** Marx, *Theses on Feuerbach*, Thesis XI (written 1845, published posthumously 1888), hosted in full at marxists.org
- **Speaker:** Marx, in private notes (not published in his lifetime; Engels published them as an appendix after Marx's death)
- **Exact wording:** "The philosophers have only interpreted the world, in various ways; the point, however, is to change it." (Also rendered: "Philosophers have hitherto only interpreted the world...")
- **Context:** This caps eleven short theses arguing that earlier materialism and idealism alike failed to grasp human sense-activity as practical, revolutionary activity. It is Marx's own epigrammatic summary of why *theory* matters to him only as it bears on practice — it is not a claim that interpretation or theory is worthless, which the other ten theses, all doing theoretical work, would make a strange thing for Marx to mean.
- **Entry:** [Marx](eras/04-19th-century/karl-marx.md)

### "Leap of faith" (misattribution)
- **Source:** Not Kierkegaard's phrase. Commonly attributed to *Fear and Trembling* (1843) or *Concluding Unscientific Postscript* (1846)
- **Speaker:** Popular shorthand, not a Kierkegaardian pseudonym
- **Exact wording:** "not in the text" — confirmed by a word search of Walter Lowrie's translation of *Fear and Trembling*, where "leap of faith" does not occur once while "leap" appears eight times on its own ("the great leap whereby I pass," "the continual leap in existence"), and by the Cambridge Companion to Kierkegaard: Kierkegaard never uses any Danish equivalent of "leap of faith." What he and his pseudonyms (e.g., Johannes de silentio in *Fear and Trembling*) discuss is a "leap," or a "qualitative leap," in connection with faith — a related but distinct idea
- **Context:** "Leap of faith" as popularly used suggests an arbitrary, unreasoning jump — "just believe, no evidence needed." Kierkegaard's "leap," modeled on Abraham in *Fear and Trembling*, is narrower: a commitment reachable by no ordinary ethical or rational deliberation, because faith for him involves a paradox reason cannot resolve. It is a point about the structure of commitment, not an endorsement of believing things for no reason.
- **Entry:** [Kierkegaard](eras/04-19th-century/soren-kierkegaard.md)

### "God is dead"
- **Source:** Nietzsche, *The Gay Science*, §125, "The Madman" (1882); tr. Walter Kaufmann (Vintage, 1974), pp. 181–82; Common's older version reads "How shall we console ourselves, the most murderous of all murderers?"
- **Speaker:** A madman, in a parable Nietzsche narrates — not Nietzsche's own flat assertion, and not a character who is cheerful about it
- **Exact wording:** "God is dead. God remains dead. And we have killed him. How shall we comfort ourselves, the murderers of all murderers?"
- **Context:** The madman runs into a marketplace with a lantern in daylight, shouting that he seeks God, and is laughed at by people who do not believe in God either — he says they, too, are God's murderers, and that they have not yet understood what they have done: no sun to orient by, no up or down, the unmooring of all inherited values. He ends by saying he has come too early, and the parable closes with him smashing his lantern in despairing silence. It is a diagnosis of cultural unbelief and the nihilism Nietzsche sees coming, delivered by a figure whose grief and horror are the point — not an atheist's victory lap.
- **Entry:** [Nietzsche](eras/04-19th-century/friedrich-nietzsche.md)

### "What does not kill me makes me stronger"
- **Source:** Nietzsche, *Twilight of the Idols*, "Maxims and Arrows," §8 (1888); wording consistent across independent transcriptions
- **Speaker:** Nietzsche, in his own voice, in a list of one-line aphorisms
- **Exact wording:** "That which does not kill me, makes me stronger." (Also rendered: "What does not kill me makes me stronger.")
- **Context:** As an isolated maxim in a list of 44 numbered aphorisms, it is already Nietzsche's deliberately compressed, provocative style — not a worked-out argument, let alone a self-help endorsement that *all* suffering is good or that cruelty should be sought out. Nietzsche's broader ethics (self-overcoming, the affirmation of a life one could will to repeat) is about what one does with unavoidable hardship, not a claim that more hardship is always better.
- **Entry:** [Nietzsche](eras/04-19th-century/friedrich-nietzsche.md)

### "There are no facts, only interpretations"
- **Source:** Nietzsche, unpublished notebook entry (1886–87), compiled posthumously by his sister Elisabeth Förster-Nietzsche and Peter Gast as *The Will to Power*, §481 — a book Nietzsche never assembled or approved for publication
- **Speaker:** Nietzsche, in a private notebook, not a finished, published work
- **Exact wording:** "Against positivism, which halts at phenomena — 'There are only facts' — I would say: No, facts is precisely what there is not, only interpretations."
- **Context:** The repo should flag this one doubly: not only is the line routinely read as a blanket "nothing is true, everything is relative" (itself a misreading — Nietzsche is attacking a specific positivist slogan, not denying that interpretations can be better or worse grounded), but it was never published by Nietzsche himself. *The Will to Power* is a posthumous assemblage from notebooks, shaped by editors with their own agenda, and scholars disagree about how much weight any single note in it should carry against Nietzsche's finished books.
- **Entry:** [Nietzsche](eras/04-19th-century/friedrich-nietzsche.md)

## 20th Century

### "All history is the history of thought"
- **Source:** R. G. Collingwood, *The Idea of History* (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1946), Epilegomena §1, "Human Nature and Human History," p. 215
- **Speaker:** Collingwood, in his own voice (the book was edited after his death by T. M. Knox)
- **Exact wording:** "All history is the history of thought."
- **Context:** Narrower than it sounds. "Thought" means the reasoning behind deliberate actions, which the historian recovers by re-enacting it: "The history of thought, and therefore all history, is the re-enactment of past thought in the historian's own mind" (same page). Critics ask what becomes of passions, unintended consequences and economic forces on this view.
- **Entry:** [Dilthey and Collingwood](eras/04-19th-century/dilthey-and-collingwood.md)

### "Whereof one cannot speak, thereof one must be silent"
- **Source:** Wittgenstein, *Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus*, proposition 7 (1921; English 1922), tr. C. K. Ogden with Frank Ramsey
- **Speaker:** Wittgenstein, in his own voice — the book's final, one-sentence proposition
- **Exact wording:** "Whereof one cannot speak, thereof one must be silent." (Ogden/Ramsey, 1922). The now-standard Pears & McGuinness translation (1961) renders it "What we cannot speak about we must pass over in silence."
- **Context:** This is not a dismissal of ethics, aesthetics, or the meaning of life as worthless nonsense. The Tractatus's own closing propositions (6.4–6.522) say the opposite: ethical and aesthetic value, and "the mystical," are precisely what cannot be put into meaningful propositions about facts — but Wittgenstein calls them *the most important things*, which is exactly why language cannot capture them and philosophy must stop trying to state them as if they were further facts.
- **Entry:** [Wittgenstein (early · late)](eras/05-20th-century/wittgenstein-early.md)

### "Existence precedes essence"
- **Source:** Sartre, *Existentialism Is a Humanism* (lecture 1945, published 1946), full English text hosted at marxists.org
- **Speaker:** Sartre, in his own voice
- **Exact wording:** "What do we mean by saying that existence precedes essence? We mean that man first of all exists, encounters himself, surges up in the world — and defines himself afterwards."
- **Context:** Sartre's point is specifically that there is no pre-given human nature or blueprint (no "essence") that a person merely fills in — a person is nothing but what they make of themselves through choices and action. This is Sartre's own formula for his own existentialism; misconceptions.md is right to flag that Kierkegaard, Heidegger (who rejected the "existentialist" label for *Being and Time*), Camus (who also rejected it), and Beauvoir (who corrected Sartre on several points) do not all share it as a common creed.
- **Entry:** [Sartre](eras/05-20th-century/jean-paul-sartre.md)

### "Hell is other people"
- **Source:** Sartre, *No Exit* (*Huis Clos*, 1944); the line's standard English rendering is confirmed across Britannica and multiple scholarly summaries of the play's final scene
- **Speaker:** Garcin, one of three characters trapped together for eternity in a single room — not Sartre speaking directly, and not his final word on other people in general
- **Exact wording:** "L'enfer, c'est les autres" — "Hell is—other people!" in Stuart Gilbert's translation — Garcin's realization near the play's end, capping his speech about torture-chambers and red-hot pokers
- **Context:** The line is the punchline of a play whose whole premise is that the traditional torture-chamber hell is unnecessary: three strangers who need each other's approval, and torment each other by watching and judging, are hell enough for one another with no devils required. It dramatizes a specific claim from Sartre's *Being and Nothingness* about the way another person's gaze can fix and objectify you — it is not Sartre's considered, final verdict that all human relationships are inherently infernal; his later work (and *Existentialism Is a Humanism*'s emphasis on responsibility to others) complicates any such flat reading.
- **Entry:** [Sartre](eras/05-20th-century/jean-paul-sartre.md)

### "The problem of the Twentieth Century is the problem of the color line"
- **Source:** W. E. B. Du Bois, *The Souls of Black Folk* (1903), "The Forethought" (public domain; Project Gutenberg #408)
- **Speaker:** Du Bois, in his own voice
- **Exact wording:** "for the problem of the Twentieth Century is the problem of the color line."
- **Context:** The line opens a book about the American South after Emancipation, but Du Bois meant it globally: the second chapter repeats it as "the relation of the darker to the lighter races of men in Asia and Africa, in America and the islands of the sea." The book's first chapter then introduces "double-consciousness," the sense "of always looking at one's self through the eyes of others."
- **Entry:** [W. E. B. Du Bois and Frantz Fanon](eras/05-20th-century/du-bois-and-fanon.md)

### "One is not born, but rather becomes, a woman"
- **Source:** Beauvoir, *The Second Sex* (*Le Deuxième Sexe*), Volume II, opening line (1949); French original "On ne naît pas femme: on le devient," English wording from H. M. Parshley's 1953 translation; the 2010 Borde and Malovany-Chevallier translation, which the study guide uses, drops the article: "One is not born, but rather becomes, woman"
- **Speaker:** Beauvoir, in her own voice
- **Exact wording:** "One is not born, but rather becomes, a woman. No biological, psychological, or economic fate determines the figure that the human female presents in society; it is civilization as a whole that produces this creature, intermediate between male and eunuch, which is described as feminine."
- **Context:** This opens Beauvoir's argument that "woman," as a social role and identity, is produced by society acting on a female body — not that biological sex itself is unreal or irrelevant, and not a claim about gender identity in the sense that later debates use the term. Her larger framework treats woman as cast in the position of "the Other" relative to a male-default "humanity," a structural claim about how one group has historically defined itself against another.
- **Entry:** [Beauvoir](eras/05-20th-century/simone-de-beauvoir.md)

### "The banality of evil"
- **Source:** Arendt, *Eichmann in Jerusalem: A Report on the Banality of Evil* (1963) — the phrase is literally the book's subtitle
- **Speaker:** Arendt, in her own voice, reporting on Adolf Eichmann's trial in Jerusalem
- **Exact wording:** "A Report on the Banality of Evil" (subtitle); Arendt's point in the book is that Eichmann struck her not as a monstrous fanatic but as a disturbingly ordinary bureaucrat concerned with career and paperwork
- **Context:** "Banal" here does not mean the Holocaust itself was ordinary or minor — Arendt is making a claim about the *perpetrator's* psychology and self-understanding: Eichmann's defense that he "did his duty," obeyed all orders as his oath demanded, displaying neither hatred nor guilt, is what she calls banal. The phrase describes a specific, disturbing kind of evil achieved through unthinking bureaucratic obedience, not a general theory that all evil is trivial.
- **Entry:** [Arendt](eras/05-20th-century/hannah-arendt.md)

### "'Meanings' just ain't in the head"
- **Source:** Hilary Putnam, "The Meaning of 'Meaning'" (1975), in *Mind, Language and Reality: Philosophical Papers*, vol. 2 (Cambridge University Press, 1975), p. 227
- **Speaker:** Putnam, in his own voice
- **Exact wording:** "Cut the pie any way you like, 'meanings' just ain't in the head!"
- **Context:** The conclusion of the Twin Earth thought experiment. On Twin Earth a liquid exactly like water is in fact XYZ, not H₂O; an earthling and her twin are in the same mental state, yet "water" refers to different stuff in their mouths. So what is in the head does not fix reference: meaning depends partly on the world and on the speaker's community. This is semantic externalism, distinct from the functionalism Putnam had defended in philosophy of mind.
- **Entry:** [Sellars, Davidson, Putnam and Lewis](eras/05-20th-century/analytic-philosophy-after-quine.md)

## Misattributions

### "I disapprove of what you say, but I will defend to the death your right to say it" (misattributed to Voltaire)
- **Source:** Not Voltaire. Written by Evelyn Beatrice Hall (under the pen name S. G. Tallentyre) in her 1906 biography *The Friends of Voltaire*, as her own summary of Voltaire's attitude — confirmed by Quote Investigator's sourcing and the Wikipedia/Wikiquote entries on Hall
- **Speaker:** Evelyn Beatrice Hall, writing about Voltaire, not Voltaire himself
- **Exact wording:** "I disapprove of what you say, but I will defend to the death your right to say it," presented by Hall as narrative commentary on Voltaire's beliefs, not as something she claimed he actually wrote or said
- **Context:** Voltaire (1694–1778) died well over a century before the line was written. Hall's biography was popular enough, and the sentiment close enough to things Voltaire did write about tolerance, that the line drifted from "Hall's gloss on Voltaire" to "a Voltaire quotation" over the 20th century. It belongs in the repo as a caution about biographies that paraphrase a subject in quotation marks.
- **Entry:** (no dedicated entry file; see the broader Enlightenment context in [Diderot](eras/03-early-modern/denis-diderot.md) and [Lessing](eras/03-early-modern/gotthold-ephraim-lessing.md))
