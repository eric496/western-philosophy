# Schools, Isms, and Movements
**English** | [简体中文](zh/schools-and-isms.md)

Philosophers get sorted into schools, movements, and "-isms" — some they founded, some they joined,
some that were invented for them centuries later, and a few they flatly rejected. This file catalogues
the labels that organize this repo: what each one claims, who here belongs to it, and what the label
itself is worth. It complements [`themes.md`](themes.md), which cuts across the figures by topic; this
file sorts the figures themselves. Labels are flagged where they are retrospective, contested, or
refused by their supposed members.


## Ancient and Medieval

### Pythagoreanism
- **What it is:** The movement founded by Pythagoras in Croton (c. 530 BCE), at once a philosophical school and a religious brotherhood bound by a rule of life. Members held property in common, observed dietary taboos (most famously on beans), kept the teachings secret, and practiced daily self-examination.
- **Core claims:** number is the principle of all things; the cosmos is ordered by mathematical ratio, audible in music as harmony; the soul is immortal and transmigrates between bodies, human and animal; purification of the soul, through study and ritual discipline, frees it from the cycle of rebirth.
- **Figures here:** [Pythagoras](eras/01-ancient/pythagoras.md)
- **The label:** Pythagoras wrote nothing, so "Pythagoreanism" is reconstructed from later reports (Aristotle, the later tradition) and covers both the brotherhood's way of life and the mathematics attributed to it.

### Platonism
- **What it is:** The philosophy of Plato and the tradition descending from his Academy, founded in Athens c. 387 BCE and continued, in changing forms, for some nine centuries. Its center is the claim that the sensible world is intelligible only through a higher, unchanging order.
- **Core claims:** the Forms (Ideas) are the truly real, eternal objects of knowledge, of which sensible things are copies or participants; knowledge is recollection of what the soul knew before birth; the soul is immortal and tripartite; the just city mirrors the just soul; the Good is the highest Form, source of being and knowledge.
- **Figures here:** [Plato](eras/01-ancient/plato.md)
- **The label:** "Platonism" in modern philosophy of mathematics — the view that abstract objects exist independently — borrows the name but is a distinct, narrower doctrine.

### Aristotelianism (the Peripatetic school)
- **What it is:** The philosophy of Aristotle and of the school he founded at the Lyceum in Athens (335 BCE), whose members were called Peripatetics. Transmitted through Greek, Arabic, and Latin commentary traditions, it became the default framework of medieval university philosophy and much of Islamic falsafa.
- **Core claims:** form and matter together constitute substances, known by abstraction from experience; nature is explained by four causes, with final causes throughout the living world; virtue is a habituated mean, and flourishing (eudaimonia) is rational activity over a complete life; logic, codified in the *Organon*, is the instrument of all inquiry.
- **Figures here:** [Aristotle](eras/01-ancient/aristotle.md)
- **The label:** "Peripatetic" is traditionally explained by Aristotle's habit of walking while teaching in the covered walkway (peripatos) of the Lyceum.

### The Sophists
- **What it is:** Not a school or doctrine but a professional movement: itinerant teachers in 5th-century BCE Greece who, for fees, taught rhetoric, argument, and the skills of political success in the democracies. Protagoras and Gorgias are its best-known figures; they shared a trade and a milieu, not a creed.
- **Core claims:** no common doctrine — characteristic themes include the teachability of virtue and excellence; the power of speech (logos) to persuade and even deceive; Protagoras's relativist slogan that man is the measure; Gorgias's display pieces arguing that nothing exists, or can be known or communicated.
- **Figures here:** [Protagoras (with Gorgias)](eras/01-ancient/protagoras-and-the-sophists.md)
- **The label:** "Sophist" (sophistēs) originally meant an expert or wise man; Plato's hostile portraits turned it into the pejorative term for a mercenary maker of false arguments it remains today.

### Cynicism
- **What it is:** A way of life more than a doctrine, founded by Antisthenes and made famous by Diogenes of Sinope, who performed the philosophy in public — living in a large jar, carrying a lamp in daylight, and defying convention and Alexander alike. Its goal was self-sufficiency (autarkeia) through radical simplicity.
- **Core claims:** virtue is the only good, and it suffices for happiness; wealth, reputation, and convention (nomos) are worthless or corrupting; one should live according to nature (physis), needing as little as possible; shamelessness and frank speech (parrhēsia) expose social pretense; the wise person is a citizen of the world (cosmopolitēs), not of one city.
- **Figures here:** [Diogenes](eras/01-ancient/diogenes-of-sinope-and-the-cynics.md)
- **The label:** "Cynic" comes from kynikos, "dog-like" — a nickname (Diogenes was called "the Dog") that the Cynics embraced; the modern sense of cynicism as sneering distrust is a distant descendant.

### Epicureanism
- **What it is:** The school founded by Epicurus in his Athens garden (c. 307 BCE), teaching a complete physics, epistemology, and ethics aimed at one practical end: a tranquil, pain-free life among friends. Lucretius's poem *On the Nature of Things* is its great Roman exposition.
- **Core claims:** everything is atoms moving in void, so the soul is mortal and death is nothing to us; the gods exist but take no part in human affairs; pleasure is the good, understood as absence of pain (aponia) and disturbance (ataraxia); simple pleasures, prudence, and friendship are its surest sources; knowledge rests on sensations and preconceptions.
- **Figures here:** [Epicurus](eras/01-ancient/epicurus.md), [Cicero & Lucretius](eras/01-ancient/cicero-and-lucretius.md)
- **The label:** The school met in Epicurus's garden, hence its nickname "the philosophy of the Garden"; the modern word "epicurean" for lavish indulgence inverts the master's teaching, which prized bread, water, and conversation over luxury.

### Stoicism
- **What it is:** The school founded by Zeno of Citium in Athens (c. 300 BCE) and developed by Chrysippus, then reshaped in Rome by Seneca, Epictetus, and Marcus Aurelius. For five centuries it was the dominant practical philosophy of the Greco-Roman world.
- **Core claims:** the cosmos is a single rational, providential order (the logos), and happiness is living in agreement with nature; virtue is the only good, vice the only evil, and health, wealth, and reputation are "indifferents"; some things are in our control and others are not, and tranquility lies in wanting only the first; destructive passions rest on false judgments and can be corrected by reason; all humans share in reason, grounding a cosmopolitan ethics.
- **Figures here:** [Zeno of Citium](eras/01-ancient/zeno-of-citium-and-the-stoics.md), [Seneca](eras/01-ancient/roman-stoics.md), [Epictetus](eras/01-ancient/roman-stoics.md), [Marcus Aurelius](eras/01-ancient/roman-stoics.md)
- **The label:** Named for the Stoa Poikile, the Painted Porch in Athens where Zeno taught; the modern adjective "stoic" (uncomplaining endurance) captures only the school's emotional discipline, not its physics or logic.

### Skepticism
- **What it is:** An umbrella for two ancient movements that made doubt itself a practice. Pyrrhonian skepticism, reviving Pyrrho's example and preserved in Sextus Empiricus, suspends judgment on all non-evident matters to reach tranquility; Academic skepticism, developed in Plato's Academy under Arcesilaus and Carneades, argued against the Stoics that nothing can be certainly known and acted on the merely plausible.
- **Core claims:** for every argument an equal counter-argument can be found (isostheneia); suspension of judgment (epochē) follows, and tranquility (ataraxia) follows it, "as a shadow follows the body"; the skeptic lives by appearances and custom without assenting to dogmas; (Academic form) the wise person withholds assent where certainty is impossible.
- **Figures here:** [Pyrrho](eras/01-ancient/pyrrho-of-elis-and-the-skeptics.md), [Cicero & Lucretius](eras/01-ancient/cicero-and-lucretius.md)
- **The label:** From skeptikos, "inquirer" — the Pyrrhonists insisted they were still searching, not asserting that nothing can be known, which would itself be a dogma.

### Neoplatonism
- **What it is:** The last great system of ancient Greek philosophy, built by Plotinus in 3rd-century Rome and extended by Porphyry, Iamblichus, and Proclus. It systematized Plato with Aristotelian and Stoic materials into a single hierarchical vision of reality and a program for the soul's return to its source.
- **Core claims:** all things derive by emanation from the One, an absolutely simple first principle beyond being and intellect; below it stand Intellect, Soul, and the sensible world, each less unified than the last; matter is privation and the lowest limit of the procession; the soul, fallen into body, returns by contemplation and virtue toward union with the One; Plato's dialogues, rightly read, contain the whole system.
- **Figures here:** [Plotinus](eras/01-ancient/plotinus.md), [Porphyry, Iamblichus, Proclus & Hypatia](eras/01-ancient/late-neoplatonists.md)
- **The label:** "Neoplatonism" is a modern scholarly coinage (18th–19th century); Plotinus and his successors called themselves simply Platonists and believed they were expounding Plato, not founding anything new.

### Augustinianism
- **What it is:** The tradition formed by Augustine's synthesis of Christian doctrine with Platonic philosophy, dominant in Latin theology for a millennium and repeatedly revived — by Anselm, the Franciscan school, and Jansenists and Reformers claiming him against each other. Its marks are inwardness, illumination, and the primacy of grace.
- **Core claims:** evil is not a substance but a privation of good; the human will is bound by original sin and freed only by grace, not by its own effort; knowledge of eternal truths comes by divine illumination of the mind; time is a distension of the soul, created with the world, not before it; history divides into two cities, the earthly and the City of God, intermixed until the end.
- **Figures here:** [Augustine](eras/02-medieval/augustine-of-hippo.md)
- **The label:** Augustine founded no school and wrote no system; "Augustinianism" names the long tradition that treated his works as its charter, and different ages have drawn quite different Augustines from them.

### Scholasticism
- **What it is:** The philosophy and theology of the medieval schools and universities (c. 1100–1500) — a method more than a doctrine. Its practitioners, the Schoolmen, worked by commentary on set texts (above all Aristotle and Peter Lombard's *Sentences*) and by the quaestio: a question stated, objections listed, an authority cited against them, a determination argued, and each objection answered in turn.
- **Core claims:** no single creed — scholastics disputed nearly everything; shared commitments include faith and reason as compatible routes to truth, with philosophy as theology's instrument; reality analyzed through Aristotle's categories, causes, and act–potency distinction; universals, individuation, and the relation of God's knowledge to contingent events as standing problems; conclusions tested by public disputation.
- **Figures here:** [Anselm](eras/02-medieval/anselm-of-canterbury.md), [Abelard](eras/02-medieval/peter-abelard.md), [Aquinas](eras/02-medieval/thomas-aquinas.md), [Duns Scotus](eras/02-medieval/john-duns-scotus.md), [Ockham](eras/02-medieval/william-of-ockham.md)
- **The label:** From scholasticus, "of the schools"; Renaissance humanists and Enlightenment writers used "scholastic" as a slur for sterile subtlety, a reputation modern scholarship has largely reversed.

### Thomism
- **What it is:** The school formed around Thomas Aquinas's synthesis of Aristotle and Christian doctrine, developed by Dominican commentators, made semi-official for Catholic teaching by Leo XIII's *Aeterni Patris* (1879), and still the most institutionally continuous of the medieval schools.
- **Core claims:** in God alone essence and existence are identical; in creatures they really differ, and everything that exists does so by participation in being (esse); God's existence can be demonstrated from effects (the Five Ways), while the Trinity and Incarnation are known by faith alone; the soul is the substantial form of the body, so the human being is one substance, not two; natural law is rational creatures' participation in the eternal law, and grace perfects nature rather than destroying it.
- **Figures here:** [Aquinas](eras/02-medieval/thomas-aquinas.md)
- **The label:** Aquinas was canonized in 1323 and named a Doctor of the Church in 1567; "Thomism" as a school label belongs to his later followers, who have never agreed on a single reading of him.

### Scotism
- **What it is:** The school formed around John Duns Scotus, the Franciscan rival tradition to Thomism in the later medieval universities. Where Aquinas analogized, Scotus univocated; where Aquinas derived individuation from matter, Scotus posited an individual form — and the two schools disputed these points for centuries.
- **Core claims:** being is said univocally of God and creatures, so theology can reason about God with the same concept of being; each individual is individuated by a positive "thisness" (haecceitas), not by matter alone; God's will is primary — things are good because God wills them, not willed because independently good; the formal distinction holds between inseparable aspects of one thing; Mary's Immaculate Conception, which Scotus defended, follows from Christ's perfect mediation.
- **Figures here:** [Duns Scotus](eras/02-medieval/john-duns-scotus.md)
- **The label:** Scotus's nickname, Doctor Subtilis ("the Subtle Doctor"), was earned; his followers' "Scotism" was for centuries one of the three great university schools, alongside Thomism and nominalism.

### Nominalism
- **What it is:** The late-medieval position, classically associated with William of Ockham, that universals are not things but names or mental signs. It arose as one side of the problem Porphyry had posed a thousand years earlier — whether genera and species exist in reality or only in thought — and became the banner of the "modern way" (via moderna) against the older realism.
- **Core claims:** only individual things exist; a universal term like "humanity" signifies many individuals directly and is a sign in the mind or in language, not a shared entity; abstract entities are not to be posited without necessity — the principle later called Ockham's razor; knowledge begins in intuitive cognition of singulars; God's absolute power (potentia absoluta) means the created order is contingent and must be learned from experience, not deduced.
- **Figures here:** [Ockham](eras/02-medieval/william-of-ockham.md)
- **The label:** From nomina, "names"; "realism" is its opposite on universals and has nothing to do with the modern political sense — the medieval realists (Aquinas moderately, Scotus formally) held that universals have some foundation in things.

### Apophatic (negative) theology
- **What it is:** A strand of theology, running from Pseudo-Dionysius through Eriugena, Eckhart, and Nicholas of Cusa, holding that God exceeds every name and concept, so that negations ("God is not finite, not a being among beings") approach the truth better than affirmations. It is a discipline of speech about God, paired in most of its authors with an affirmative (cataphatic) theology it qualifies.
- **Core claims:** God is beyond being and beyond intellect, so no predicate applies to God in its creaturely sense; every affirmation must be unsaid by a negation, and the negation itself transcended — God is "beyond assertion and denial"; unknowing (learned ignorance, in Cusa's phrase) is the highest mode of knowing God; the soul approaches God by stripping away images and concepts, in darkness and silence; language about God is analogical or symbolic, never literal description.
- **Figures here:** [Pseudo-Dionysius & Meister Eckhart](eras/02-medieval/pseudo-dionysius-and-eckhart.md), [John Scotus Eriugena](eras/02-medieval/john-scotus-eriugena.md), [Nicholas of Cusa](eras/02-medieval/nicholas-of-cusa.md)
- **The label:** From apophasis, Greek for "denial" or "saying away"; its classic source wrote as "Dionysius the Areopagite," a pseudonym that gave the *Mystical Theology* quasi-apostolic authority for a thousand years.

## Early Modern

### Renaissance humanism
- **What it is:** A cultural and educational movement, beginning in 14th-century Italy, that revived classical Greek and Roman texts and put the *studia humanitatis* — grammar, rhetoric, poetry, history, moral philosophy — at the center of learning. It was less a doctrine than a program: recover ancient sources in their original languages, and form eloquent, civically engaged people through them.
- **Core claims:** classical antiquity is a model worth recovering firsthand; human dignity and capability deserve celebration; rhetoric and eloquence are intellectual virtues; education should serve civic life; ancient texts must be read historically, in context.
- **Figures here:** [Ficino, Pico, Erasmus & Bruno](eras/03-early-modern/renaissance-humanists.md), [Montaigne](eras/03-early-modern/michel-de-montaigne.md)
- **The label:** "Humanism" is a 19th-century coinage (from *Humanismus*, 1808); the Renaissance figures themselves spoke of the *studia humanitatis*, and "humanist" (*umanista*) was slang for a teacher of those subjects.

### Machiavellianism
- **What it is:** The label attached to Machiavelli's name for a politics of cunning, deception, and ruthless expediency. Machiavelli's own work is better described as political realism: in *The Prince* (written 1513) and the *Discourses on Livy* he studied the "effectual truth" of how power is actually won and kept, rather than how rulers ought ideally to behave — and the *Discourses* defend republics, not tyrants.
- **Core claims:** politics must be judged by outcomes and necessities, not intentions; *virtù* (skill, audacity) contends with *fortuna* (chance); a ruler who insists on being good in all things may ruin himself and his state; republics need laws and citizen arms, not mercenaries.
- **Figures here:** [Machiavelli](eras/03-early-modern/niccolo-machiavelli.md)
- **The label:** "Machiavellian" became an insult in Elizabethan England, where stage villains bore his name; the claim that "Old Nick" (the Devil) derives from Niccolò is a popular folk etymology, often repeated but never proven.

### Rationalism
- **What it is:** The early modern position that reason is the primary source of knowledge: some truths are known independently of sense experience, through innate ideas or self-evident principles, and knowledge ideally forms a deductive system modeled on mathematics. Its three canonical figures disagree deeply — on substance, God, and the mind — beyond that shared confidence in reason.
- **Core claims:** the senses alone cannot ground certain or universal knowledge; some ideas or principles are innate; reality has an intelligible structure reason can grasp; deduction from clear first principles is the paradigm of knowing; metaphysics can be built systematically.
- **Figures here:** [Descartes](eras/03-early-modern/rene-descartes.md), [Spinoza](eras/03-early-modern/baruch-spinoza.md), [Leibniz](eras/03-early-modern/gottfried-wilhelm-leibniz.md)
- **The label:** The rationalism/empiricism pairing is largely a later historians' grid, systematized in the wake of Kant; the figures so labeled never formed a school or used the term of themselves.

### Empiricism
- **What it is:** The early modern position that all knowledge of the world derives from experience: the mind begins without innate ideas, and concepts are traced to impressions, sensations, and reflection on them. Where rationalists prized deduction, empiricists prized observation — and pressed the view hard enough to generate famous problems about causation, the self, and the external world.
- **Core claims:** there are no innate ideas or principles; every idea traces to experience; knowledge of matters of fact is probable, not certain; abstract terms must be cashed out in experiential content; claims beyond experience (substance, necessary connection) are suspect.
- **Figures here:** [Locke](eras/03-early-modern/john-locke.md), [Berkeley](eras/03-early-modern/george-berkeley.md), [Hume](eras/03-early-modern/david-hume.md)
- **The label:** Like its rationalist twin, the category is a retrospective historians' scheme; Berkeley and Hume reached conclusions (immaterialism, skepticism) that sit oddly under one banner, and neither marched under it.

### Immaterialism
- **What it is:** Berkeley's doctrine that there is no material substance: what we call physical objects are collections of ideas perceived by minds, and their being consists in being perceived (*esse est percipi*). Matter, as philosophers posited it — an unthinking substratum existing unperceived — is, Berkeley argued, an incoherent abstraction; spirits and their ideas are all that exist.
- **Core claims:** to be (for sensible things) is to be perceived; there is no mind-independent material substratum; abstract ideas of matter are impossible; objects continue to exist because God perceives them; the view claims to vindicate common sense against skepticism, not deny the real world.
- **Figures here:** [Berkeley](eras/03-early-modern/george-berkeley.md)
- **The label:** Unlike most labels here, this one is the philosopher's own: Berkeley used "immaterialism" for his system, though posterity more often files him under "idealism" or "empiricism."

### Deism
- **What it is:** The religion of reason that flourished in 17th- and 18th-century England and France: God exists and created the universe, but does not intervene in it — no miracles, no revelation, no providence to petition. True religion is the morality and worship discoverable by unaided reason, shared (deists held) beneath the corruptions of every priestcraft. Its classic English spokesmen (Herbert of Cherbury, Toland, Tindal) have no entries here; its famous continental champion is Voltaire.
- **Core claims:** God's existence and attributes are knowable by reason alone; revelation is unnecessary and historically unreliable; miracles violate the order God himself established; morality, not doctrine or ritual, is religion's core; religious persecution is irrational.
- **Figures here:** [Voltaire](eras/03-early-modern/bayle-and-voltaire.md)
- **The label:** "Deist" began as a term of abuse from orthodox critics; many so labeled, Voltaire included, accepted it while insisting they were theists against atheism on one side and superstition on the other.

### Materialism
- **What it is:** In its early modern form, the doctrine that matter in motion is all there fundamentally is: thought, life, and feeling are to be explained mechanically, without immaterial souls or final causes. Hobbes built it into a complete system — philosophy as the study of bodies and their motions, psychology and politics included; in the next century La Mettrie (*Man a Machine*, 1747) and Diderot pushed it toward living, sensing matter.
- **Core claims:** everything real is material or depends on matter; mental phenomena are bodily processes; explanation is mechanical — contact, motion, organization; teleology and incorporeal substance are fictions; religion's supernatural claims lose their basis.
- **Figures here:** [Hobbes](eras/03-early-modern/thomas-hobbes.md), [Cavendish](eras/03-early-modern/early-modern-women-philosophers.md), [Diderot](eras/03-early-modern/denis-diderot.md)
- **The label:** Cavendish shows the label's looseness: her matter is self-moving and perceptive ("vitalist materialism"), a deliberate middle path between Hobbes's dead mechanism and immaterial souls.

### Moral sense theory
- **What it is:** The 18th-century British theory that moral approval is the work of a dedicated faculty — a moral sense — rather than reason or self-interest alone. Shaftesbury introduced the idea of an inward sense that relishes virtue as the eye relishes harmony; Hutcheson systematized it and tied it to benevolence; Hume recast the territory as moral sentimentalism, grounding judgment in sympathy-corrected feeling.
- **Core claims:** we approve virtue immediately, by feeling, not by calculation; benevolence, not egoism, is what the sense approves; moral distinctions are perceived, not deduced; Hutcheson's formula — the best action procures the greatest happiness for the greatest numbers — anticipates utilitarianism; Butler adds that conscience, Hutcheson's sense included, claims authority over every other motive.
- **Figures here:** [Shaftesbury, Hutcheson & Butler](eras/03-early-modern/moral-sense-school.md), [Hume](eras/03-early-modern/david-hume.md)

### Common sense philosophy
- **What it is:** The Scottish school founded by Thomas Reid in reaction to the skeptical endpoint of the "way of ideas" running from Descartes through Locke to Hume. Reid argued that perception and memory rest on first principles of common sense — beliefs built into our constitution, which philosophy cannot coherently doubt and should not try to prove. James Beattie and others popularized the school, which dominated Scottish and much American teaching for decades.
- **Core claims:** the external world, other minds, and the reliability of our faculties are known directly, not inferred from ideas; skeptical conclusions indict the premises that produced them, not common sense; perception is an original operation of the mind, involving conception and irresistible belief; first principles are the starting points, not the conclusions, of philosophy.
- **Figures here:** [Reid](eras/03-early-modern/thomas-reid.md)
- **The label:** Reid's "common sense" is a technical term — the shared constitution of human judgment — not an appeal to popular opinion, though critics from Kant onward sometimes read it as the latter.

### The Enlightenment
- **What it is:** Not a doctrine but a movement: the 18th-century campaign, across Europe, to subject inherited authority — church, crown, custom — to the bar of reason and public criticism. It had no creed; its participants disagreed about religion, politics, and reason itself. Kant's 1784 answer to "What is Enlightenment?" — humanity's emergence from self-imposed immaturity, *sapere aude* — is its most famous self-description, and Hamann and Herder's Counter-Enlightenment protest (reason is embodied, linguistic, historical) grew up inside the same century.
- **Core claims:** knowledge and criticism should be public; toleration over dogma; laws and institutions answer to reason and human welfare; science and education drive progress; tradition carries no authority merely by being old.
- **Figures here:** [Bayle & Voltaire](eras/03-early-modern/bayle-and-voltaire.md), [Montesquieu](eras/03-early-modern/montesquieu.md), [Diderot](eras/03-early-modern/denis-diderot.md), [Kant](eras/03-early-modern/immanuel-kant.md), [Hamann & Herder](eras/03-early-modern/hamann-and-herder.md)
- **The label:** Contemporaries spoke of a *siècle des Lumières* or *Aufklärung*, but "the Enlightenment" as a single unified movement is a later historians' construction — plural "Enlightenments" (national, religious, radical) is now common scholarly usage.

### Liberalism
- **What it is:** In its early, classical form, the political doctrine that government exists by consent to protect individuals' natural rights — life, liberty, and property — and is limited by law, toleration, and constitutional forms. Locke's *Second Treatise* (1689) is its founding text here; Montesquieu added the separation of powers, and the tradition runs forward into the 19th century, where Mill restates it around individuality and the harm principle.
- **Core claims:** political authority rests on consent, not divine right or inheritance; individuals hold rights government may not violate; religious toleration is a right, not a concession; law should bind rulers as well as ruled; resistance to tyranny can be justified.
- **Figures here:** [Locke](eras/03-early-modern/john-locke.md), [Mill](eras/04-19th-century/john-stuart-mill.md)
- **The label:** The word is anachronistic for Locke: "liberalism" as a political name appears only in the early 19th century (from Spain's *liberales*), and was applied backward to the tradition he founded.

### Natural law theory
- **What it is:** A tradition, running from medieval scholasticism into the foundations of modern international law, holding that there is a law above human enactment — grounded in God, nature, or reason — by which positive laws and rulers can be judged. Suárez gave it its most rigorous scholastic form; Grotius famously held it would hold "even if we should concede... that there is no God" (*etiamsi daremus*), and built the law of nations and just war theory on it. Hobbes and Locke each transformed the inheritance — into sovereign command's backdrop and into natural rights.
- **Core claims:** some norms bind all peoples, prior to any legislator; unjust enactments fall short of true law; war and treaty-making are subject to moral rules; rights and obligations can be derived from human nature and reason; Pufendorf (no entry here) transmitted the system to the Enlightenment.
- **Figures here:** [Suárez & Grotius](eras/03-early-modern/suarez-and-grotius.md), [Hobbes](eras/03-early-modern/thomas-hobbes.md), [Locke](eras/03-early-modern/john-locke.md)

## 19th Century

### German idealism
- **What it is:** The movement in German philosophy running from Kant's critical turn through Fichte and Schelling to Hegel, united by the claim that mind is not a passive receiver of a ready-made world but actively constitutes experience and, in the later systems, reality itself. Kant belongs to it only as its founder: his successors took his transcendental idealism in directions — absolute idealism, the self-positing I, the identity of nature and spirit — that he would not have endorsed.
- **Core claims:** the mind structures all possible experience; freedom and self-consciousness are the starting points of philosophy; reason develops historically and dialectically; nature and spirit form one self-unfolding whole.
- **Figures here:** [Kant](eras/03-early-modern/immanuel-kant.md), [Johann Gottlieb Fichte](eras/04-19th-century/johann-gottlieb-fichte.md), [Schelling](eras/04-19th-century/friedrich-schelling.md), [Hegel](eras/04-19th-century/g-w-f-hegel.md)
- **The label:** A retrospective construction of later historians of philosophy; the thinkers themselves spoke of criticism, the Science of Knowledge, or absolute idealism, not of belonging to a school with this name.

### Romanticism
- **What it is:** As a philosophical movement, Romanticism is the reaction against Enlightenment rationalism that treated feeling, imagination, language, history, and organic nature as sources of truth in their own right. Its roots lie in Hamann's and Herder's critiques of abstract reason; it reaches systematic form in Schleiermacher's account of religion as intuition and feeling and in Schelling's philosophy of nature and art.
- **Core claims:** feeling and intuition disclose what analysis cannot; each culture and language expresses a distinctive spirit; nature is a living, productive whole, not a machine; art is a genuine organ of knowledge; the infinite is approached through the finite, not by abandoning it.
- **Figures here:** [Hamann & Herder](eras/03-early-modern/hamann-and-herder.md), [Schleiermacher](eras/04-19th-century/friedrich-schleiermacher.md), [Schelling](eras/04-19th-century/friedrich-schelling.md)
- **The label:** Borrowed from literature and the arts, where it named the revival of the medieval "romance"; its borders as a philosophy are correspondingly fuzzy, and several figures placed here also belong to German idealism.

### Pessimism
- **What it is:** The philosophical position that existence is fundamentally bad — that suffering is not an accident of life but its structure — and that non-existence would be preferable. Schopenhauer is its systematizer: building on his metaphysics of the blind, striving Will, he argued that desire guarantees dissatisfaction, since satisfaction is brief and boredom follows, so life swings between pain and boredom.
- **Core claims:** the world is driven by an aimless, insatiable Will; willing entails suffering, and pleasure is merely the temporary absence of pain; individuation is the source of conflict and egoism; release comes only through denial of the will, in aesthetic contemplation, compassion, and ascetic resignation.
- **Figures here:** [Schopenhauer](eras/04-19th-century/arthur-schopenhauer.md)
- **The label:** The word was coined in the eighteenth century as the antonym of Leibnizian "optimism"; Schopenhauer turned what had been a term of abuse in reviews into the name of a worked-out metaphysical system.

### Utilitarianism
- **What it is:** The moral theory that the right act, rule, or policy is the one that produces the greatest overall happiness — or, in negative form, the least suffering — counting everyone's welfare impartially. Bentham founded it as a program of legal and social reform, Mill defended and humanized it, and Sidgwick gave it its most rigorous and self-critical statement.
- **Core claims:** consequences alone determine rightness; happiness (pleasure and the absence of pain) is the sole intrinsic good; each person's good counts for exactly as much as anyone else's; morality and legislation should be judged by this single public standard.
- **Figures here:** [Bentham](eras/04-19th-century/jeremy-bentham.md), [Mill](eras/04-19th-century/john-stuart-mill.md), [Sidgwick](eras/04-19th-century/henry-sidgwick.md)
- **The label:** Bentham adopted "utilitarian" as the name of his sect before Mill's *Utilitarianism* (1861) fixed it as the theory's public title; Bentham himself usually preferred to speak of "the greatest happiness principle."

### Positivism
- **What it is:** Comte's doctrine that knowledge matures through three stages — theological, metaphysical, and finally positive (scientific) — and that genuine inquiry confines itself to observable relations and laws, renouncing questions about ultimate causes and essences. It was also a social program: a science of society, which Comte named sociology, would reorganize politics and morals on a factual basis.
- **Core claims:** the law of three stages governs every science and society itself; only phenomena and their laws are knowable; the sciences form a hierarchy crowned by sociology; social order should be rebuilt on positive knowledge rather than theology or metaphysics.
- **Figures here:** [Auguste Comte](eras/04-19th-century/auguste-comte.md)
- **The label:** Comte's own coinage, from *positif* in its senses of real, certain, and constructive; in his late work he refounded positivism as a Religion of Humanity, a development many admirers of the early doctrine declined to follow.

### Young Hegelians
- **What it is:** The radical wing of Hegel's school in the 1830s and 1840s, who turned his dialectic against the Prussian state and orthodox Christianity that the "Old Hegelians" used it to defend. Strauss's *Life of Jesus* (1835) opened the split; Feuerbach transformed Hegel's Spirit into human self-consciousness, Stirner dissolved every fixed idea into the ego, and the young Marx passed through the movement on his way beyond it.
- **Core claims:** religion is humanity's self-alienated projection of its own essence; Hegel's system, honestly applied, criticizes rather than sanctifies the existing order; philosophy must become practical and change the world it interprets; every abstraction set above individuals — God, state, morality — is a domination to be dissolved.
- **Figures here:** [Feuerbach & Stirner](eras/04-19th-century/sidebars-feuerbach-and-stirner.md), [Marx](eras/04-19th-century/karl-marx.md)
- **The label:** The name marks a generational split within Hegelianism rather than a shared system; its members diverged sharply, and Marx's *The German Ideology* is largely an attack on his former Young Hegelian allies.

### Marxism
- **What it is:** The theory of history, capital, and revolution developed from Marx's work: societies are shaped by their modes of production, history proceeds through class struggle, and capitalism generates the conditions of its own supersession. As an "-ism" it names both that body of theory and the political movements and states that later claimed its authority, often in forms Marx never saw.
- **Core claims:** the economic base conditions law, politics, and ideas; history is the history of class struggles; labor is the source of value, and surplus value the mechanism of exploitation; workers are alienated from their product, activity, and fellows under capital; philosophy should not only interpret the world but change it.
- **Figures here:** [Marx](eras/04-19th-century/karl-marx.md)
- **The label:** Marx reportedly distanced himself from his French admirers with "I am not a Marxist" — the remark survives through Engels's letters of 1890, which quote it against the doctrinaire "Marxism" of Marx's own lifetime.

### Transcendentalism
- **What it is:** The American movement centered on Emerson and the Concord circle, which fused Kantian and Romantic themes with Unitarian dissent and Eastern scriptures into a doctrine of self-trust: divinity is present in nature and in every soul, and intuition grasps truths that institutions and book-learning obscure. Thoreau gave it its most exacting practical form at Walden Pond.
- **Core claims:** nature is the symbol and manifestation of spirit; the individual conscience outranks churches, creeds, and conformity; truth is known by direct intuition, not inherited authority; a simple, self-reliant life is both a spiritual discipline and a social protest.
- **Figures here:** [Emerson & Thoreau](eras/04-19th-century/emerson-and-thoreau.md)
- **The label:** Emerson traced the name to Kant's "transcendental" philosophy, filtered through Coleridge; contemporaries first used it half-mockingly for views they found obscure, and the movement adopted it in Emerson's 1842 lecture "The Transcendentalist."

### British idealism
- **What it is:** The dominant school of British philosophy from the 1870s to the First World War, which answered empiricism and utilitarianism with a Hegelian-inspired absolute idealism. Green grounded ethics and politics in an eternal consciousness realizing itself in social life; Bradley pressed the logic of relations toward a monism in which finite things are appearance and only the Absolute is fully real.
- **Core claims:** reality is a single, coherent spiritual whole; thought and its object cannot be pried apart as empiricism supposes; the self and the social order are internally related, not externally assembled; contradiction in finite experience points beyond appearance to the Absolute.
- **Figures here:** [T. H. Green & F. H. Bradley](eras/04-19th-century/british-idealists.md)
- **The label:** Largely a historian's grouping — its members were also called British Hegelians — and the school effectively ended when Russell and Moore, both formed inside it, led the analytic revolt against it.

### Pragmatism
- **What it is:** The American philosophy, founded by Peirce and popularized by James, that tests concepts and beliefs by their practical consequences: the meaning of an idea is the difference it would make to experience and conduct. Dewey, its third founder, developed it into a general logic of inquiry and a philosophy of education and democracy.
- **Core claims:** meaning is fixed by conceivable practical effects (Peirce's pragmatic maxim); truth is what inquiry would converge on, or what proves itself in experience, not a static copy of reality; ideas are instruments for solving problems, not mirrors of nature; fallibilism — every belief is revisable — is the method's governing attitude.
- **Figures here:** [Peirce](eras/04-19th-century/charles-sanders-peirce.md), [James](eras/04-19th-century/william-james.md), [John Dewey](eras/05-20th-century/john-dewey.md)
- **The label:** Peirce took "pragmatism" from Kant's *pragmatisch*; when James's looser version took over the name, Peirce renamed his own doctrine "pragmaticism" (1905), a word he called ugly enough to be safe from kidnappers.

### Feminism
- **What it is:** In its first-wave philosophical form, the argument that women's subordination is a social arrangement, not a natural fact, and that women are owed equal education, legal standing, property, and the vote. Wollstonecraft is its forerunner, grounding the claim in Enlightenment reason; Harriet Taylor Mill and John Stuart Mill gave it its classic liberal statement in *The Enfranchisement of Women* (1851) and *The Subjection of Women* (1869).
- **Core claims:** the apparent inferiority of women is produced by denied education and enforced dependence; no one may rightly decide another class of persons' sphere for them; legal and social equality follows from the same principles already granted to men; custom and prejudice, not nature, are the obstacles.
- **Figures here:** [Mary Wollstonecraft](eras/03-early-modern/mary-wollstonecraft.md), [Harriet Taylor Mill](eras/04-19th-century/harriet-taylor-mill.md), [Mill](eras/04-19th-century/john-stuart-mill.md)
- **The label:** Retrospective for all three figures: the word *féminisme* was coined in French decades after Wollstonecraft wrote (it is often traced to Fourier, 1837) and entered general use only in the 1880s and 1890s.

## 20th Century

### Phenomenology
- **What it is:** A method and movement founded by Edmund Husserl that studies experience from the first-person point of view, describing the structures of consciousness — how things are given to us — before explaining them. The phenomenologist "brackets" assumptions about the external world (the *epochē*) to attend to intentionality: consciousness is always consciousness *of* something.
- **Core claims:** consciousness is intentional, directed at objects; description of lived experience precedes causal explanation; the lifeworld of everyday experience is the ground of all theory; later versions (Heidegger, Merleau-Ponty) make existence and the body, not pure consciousness, the starting point.
- **Figures here:** [Husserl](eras/05-20th-century/edmund-husserl.md), [Heidegger](eras/05-20th-century/martin-heidegger.md), [Merleau-Ponty](eras/05-20th-century/maurice-merleau-ponty.md)
- **The label:** Husserl launched the name with the slogan "back to the things themselves"; his successors transformed the method so thoroughly that phenomenology names a family of projects rather than one doctrine.

### Existentialism
- **What it is:** A loose movement centered on individual existence: freedom, choice, anxiety, death, and the responsibility of making oneself in a world without a given meaning or essence. Its best-known formula is Sartre's "existence precedes essence" — we are not born with a nature that decides what we must become.
- **Core claims:** existence precedes essence; human beings are radically free and responsible for their choices; anxiety and the awareness of death disclose that freedom; "bad faith" is the evasion of it; meaning is made, not found.
- **Figures here:** [Kierkegaard](eras/04-19th-century/soren-kierkegaard.md) (the great forerunner), [Karl Jaspers](eras/05-20th-century/karl-jaspers.md), [Sartre](eras/05-20th-century/jean-paul-sartre.md), [Beauvoir](eras/05-20th-century/simone-de-beauvoir.md), [Camus](eras/05-20th-century/albert-camus.md)
- **The label:** Sartre accepted the name in his 1945 lecture *Existentialism Is a Humanism* and made it famous; Camus explicitly rejected it, insisting he was not an existentialist, and Kierkegaard, who died in 1855, never heard the word.

### Logical positivism
- **What it is:** The program of the Vienna Circle in the 1920s–30s: philosophy should be the logical clarification of science, and a statement is meaningful only if it is either analytic (true by definition or logic) or empirically verifiable. Metaphysics, theology, and much traditional ethics fail that test and are dismissed as literally meaningless rather than false.
- **Core claims:** the verifiability criterion of meaning; philosophy as the logic of science, not a rival source of knowledge; the unity of science in one empirical language; metaphysical statements are pseudo-statements.
- **Figures here:** [Vienna Circle](eras/05-20th-century/vienna-circle-logical-positivism.md), [Rudolf Carnap](eras/05-20th-century/rudolf-carnap.md)
- **The label:** The Circle called its outlook the "scientific world-conception" in its 1929 manifesto; "logical positivism" was the name that stuck in the English-speaking world, where A. J. Ayer's *Language, Truth and Logic* (1936) popularized it.

### Analytic philosophy
- **What it is:** Not a doctrine but a tradition and style: philosophy done through the analysis of language, logic, and concepts, prizing clarity, argument, and piecemeal problems over grand systems. It began around 1900 with Frege's logic and the revolts of Russell and Moore against British idealism, and became the dominant approach in the English-speaking world.
- **Core claims:** no shared creed — the common marks are respect for logic and science; suspicion of obscurity; progress through small, well-defined questions; concepts and language as the philosopher's primary instruments.
- **Figures here:** [Frege](eras/05-20th-century/gottlob-frege.md), [Russell](eras/05-20th-century/bertrand-russell.md), [G. E. Moore](eras/05-20th-century/g-e-moore.md), [Wittgenstein](eras/05-20th-century/wittgenstein-early.md), [Quine](eras/05-20th-century/w-v-o-quine.md)
- **The label:** "Analytic" names a method, not a set of conclusions — its practitioners (a theist like Moore's successors, a naturalist like Quine) have disagreed about almost everything else, which is why historians treat it as a tradition rather than a school.

### Ordinary language philosophy
- **What it is:** A mid-20th-century Oxford-centered approach holding that many philosophical puzzles arise from misunderstanding how words are actually used, and dissolve when that use is carefully described. It draws on the later Wittgenstein's idea that meaning is use, and on his picture of "language-games" embedded in forms of life.
- **Core claims:** meaning is use in ordinary practice; philosophical confusion is often a grammatical illusion; careful attention to what we say when is a philosophical method; many traditional problems need therapy, not solutions.
- **Figures here:** [Wittgenstein](eras/05-20th-century/wittgenstein-late.md), [Austin & Strawson](eras/05-20th-century/austin-and-strawson.md), [Gilbert Ryle](eras/05-20th-century/gilbert-ryle.md)
- **The label:** Its practitioners rarely used the name themselves — Ryle preferred to speak of "conceptual geography" — and the movement faded by the 1970s as analytic philosophy turned back to systematic theory.

### Hermeneutics
- **What it is:** The theory of interpretation: originally the art of reading scripture and classical texts, it became, through Dilthey and Gadamer, a general account of how human beings understand anything — texts, history, other people, themselves. Understanding is never presuppositionless: we always approach a subject with fore-conceptions formed by tradition, and interpretation moves in a "hermeneutic circle" between part and whole.
- **Core claims:** all understanding is interpretation; prejudice (pre-judgment) from tradition is its condition, not merely its obstacle; meaning emerges in a "fusion of horizons" between interpreter and text; the human sciences understand (*verstehen*) where natural science explains.
- **Figures here:** [Dilthey & Collingwood](eras/04-19th-century/dilthey-and-collingwood.md) (Dilthey as forerunner), [Hans-Georg Gadamer](eras/05-20th-century/hans-georg-gadamer.md), [Paul Ricoeur](eras/05-20th-century/paul-ricoeur.md)
- **The label:** The word descends from Hermes, messenger of the gods; Gadamer's *Truth and Method* (1960) deliberately provocatively argues that the human sciences' truth is not exhausted by scientific method.

### Critical theory / the Frankfurt School
- **What it is:** The project of the Institute for Social Research, founded in Frankfurt in 1923: a Marxist-inspired but interdisciplinary critique of modern capitalist society, its culture industry, and the forms of reason that dominate nature and people alike. Where orthodox Marxism expected revolution, critical theory asked why emancipation kept failing.
- **Core claims:** enlightenment reason turns into a new mythology of domination; mass culture (the "culture industry") manufactures consent and pseudo-individuality; critique must be immanent, judging society by its own professed norms; theory serves emancipation, not neutral description.
- **Figures here:** [Horkheimer](eras/05-20th-century/frankfurt-school.md), [Adorno](eras/05-20th-century/frankfurt-school.md), [Benjamin](eras/05-20th-century/frankfurt-school.md), [Habermas](eras/05-20th-century/jurgen-habermas.md)
- **The label:** Horkheimer's 1937 essay "Traditional and Critical Theory" gave the program its name; Habermas, of the second generation, rebuilt it around communication and democracy rather than the first generation's pessimism.

### Structuralism
- **What it is:** A method, imported from Saussure's linguistics and Lévi-Strauss's anthropology, that explains cultural phenomena — myths, kinship, texts — by the underlying systems of relations and differences that produce them, rather than by individual intentions or origins. The subject is decentered: structures speak through people more than people speak through structures.
- **Core claims:** meaning arises from differences within a system, not from things themselves; surface phenomena express deep relational structures; the autonomous human subject is an effect, not the source, of meaning; synchrony (the system at a moment) takes priority over diachrony.
- **Figures here:** no entry in this repo is a structuralist proper; its pressure is felt in [Foucault](eras/05-20th-century/michel-foucault.md) and [Deleuze](eras/05-20th-century/gilles-deleuze.md), who absorbed its methods while breaking with it.
- **The label:** Lévi-Strauss, structuralism's central figure, has no entry in this repo; the movement is included here because the poststructuralists catalogued next are unintelligible without it.

### Poststructuralism
- **What it is:** A retrospective label for a generation of French thinkers who took structuralism's decentering of the subject and meaning further, arguing that structures themselves are unstable: texts undermine their own oppositions (Derrida's deconstruction), power produces the subjects and truths it claims merely to govern (Foucault), and desire and difference resist every fixed code (Deleuze).
- **Core claims:** binary oppositions (speech/writing, nature/culture) are hierarchies that texts themselves subvert; meaning is deferred and differential, never fully present; power and knowledge are mutually constitutive; grand unifying systems are suspect.
- **Figures here:** [Derrida](eras/05-20th-century/jacques-derrida.md), [Foucault](eras/05-20th-century/michel-foucault.md), [Deleuze](eras/05-20th-century/gilles-deleuze.md)
- **The label:** Largely an Anglo-American coinage of the 1970s for grouping these thinkers in literature departments; none of them campaigned under it, and Foucault flatly denied being a structuralist, let alone a poststructuralist.

### Postmodernism
- **What it is:** A contested umbrella term for the suspicion, across philosophy, art, and architecture from the 1970s on, that the modern era's grand legitimating stories — progress, reason, emancipation, science as the one true account — have lost their credibility. Jean-François Lyotard's *The Postmodern Condition* (1979) defined it, "simplifying to the extreme," as "incredulity toward metanarratives."
- **Core claims:** metanarratives no longer legitimate knowledge; knowledge is fragmented into local language-games and performances; high and low culture, original and copy, blur; truth claims are entangled with power and institutions.
- **Figures here:** Lyotard has no entry in this repo; the label is most often applied, loosely, to [Foucault](eras/05-20th-century/michel-foucault.md), [Derrida](eras/05-20th-century/jacques-derrida.md), and [Deleuze](eras/05-20th-century/gilles-deleuze.md).
- **The label:** The loosest in this catalogue — Foucault called himself a historian, Derrida resisted the term, and critics such as Habermas attacked "postmodernism" as a polemical target as much as a position anyone held.

### Feminism
- **What it is:** In philosophy, the analysis and criticism of women's subordination and of the gendered assumptions built into concepts, canons, and institutions. Beauvoir's *The Second Sex* (1949) founded its modern form — "one is not born, but rather becomes, a woman" — and later waves pressed further, into psychoanalysis, language, and the performance of gender itself.
- **Core claims:** gender is socially made, not natural destiny; women's experience is a standpoint philosophy has ignored; the canon and its categories are not neutral; (second wave) the personal is political; (third wave) gender is performed and identities intersect.
- **Figures here:** [Beauvoir](eras/05-20th-century/simone-de-beauvoir.md), [Irigaray](eras/05-20th-century/feminist-philosophy-after-beauvoir.md), [Kristeva](eras/05-20th-century/feminist-philosophy-after-beauvoir.md), [Judith Butler](eras/05-20th-century/feminist-philosophy-after-beauvoir.md); adjacent figures who write on women, care, and attention from partly different premises include [Iris Murdoch](eras/05-20th-century/iris-murdoch.md), [Mary Midgley](eras/05-20th-century/mary-midgley.md), and [Martha Nussbaum](eras/05-20th-century/martha-nussbaum.md).
- **The label:** The wave metaphor (first-wave suffrage, second-wave 1960s–80s liberation, third-wave 1990s difference and performativity) is a convenient simplification that historians of feminism themselves dispute.

### Libertarianism
- **What it is:** The political philosophy that individual rights — above all self-ownership and property — strictly limit what the state may do: no redistribution, no paternalism, no conscription of persons or goods for social goals. Robert Nozick's *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* (1974) is its philosophical landmark, written as a direct reply to Rawls.
- **Core claims:** individuals have rights that may not be violated even for the general good; only a minimal "night-watchman" state is justifiable; holdings justly acquired and transferred may not be patterned by redistribution; taxation of earnings is, in Nozick's phrase, on a par with forced labor.
- **Figures here:** [Robert Nozick](eras/05-20th-century/robert-nozick.md)
- **The label:** The word earlier named a left-wing, often anarchist anti-authoritarianism (and still does in much of Europe); its now-dominant pro-property meaning is an American redefinition of the 1950s–70s.

### Communitarianism
- **What it is:** A critique of liberal individualism that emerged in the 1980s: the self is not an unencumbered chooser standing before its values but is constituted by the communities, traditions, and narratives that formed it — so justice and the good cannot be specified in abstraction from shared ways of life. Alasdair MacIntyre's *After Virtue* (1981) is its deepest statement.
- **Core claims:** identity is narrative and tradition-constituted; liberal neutrality between conceptions of the good is impossible and undesirable; virtues are intelligible only within practices and traditions; rights-talk without a shared good produces moral fragmentation.
- **Figures here:** [Alasdair MacIntyre](eras/05-20th-century/alasdair-macintyre.md), defined in debate with [Rawls](eras/05-20th-century/john-rawls.md)
- **The label:** A label of the seminar room more than the manifesto — MacIntyre has repeatedly denied being a communitarian, and the "liberal–communitarian debate" names a conversation among Rawls's critics (Sandel, Taylor, Walzer) as much as a movement.

### Functionalism (philosophy of mind)
- **What it is:** The theory that mental states are defined not by their physical stuff but by their causal role — what they do, their relations to inputs, outputs, and other states. Pain is whatever occupies the pain-role, so minds could in principle be realized in brains, silicon, or any suitable material ("multiple realizability"); Putnam developed the view in the 1960s from the analogy between minds and computing machines.
- **Core claims:** mental states are individuated by function, not by physical composition; the same mind-type can be realized in different materials; psychology is autonomous from neuroscience in the way software is from hardware; folk-psychological roles are real explanations.
- **Figures here:** [Putnam](eras/05-20th-century/analytic-philosophy-after-quine.md), [Philosophy of mind after Ryle](eras/05-20th-century/philosophy-of-mind-after-ryle.md)
- **The label:** Putnam himself later abandoned the theory he had founded — his "meaning ain't in the head" externalism and his critique of functionalism in *Representation and Reality* (1988) are part of the same career.

### Externalism
- **What it is:** The thesis that the contents of thoughts and the meanings of words are not fixed solely by what is inside the head, but depend partly on the physical and social environment. Putnam's Twin Earth thought experiment (1975) — a molecule-for-molecule duplicate where "water" is a different substance, XYZ — argues that duplicates can mean different things; Kripke's causal-historical account makes reference depend on chains of use reaching back to an initial baptism.
- **Core claims:** meanings "just ain't in the head"; reference is fixed partly by causal links to the world; linguistic competence is a division of labor with experts and community; mental content is wide, not narrow.
- **Figures here:** [Putnam](eras/05-20th-century/analytic-philosophy-after-quine.md), [Saul Kripke](eras/05-20th-century/saul-kripke.md)
- **The label:** The position is defined against "internalism," its traditional opposite; Burge extended the argument from words to thoughts themselves (anti-individualism), making externalism the default view in much philosophy of mind and language since the 1970s.
