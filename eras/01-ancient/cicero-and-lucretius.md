## At a glance

- **Dates:** Cicero, 106–43 BCE; Lucretius, c. 99–c. 55 BCE
- **Place:** Cicero born at Arpinum, active in Rome; Lucretius' life almost entirely unknown
- **School/tradition:** Both brought Greek Hellenistic philosophy into Latin — Cicero as an Academic skeptic surveying every school, Lucretius as a committed Epicurean
- **Known for:** Cicero: translating and inventing a Latin vocabulary for philosophy, and works on skepticism, the gods, duties, and natural law. Lucretius: *On the Nature of Things*, the fullest surviving account of Epicurean physics — atoms, the swerve, the mortal soul, and "death is nothing to us."

**Responds to:** [Epicurus](epicurus.md), [Zeno of Citium & the Stoics](zeno-of-citium-and-the-stoics.md), [Pyrrho & the Skeptics](pyrrho-of-elis-and-the-skeptics.md), [Democritus](democritus-of-abdera.md), [Plato](plato.md) · **Answered by:** [Augustine](../02-medieval/augustine-of-hippo.md) (converted to philosophy by Cicero's lost *Hortensius*), [Aquinas](../02-medieval/thomas-aquinas.md) (natural law), and the Renaissance readers who recovered Lucretius

## Why these two together

Greek philosophy was written in Greek, for Greeks. The question of the first century BCE was whether it could live in Latin, for Romans — a practical people who, as Cicero admits, cared more for law, war, and governance than for abstract theory. Cicero and Lucretius are the two Romans of that generation who proved it could, and they did so from opposite ends of the philosophical world. They never form a school together; pairing them shows a single hinge in intellectual history from two sides.

Cicero (106–43 BCE) was a statesman who turned to philosophy when politics shut him out, and who tried to put the *whole* of Greek thought into readable Latin prose — skeptic, Stoic, Epicurean, and Academic views alike, each given a hearing and then weighed. Lucretius (c. 99–c. 55 BCE), his near-contemporary, did the opposite: he committed himself wholly to one school, Epicureanism, and poured its physics into six books of Latin verse. Cicero even praised that poem in a surviving letter. Between them they are why so much Hellenistic philosophy survived into the Latin West at all — and why its basic vocabulary is still Latin.

## Biography

### Cicero

Marcus Tullius Cicero was born on January 3, 106 BCE, at Arpinum, a hill town southeast of Rome, into a family of local standing but no senatorial pedigree. As a *novus homo* — a "new man" without consular ancestors — he climbed the Roman ladder of offices on the strength of his oratory, reaching the consulship, the highest office in the republic, in 63 BCE. As consul he suppressed the conspiracy of Catiline, executing several conspirators without trial; the act made his name and later cost him an exile. He studied philosophy seriously in Athens and Rhodes and kept company with teachers of every school, but professed lifelong loyalty to the Academy.

Cicero's philosophical writing came in bursts, almost always when politics barred him from public life. The dialogues on the state and the laws belong to the troubled 50s. Then, after civil war left Julius Caesar in sole power and Cicero without a political role, and after the death of his beloved daughter Tullia in childbirth, he produced in barely two years (c. 46–44 BCE) a torrent of philosophical works. He described the project frankly as an attempt to cover "the whole of philosophy" for Roman readers. After Caesar's assassination in 44 he re-entered politics with a series of ferocious speeches against Mark Antony. It killed him: proscribed in the second triumvirate, he was put to death on Antony's orders in 43 BCE near Formiae.

### Lucretius

Of Titus Lucretius Carus we know almost nothing certain. Scholars place his life from the mid-90s to the mid-50s BCE (hence the conventional c. 99–c. 55). Our one contemporary trace is a letter Cicero wrote to his brother Quintus in February 54 BCE, agreeing that Lucretius' poem shows both brilliance and craftsmanship — a remark that also fixes the poem's rough date. The poem itself is addressed to a Roman aristocrat named Memmius, most likely Gaius Memmius, praetor in 58 BCE, which suggests a relationship of patronage.

Everything more biographical is late and shaky. Writing four centuries later, Jerome reports that Lucretius was born in 94 BCE, was driven mad by a love potion, composed his poem in the lucid intervals between fits, and killed himself — after which Cicero edited the work for publication. Almost none of this can be trusted. It does not fit the high regard Lucretius enjoyed among the next generation of poets, and it reads instead like later Christian hostility to a philosopher who denied that the gods run the world; Lactantius, who mines Lucretius for Epicurean doctrine, simply calls him "mad." The one plausible nugget is that Lucretius died before finishing the poem, which does show signs of lacking a final revision.

## Major theories and concepts

### Cicero: Academic skepticism as a method

Cicero's allegiance was to the New (skeptical) Academy, the tradition begun by Arcesilaus and best known through Carneades. Its method was not to assert doctrines but to argue every question on both sides and then follow whichever case was, for now, the more persuasive. This suited Cicero exactly. It let him expound Stoic, Epicurean, and Academic positions in turn, withhold final assent, and still act and decide in practice.

The engine of the position is a theory of judgment. The Stoics held there are "cataleptic" impressions — perceptions so clear they can be grasped as certainly true. The skeptic denies that any impression wears such a guarantee on its face, and so refuses certainty. The obvious objection, which Cicero stages through the character Lucullus, is that this paralyzes action: without certainty, how do you ever decide anything? Cicero's reply, borrowed from Carneades and Clitomachus, is that the skeptic lives by *plausibility* (in Latin *probabile*, or *veri simile*, "resembling the truth"). You can "approve" an impression as plausible and act on it without "completely" assenting that it is true. Drink the water that looks and smells clean, while granting it could still turn out foul. You were not wrong, because you only ever took it to be plausible. The tension critics press is whether plausibility can mean anything without a prior grip on truth — can something "resemble the truth" if truth itself is out of reach?

### Cicero: the gods, duties, and natural law

In *On the Nature of the Gods* Cicero sets three theologies against one another: the Epicurean view (spoken by Velleius), on which the gods exist but are blissfully detached and do not govern the world; the Stoic view (Balbus), on which a providential god orders the universe down to the smallest detail; and the Academic critique (Cotta), which takes both apart. Cicero stages himself as a young listener and renders no final verdict — the dialogue form *is* the skeptical method at work.

His ethics and politics pull in a more committed direction. In *On Duties* he works largely from Stoic materials to ask how one should actually behave — how the honorable and the useful relate, and what we owe others in concrete roles and circumstances. And in the *Republic* and the *Laws* he argues for a genuine natural law: a universal standard, the same for all peoples and all times, with authority over any particular city's statutes. This law, he says, is in effect divine reason; human laws are to be judged against it, and an unjust statute is not truly law at all. Here the skeptic who suspends judgment elsewhere commits himself, which is one of the standing puzzles about Cicero.

### Lucretius: atoms, void, and the swerve

Lucretius' poem is an exposition of the physics of [Epicurus](epicurus.md), itself built on the atomism of [Democritus](democritus-of-abdera.md). Reality is atoms and void: tiny, indestructible bodies moving through empty space, combining and dissolving. Nothing comes from nothing and nothing passes into nothing; every object, the soul and the cosmos included, is a temporary cluster of atoms that will one day scatter. This dissolves the need for gods who build or steer the world.

To this inheritance Lucretius gives the fullest surviving statement of one famous addition: the *swerve* (Latin *clinamen*). Atoms falling through the void at equal speed would travel in parallel lines forever and never meet, so no world could form. Lucretius says they deviate — a minimal, random shift, at no fixed time or place — just enough to begin colliding. The swerve does a second job too: it breaks the otherwise unbroken chain of cause and effect, leaving room for voluntary action, so that the will is not locked inside an infinite causal sequence. It is worth flagging honestly that the swerve is attributed to Epicurus in our sources but appears in none of his own surviving writings; how much weight he placed on it, and exactly how a random swerve is supposed to underwrite responsible agency rather than mere chance, are debated to this day.

### Lucretius: the mortal soul and the fear of death

If the soul is just another atomic compound, it forms with the body and dissolves with it. Book 3 is built to drive this home, piling up a long catalogue — around thirty arguments — that the soul is mortal: it grows and ages with the body, is dulled by wine and disease, and cannot plausibly survive once the body that sustains it is gone.

From this follows the Epicurean conclusion the whole poem aims at: **death is nothing to us**. Where there is no sensation there is no subject left to be harmed, so "being dead" is not a state anyone endures. To dread it, Lucretius argues, is a confusion — you imagine yourself somehow present at your own non-existence, watching the worms or the dark. His most striking move is the *symmetry argument*: we feel no horror at the infinite time *before* we were born, so we have no more reason to dread the matching stretch *after* we die. The point is therapeutic. Physics is studied not for its own sake but to remove the terror that poisons life.

## Key works

- **Cicero, *On the Republic* (De re publica)** (begun c. 54 BCE) — a dialogue on the best form of state and on justice; survives only in fragments, but carries the core of his natural-law argument.
- **Cicero, *On the Laws* (De legibus)** (c. 52 BCE, possibly never finished) — the companion piece, grounding law in nature and divine reason.
- **Cicero, *Academica* (On Academic Scepticism)** (c. 45 BCE) — his defense of the skeptical method; survives in damaged, overlapping editions.
- **Cicero, *On Ends* (De finibus bonorum et malorum)** (c. 45 BCE) — a survey and critique of Epicurean, Stoic, and Academic accounts of the highest good.
- **Cicero, *Tusculan Disputations*** (c. 45 BCE) — on death, pain, and the management of the emotions; consolation as philosophy.
- **Cicero, *On the Nature of the Gods* (De natura deorum)** (c. 45 BCE) — the three-cornered debate on divine providence; a primary source for Epicurean and Stoic theology alike.
- **Cicero, *On Duties* (De officiis)** (44 BCE) — his last major work, on practical conduct and obligation; hugely influential for later ethics.
- **Lucretius, *On the Nature of Things* (De rerum natura)** (c. 50s BCE) — six books of Latin hexameter verse: atoms and void (1–2), the mortal soul and the fear of death (3), sensation and thought (4), the origins of the world and of civilization (5), and natural explanations of frightening phenomena (6). It opens, strikingly for an Epicurean work, with a hymn to Venus.

## Contributions

Cicero built the Latin language of philosophy. Translating Greek into Latin, he minted words where none existed; through that work we get, among many others, the English *morals*, *property*, *individual*, *science*, *image*, and *appetite*. He also preserved, in his surveys, much of what we know about the Hellenistic schools, and he introduced the philosophical dialogue into Latin literature. Lucretius' contribution is narrower and deeper: he gave the Epicurean system its fullest and most eloquent surviving expression, and proved that rigorous physics could be done in poetry. Without his poem our picture of ancient atomism would be far thinner.

## Impact and legacy

Cicero became the schoolroom of the Latin West. His prose was the model of style for centuries, and his moral and political works were read continuously where much Greek philosophy was lost. *On Duties* shaped Christian and Enlightenment ethics; his natural-law argument fed directly into medieval theories, [Aquinas](../02-medieval/thomas-aquinas.md) among them, and on into modern ideas of universal human rights. His lost *Hortensius* famously turned the young [Augustine](../02-medieval/augustine-of-hippo.md) toward philosophy.

Lucretius' afterlife is one of the great stories in the transmission of ideas. His poem survived the Middle Ages in a mere handful of manuscripts and nearly vanished; it reached us through two ninth-century Carolingian copies. In 1417 the humanist and papal secretary Poggio Bracciolini found a copy in a monastery library (probably the Benedictine abbey of Fulda) and put it back into circulation. Its return — materialist, godless, insisting the soul dies with the body — helped feed Renaissance naturalism and, in the seventeenth century, Pierre Gassendi's project of a Christianized atomism, which in turn opened space for the new science.

## Criticisms and debates

- **Was Cicero original?** He was long dismissed as a mere compiler who dressed up Greek sources. Modern scholars defend him: the choice of what to translate, how to stage the debates, and where to commit was itself philosophical work.
- **The skeptic's consistency.** Critics ancient and modern press the old charge that denying certainty is self-undermining — is it certain that nothing is certain? — and that plausibility smuggles in the very notion of truth it claims to withhold.
- **The swerve.** Does a random atomic deviation really secure free will, or merely swap determinism for chance? And is the swerve Epicurus' own doctrine or a later development, given its absence from his surviving texts?
- **The fear of death.** The symmetry argument invites the reply that the two voids are not symmetrical: death deprives me of a future I would otherwise have had, whereas the time before birth deprived me of nothing. Whether deprivation can harm a subject who no longer exists is still a live debate.

## Misreadings to avoid

- Cicero was not a doctrinaire member of one school. As an Academic skeptic he could expound Stoicism or Epicureanism in detail without endorsing it, so no single speaker in his dialogues simply *is* "what Cicero thought."
- *On the Nature of Things* is not an attack on religion for its own sake. Its aim is therapeutic — to remove fear — not to shock.
- Lucretius' "death is nothing to us" is not a counsel of despair or indifference to life. The reasoning runs the other way: freed from dread of the afterlife, a finite life can be lived fully and gratefully.
- The love-potion-and-suicide story about Lucretius is late, hostile legend, not biography.

## What to read first

1. Cicero, *On Duties*, Book 1 — the most accessible way into his ethics.
2. Lucretius, *On the Nature of Things*, Book 3 — the mortal soul and the argument against fearing death, the heart of the poem.
3. Cicero, *On the Nature of the Gods*, Book 1 — the Epicurean theology and its Academic critique, which pairs naturally with Lucretius.
4. A modern introduction ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/), "Cicero" and "Lucretius") for the skepticism and swerve debates.
