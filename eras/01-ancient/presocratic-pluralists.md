## At a glance

- **Dates:** Xenophanes of Colophon (c. 570 – c. 478 BCE) · Anaxagoras of Clazomenae (c. 500 – c. 428 BCE) (conventional; both chronologies are reconstructed from late reports)
- **Place:** Colophon in Ionia, then Sicily and southern Italy (Xenophanes); Clazomenae in Ionia, Athens for twenty to thirty years, then Lampsacus on the Hellespont (Anaxagoras)
- **School/tradition:** Ionian inquiry into nature carried abroad; Xenophanes a poet-critic with no school, Anaxagoras the first philosopher known to have lived in Athens
- **Known for:** Xenophanes' attack on gods made in human image, his "one god," and the first Greek reflection on the limits of human knowledge; Anaxagoras' cosmic Mind (*Nous*), "a share of everything in everything," the sun as a red-hot stone, his impiety trial, and the book that disappointed Socrates

**Responds to:** [Thales of Miletus](thales-of-miletus.md), [Pythagoras](pythagoras.md), [Parmenides](parmenides-of-elea.md) · **Answered by:** [Heraclitus](heraclitus-of-ephesus.md), [Empedocles](empedocles.md), [Democritus](democritus-of-abdera.md), [Socrates](socrates.md), [Plato](plato.md), [Aristotle](aristotle.md), [Pyrrho & the Skeptics](pyrrho-of-elis-and-the-skeptics.md)

**Why they are grouped here.** Only Anaxagoras is a *pluralist* in the strict sense: a thinker who, after [Parmenides](parmenides-of-elea.md) argued that nothing can come from nothing, explained change by many basic ingredients that never arise or perish, as [Empedocles](empedocles.md) and [Democritus](democritus-of-abdera.md) also did. Xenophanes came two generations earlier. They share an entry because they open and close the same story. Both were Ionians who left home and carried the new inquiry into nature to new audiences, and both separated the divine, or the ordering power of the world, from anything shaped like a human body. Xenophanes removed the human face from the gods; Anaxagoras put a Mind in charge of the cosmos and then, in his critics' eyes, gave it too little to do.

## Biography

**Xenophanes** was born in Colophon, an Ionian Greek city near the coast of modern Turkey. Diogenes Laertius, writing some seven centuries later, says he flourished in the 540s BCE, was banished from his city, and lived in Sicily and at Elea in southern Italy. Xenophanes himself says, in a late poem (fragment B8 in the standard Diels–Kranz numbering, "DK," used throughout), that his thoughts had been tossed about the Greek lands for sixty-seven years, starting from the age of twenty-five, so he lived into his nineties. He was a travelling poet who recited his own verse, much of it at symposia (formal drinking parties). Ancient writers called some of his poems *silloi*, satires. About forty-five fragments survive, quoted by later authors: Clement of Alexandria and Sextus Empiricus preserve the lines on the gods, Athenaeus the drinking-party poems.

**Anaxagoras** was born around 500 BCE in Clazomenae, another Ionian city, to a landed family; Diogenes Laertius says he gave up his inheritance for philosophy. He moved to Athens, where he lived for twenty to thirty years and became a friend of the statesman Pericles. His fame grew when a large stone fell from the sky at Aegospotami in 466 BCE, which matched his claim that heavy stony bodies circle in the heavens. He also gave the first correct Greek account of eclipses. His claim that the sun, traditionally a god, is a red-hot stone led to a charge of impiety. He left Athens for Lampsacus, where he died in 428 BCE and was remembered for many years with a children's festival. The dates of his arrival, his book and his trial are all disputed: proposals for the trial range from the 450s to 430 BCE. He wrote a single book. Plato's Socrates says copies could be bought in Athens for a drachma at most. Twenty-two fragments survive, most of them quoted by the sixth-century CE commentator Simplicius.

## Major theories and concepts

### Xenophanes: gods made in our image

Homer and Hesiod, the poets every Greek learned from, told of gods who steal, commit adultery and deceive one another. Xenophanes objected that these are things people are blamed for (B11). He then made a broader point. People everywhere picture gods like themselves: Ethiopians say theirs are snub-nosed and black, Thracians that theirs are blue-eyed and red-haired (B16). If horses and cattle could draw, he adds, they would draw horse-gods and cattle-gods (B15). The argument is a debunking one. If our picture of the gods tracks our own appearance and customs, it tells us about ourselves, not about the gods. This is the first known critique of *anthropomorphism*, describing the divine in human form.

### Xenophanes: the one god

Xenophanes also offers a positive picture. There is "one god, greatest among gods and men," unlike mortals in body or thought (B23). This god sees and hears as a whole (B24), shakes all things by thought alone without effort (B25), and stays always in the same place, since moving about would not befit it (B26). The tension is plain in B23 itself: "one god" sits beside "gods" in the plural. That is why scholars dispute whether this is monotheism, or one supreme god among lesser ones.

### Xenophanes: what we can know

Xenophanes is the first Greek thinker on record to ask what entitles anyone to claim knowledge. B34 says no one has seen, or will know, the clear truth about the gods and about all the things he speaks of; even someone who happened to say exactly what is so would not know it, and only opinion is open to all. Elsewhere he says the gods did not reveal everything to mortals from the start, but by searching people find out better over time (B18). He also practised inquiry: ancient reports say he cited fossil shells and fish found inland and in quarries as evidence that land was once under the sea. The puzzle is how B34 fits with this confident inquiry. One reading makes him a sceptic; others make him a *fallibilist*, someone who holds that we may form well-grounded beliefs but can never rule out error.

### Anaxagoras: no coming-to-be, and everything in everything

Anaxagoras accepted Parmenides' rule that nothing comes from nothing or perishes into nothing. What we call coming-to-be is mixing, and passing-away is separating (B17). In the beginning "all things were together" in an unlimited mixture (B1). Even now, "in everything there is a share of everything" (B11; often translated "a portion of everything"). The ancient reports give the reason through nutrition and growth: food such as bread and milk becomes blood, bone and hair, and "how can hair come from what is not hair, and flesh from what is not flesh?" (B10, as a late commentator reports it). So those stuffs must already be in the food in amounts too small to see. Of the small, he says, there is no smallest but always a smaller (B3), so each piece, however small, still contains a share of every kind. A thing is called gold or flesh because that ingredient predominates in it (B12). The tension is the list of ingredients itself: hot and cold, flesh and hair, seeds, perhaps every kind of stuff. Scholars still debate what exactly is "in everything."

### Anaxagoras: Mind, the vortex, and the stony sun

One thing is not mixed: *Nous*, Mind or intelligence. It is "unlimited and self-ruling," mixed with nothing, the finest and purest of things, holding knowledge about everything (B12). Mind set the original mixture spinning, and the rotation, still spreading outward, separated dense from rare, wet from dry, hot from cold. The heavenly bodies are heavy stony masses held aloft by the force of the whirl, and some may break free and crash to earth. The sun is a red-hot stone, and one ancient report says he judged the moon to be as large as the Peloponnese, the southern Greek peninsula. Here the open questions sharpen. Is Mind a body, only the "finest" one, or the first notion of something non-material? And does it act for a purpose, or only start the machine?

### Socrates' disappointment

Plato's *Phaedo* (97b–99d) gives the most famous answer. Socrates recalls hearing Anaxagoras' book read aloud and being delighted that Mind is the cause of all things, since a Mind would arrange each thing as it is best for it to be. Reading on, he found Mind did almost nothing: the book explained events by air, ether and water instead. That, Socrates says, is like explaining why he sits in prison by his bones and sinews rather than by his judgement that staying is right. Aristotle made a similar complaint, that Anaxagoras brings in Mind only when other causes run out. The *Phaedo* passage is why Anaxagoras is remembered as the thinker who nearly reached explanation by purpose and stopped short.

## Key works

- Xenophanes, poems in elegiac and epic metre and the satirical *silloi* (late 6th – early 5th century BCE) — no complete work survives; about 45 fragments (DK 21). Three late sources mention a poem *On Nature*.
- Anaxagoras, one prose book, later called *On Nature* (date disputed, between c. 470 and c. 440 BCE) — 22 fragments (DK 59) on the original mixture, Mind and the vortex.
- Plato, *Apology* 26d–e and *Phaedo* 96a–99d (4th century BCE) — the stony sun at Socrates' trial, and Socrates' disappointment with Mind.
- Aristotle, *Metaphysics* I.3–5 (4th century BCE) — Anaxagoras as the first to name Mind as cause; Xenophanes as the first to teach that all is one.
- Modern editions: J. H. Lesher, *Xenophanes of Colophon: Fragments* (1992); Patricia Curd, *Anaxagoras of Clazomenae: Fragments and Testimonia* (2007); Daniel W. Graham, *The Texts of Early Greek Philosophy* (2010).

## Contributions

Xenophanes made religion a subject for argument: he gave the first explanation of why people picture gods as they do, and set a standard of moral perfection that later theology inherited. He also opened epistemology, the study of knowledge, by separating knowing from merely believing truly. Anaxagoras brought Ionian natural philosophy to Athens. He was the first to name an intelligence as the cause of cosmic order, the first to treat matter as divisible without limit, and the first Greek with a correct theory of eclipses.

## Impact and legacy

Plato and Aristotle treated Xenophanes as the forerunner of the Eleatic thinkers who held that all is one, and later tradition made him Parmenides' teacher. Plato's censorship of the poets in the *Republic* echoes his attack on Homer and Hesiod. The ancient sceptics claimed him as a founder because of B34. Anaxagoras' Mind set the agenda for Socrates, Plato and Aristotle: the demand that explanation of nature say what things are *for* starts from the disappointment in the *Phaedo*. Aristotle wrote that, beside his predecessors, Anaxagoras seemed like a sober man among random talkers. His trial became an early symbol of science in conflict with religion. Democritus kept the infinite divisibility debate going by rejecting it, and rejected the cosmic Mind as well.

## Criticisms and debates

These are open questions, not settled verdicts. Is Xenophanes' "one god" monotheism, a supreme god among many, or the cosmos itself, as some ancient reports say? Is B34 scepticism, a limit on knowledge of things beyond experience, or a general fallibilism? Was he really a founder of the Eleatic school, or is Plato's remark in the *Sophist* (242d) a playful genealogy? For Anaxagoras: did he write in reply to Parmenides, or does the chronology make that uncertain? What are the basic ingredients "in everything"? Is Mind material? Was Socrates' complaint fair, given that Anaxagoras may have meant Mind to explain order without a goal? The trial is disputed too: its date, the charges, and how far the later stories about it can be trusted.

## Misreadings to avoid

- Xenophanes was not an atheist. He criticised how people pictured the gods while urging respect for them.
- B34 is not "nothing can be known." Its target is certain knowledge of the gods and the whole cosmos; opinion stays open.
- Anaxagoras' "everything in everything" is not the claim that a stone is visibly also bread. Shares are present in tiny amounts; what predominates gives a thing its name.
- *Nous* is not the Christian God. It does not create from nothing; it orders a mixture that already exists.
- Anaxagoras did not literally predict the fall of the Aegospotami stone. His theory said such stones could fall, and one did.

## What to read first

1. Lesher's *SEP* entry "Xenophanes" — short, with the key fragments translated in place.
2. The Xenophanes fragments themselves, in Lesher (1992) or Fairbanks (1898, free online), starting with B11–B16, B23–B26 and B34.
3. Plato, *Phaedo* 96a–99d — Socrates on Anaxagoras, the best way into why Mind mattered.
4. Anaxagoras B1, B11, B12 and B17, in Curd (2007) or Graham (2010) — the whole system in four fragments.
5. Curd and Sisko's *SEP* entry "Anaxagoras" — for the debates over the ingredients, Mind and the trial.

## Study questions

1. Xenophanes argues from the variety of human pictures of the gods to their unreliability. Does that variety show the pictures are false, or only that we cannot tell which is true?
2. How can Xenophanes say no one knows the truth about the gods (B34) and also describe the one god (B23–B26)?
3. Why does Anaxagoras need "a share of everything in everything" to explain how bread becomes hair, given Parmenides' rule that nothing comes from nothing?
4. Is Socrates' complaint in the *Phaedo* fair? What would Anaxagoras gain or lose by making Mind act for the best?
5. Why might calling the sun a red-hot stone have been dangerous in Athens, and what does the trial suggest about how new science met civic religion?
