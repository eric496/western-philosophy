## At a glance

- **Dates:** Porphyry of Tyre (c. 234–c. 305) · Iamblichus of Chalcis (c. 242–c. 325) · Proclus of Athens (412–485) · Hypatia of Alexandria (born c. 350–370; died 415)
- **Place:** Tyre, Athens, Rome and Sicily; Syria (a school probably at Apamea); Constantinople, Alexandria and the Platonic school in Athens; Alexandria
- **School/tradition:** Neoplatonism after Plotinus; they called themselves simply "Platonists"
- **Known for:** carrying [Plotinus](plotinus.md) into the Middle Ages. Porphyry edited the *Enneads* and wrote the logic primer that set the medieval **problem of universals**; Iamblichus defended **theurgy**, ritual as a path to the gods; Proclus turned the system into a geometry-like chain of propositions built on **procession and return**; Hypatia taught mathematics and Platonism in Alexandria until a Christian mob killed her

**Responds to:** [Plato](plato.md), [Aristotle](aristotle.md), [Plotinus](plotinus.md) · **Answered by:** [Augustine](../02-medieval/augustine-of-hippo.md), [Boethius](../02-medieval/boethius.md), [Eriugena](../02-medieval/john-scotus-eriugena.md), [Abelard](../02-medieval/peter-abelard.md), [Aquinas](../02-medieval/thomas-aquinas.md), [Ockham](../02-medieval/william-of-ockham.md), [Nicholas of Cusa](../02-medieval/nicholas-of-cusa.md)

**Why they are grouped here.** These four span two and a half centuries and did not all agree. Iamblichus wrote a whole book against Porphyry, his own teacher. They belong together because each shaped how Plotinus's Platonism reached later readers. Porphyry fixed the text and wrote its most-read introduction to logic. Iamblichus and Proclus rebuilt the system into the form the Athenian school taught until its end in the sixth century. Hypatia shows the same tradition at work in Alexandria, as a public teaching career rather than a set of books. Her part rests on what other people wrote about her, because none of her own writings survive.

## Biography

**Porphyry (c. 234–c. 305)** was born in Tyre, in Phoenicia, with the Semitic name Malchus ("king"). He studied in Athens with the critic Longinus, who rendered the name as Porphyrios, "purple one," after Tyre's famous dye. In 263 he joined [Plotinus](plotinus.md) in Rome and stayed about five years. In his own account, Plotinus saw that he was thinking of suicide and told him to travel; he went to Sicily around 268 and was there when Plotinus died in 270. Plotinus had left him the task of revising his writings. Around 301 Porphyry published them with a *Life of Plotinus* as preface, sorted not by date but by subject into six groups of nine, the *Enneads*. He wrote on nearly everything: logic, grammar, Homer, music, astronomy and religion. His *On Abstinence* argues for vegetarianism; his *Letter to Marcella* is addressed to the widow he married late in life. His fifteen books *Against the Christians* were the most learned pagan attack on the new faith. Christian emperors later ordered them burned, and only fragments quoted by opponents survive.

**Iamblichus (c. 242–c. 325)** came from a leading family of Syria and was born at Chalcis. Eunapius names his teachers as Anatolius and then Porphyry. He later taught his own circle in Syria, probably at Apamea. He wrote a large work *On Pythagoreanism*, of which the *Life of Pythagoras* and an *Exhortation to Philosophy* survive, and commentaries on Plato and Aristotle that are now lost except through later quotation. His surviving masterpiece was framed as a reply. Porphyry had written a skeptical *Letter to Anebo*, an Egyptian priest, asking how prayer, sacrifice and divination could move gods who are beyond all change. Iamblichus answered under the name of a priest, "Abamon." Since [Marsilio Ficino's](../03-early-modern/renaissance-humanists.md) Latin version of 1497 the book has been called *On the Mysteries of the Egyptians*. His students taught the circle around the emperor Julian, who tried to restore paganism in 361–363.

**Proclus (412–485)** was born in Constantinople to a wealthy family from Lycia, in southwest Asia Minor. He studied rhetoric, then Aristotle and mathematics, in Alexandria. At eighteen, in 430–431, he moved to Athens to study at the Platonic school under Plutarch of Athens and then Syrianus. He succeeded Syrianus as head, or *diadochos* ("successor" of Plato), and led the school for nearly fifty years. His student Marinus wrote his life, which presents him as a saint of philosophy: vegetarian, unmarried, tireless at work, a writer of hymns, and devoted to pagan rites in a city that was becoming Christian. He wrote huge commentaries on Plato's *Timaeus*, *Republic*, *Parmenides* and *Alcibiades*, a commentary on Book I of Euclid, the *Platonic Theology*, and the compact *Elements of Theology*. The Athenian school lasted until the emperor Justinian moved against pagan teaching in 529.

**Hypatia (born c. 350–370; died 415)** was the daughter of Theon, an Alexandrian mathematician who edited Euclid and wrote on Ptolemy's astronomy. She worked with him and, as Philostorgius reports, far surpassed her teacher. Nothing she wrote survives. Everything we know comes from others, and the sources have to be weighed one by one.

- **The earliest report.** The church historian Socrates Scholasticus, writing in Constantinople roughly a generation after her death, devotes a chapter to her (*Ecclesiastical History* VII.15). He says she surpassed the philosophers of her time, headed the Platonic school of Plotinus's line, and lectured to students who travelled to hear her. She met magistrates in public without embarrassment and was admired for her dignity and virtue. Then he tells the political story. The imperial prefect Orestes was feuding with Cyril, the bishop of Alexandria. Rumor among the Christian crowd said Hypatia was keeping Orestes from making peace. A group led by a church reader named Peter dragged her from her carriage, took her to the church called the Caesareum, stripped her and killed her with broken tiles or shards. They then burned her body. Socrates dates this to March 415, during Lent, and, himself a Christian, condemns it.
- **A pupil's letters.** Synesius of Cyrene, who studied with her in Alexandria in the 390s and later became bishop of Ptolemais, wrote her seven surviving letters. They show a teacher whose circle of friends lasted for life. In one he asks her to have a hydroscope, an instrument for weighing liquids, cast in brass for him. In one of his last he calls her "mother, sister, teacher," and he sends her his books for judgment.
- **Later reports.** A tenth-century Byzantine encyclopedia, the *Suda*, credits her with commentaries on the arithmetic of Diophantus, an astronomical table and the *Conics* of Apollonius, all lost. A heading in Theon's commentary on Ptolemy says that his daughter Hypatia revised the text. Damascius, a pagan writing around 500, blames Cyril's envy for the murder. The seventh-century Egyptian bishop John of Nikiu calls her a witch who bewitched Orestes. Both were written long after the event and from opposite sides.
- **Modern legend.** In 1720 the deist John Toland made her murder an anti-Catholic parable. Edward Gibbon and Charles Kingsley's novel *Hypatia* (1853) fixed the image of a young, beautiful last pagan martyred by fanatics, and later writers made her the "end of ancient science." None of this is in the ancient sources. She was probably in middle age or older when she died. Socrates presents the killing as a local power struggle, not a war on science. The Great Library of Alexandria no longer existed, so she did not die defending it. The film *Agora* (2009) spread several of these inventions.

## Major theories and concepts

### Porphyry's *Isagoge* and the problem of universals

The *Isagoge* ("Introduction") is a short primer for reading [Aristotle's](aristotle.md) *Categories*, written for a student named Chrysaorius. It explains five basic terms of classification, later called the **predicables**: *genus* (animal), *species* (human), *difference* (rational, what marks a species off within its genus), *property* (a trait every member and only members have, such as the ability to laugh) and *accident* (a trait that can come and go, such as being asleep). The famous **Porphyrian tree** runs from substance down through body, living thing and animal to human and to Socrates.

At the start, Porphyry raises three deeper questions and refuses to answer them. Do genera and species exist in reality, or only in our thoughts? If real, are they bodies or bodiless? And do they exist apart from sensible things, or in them? These are the classic options. Plato is taken to say that kinds are real and separate, Aristotle that they are real but exist in things, and a stricter view that they are only concepts or words. [Boethius](../02-medieval/boethius.md) translated the *Isagoge* into Latin and commented on it twice. Through him the three questions became the **problem of universals**, the central quarrel of medieval logic, answered in rival ways by Roscelin, [Abelard](../02-medieval/peter-abelard.md), [Aquinas](../02-medieval/thomas-aquinas.md) and [Ockham](../02-medieval/william-of-ockham.md). A universal, in this sense, is anything that can be said of many things, as "human" is said of you and me.

The tension is that Porphyry was a Platonist writing a primer for Aristotle's logic. He thought the two could be harmonized: Aristotle's categories describe the sensible world, Plato's Forms the intelligible one. Whether that harmony is real or only polite became a question for every later reader.

### Porphyry against the Christians

Porphyry's attack was learned rather than abusive. He pointed out contradictions between the Gospels and argued, as Jerome reports, that the Book of Daniel was written in the second century BCE, after the events it seems to predict. Modern biblical scholars mostly agree on that date. His deeper point was philosophical: a god beyond change should not take flesh, and the soul's salvation lies in turning from the body, not in its resurrection. [Augustine](../02-medieval/augustine-of-hippo.md) took him as the most serious pagan opponent and argued with him at length in *City of God* Book X.

### Iamblichus and theurgy

**Theurgy** means "god-work": ritual actions, prayers, sacred names and objects through which, Iamblichus held, the gods themselves lift the soul toward them. Plotinus had taught that part of the soul never leaves the intelligible world, so thought alone can return it to its source. Iamblichus denied this. The soul descends completely into the body, so it cannot think its own way back. Something has to come from above. In *On the Mysteries* he argues that union with the gods comes from the rites and their divine "tokens" (*synthēmata*), which work on their own, and not from intellectual effort. If thinking were enough, every philosopher who contemplated the gods would already be united with them.

Porphyry's worry was that this made the gods subject to human magic. Iamblichus replied that the rites do not compel the gods. The gods themselves gave the rites as channels for their own goodness. Iamblichus also made the system more crowded: he multiplied levels and orders of gods between the One and the world, so that each level is reached through the one above it.

### Proclus: procession and return

Proclus's *Elements of Theology* sets out 211 propositions, each with a proof, in the style of Euclid. It opens with the claim that every plurality somehow shares in unity, and builds the whole of reality from that start. Its central law is a three-step pattern. Every effect **remains** in its cause, since it is what the cause is in a lesser form. It **proceeds** from it, since otherwise it would not be distinct. And it **returns** (or "reverts") to it, by desiring and imitating its source. A ray of light is a fair image: it stays connected to the sun, travels out from it, and points back to it. Causes give without being diminished, and every level of being is linked to every other by this outflow and return.

Proclus added the **henads**, divine "unities" just below the One that he identified with the traditional gods. He also held that evil has no cause of its own: it is a *parhypostasis*, a "side-existence" that arises when things fall short of their aims. The tension in the system is plain to most readers: the One is said to be beyond all description, yet it sits at the top of the most detailed map of reality any ancient wrote.

### Hypatia's teaching

Hypatia left no doctrine we can reconstruct. What the evidence shows is a way of teaching: mathematics and astronomy as a training for philosophy, and philosophy as a moral discipline that Christians and pagans could share. Synesius, a Christian, studied with her and went on calling her his guide. Whether her Platonism leaned more toward Porphyry's rational tradition or toward Iamblichus's ritual one is unknown. Most scholars, reading Synesius, think it was the former.

## Key works

- Porphyry, *Life of Plotinus* and edition of the *Enneads* (c. 301) — the only life of Plotinus and the arrangement every reader has used since.
- Porphyry, *Isagoge* (late 3rd century) — the five predicables and the three questions about universals; through Boethius, a medieval schoolbook for a thousand years.
- Porphyry, *Sentences* (*Starting-points Leading to the Intelligibles*), *On Abstinence*, *Letter to Marcella* — his own Platonism, ethics and spiritual advice; *Against the Christians* (15 books) and *Letter to Anebo* survive only in fragments.
- Iamblichus, *On the Mysteries* (the *Reply of Abamon*) — the defense of theurgy, written against Porphyry.
- Iamblichus, *On the Pythagorean Life* and *Protrepticus* — Pythagoras as the model philosopher.
- Proclus, *Elements of Theology* — the whole system in 211 proved propositions; the best short entry.
- Proclus, *Platonic Theology* and commentaries on the *Timaeus*, *Parmenides* and Euclid I.
- On Hypatia: Socrates Scholasticus, *Ecclesiastical History* VII.15, and the letters of Synesius (nos. 10, 15, 16, 33, 81, 124, 154). She has no surviving work.

## Contributions

Porphyry gave Plotinus's writings their lasting form and gave the Latin West its first problem in metaphysics. Iamblichus made ritual and religious practice part of Platonism, and argued that reason alone cannot save the soul. Proclus produced the most systematic metaphysics of antiquity and the law of remaining, procession and return. Hypatia is the first woman mathematician whose life is reasonably well documented, and the first philosopher whose story became a modern symbol in its own right.

## Impact and legacy

Their influence went furthest under other people's names. Around 500 a Christian writer who signed himself [Dionysius the Areopagite](../02-medieval/pseudo-dionysius-and-eckhart.md), St Paul's Athenian convert, built Proclus's hierarchies and negative theology into Christian doctrine. Because readers believed he was Paul's disciple, his books gained near-apostolic authority, and [Eriugena](../02-medieval/john-scotus-eriugena.md) translated them into Latin around 860. A ninth-century Arabic reworking of the *Elements of Theology* entered Latin in the twelfth century as the *Liber de causis* (*Book of Causes*), credited to Aristotle. [Aquinas](../02-medieval/thomas-aquinas.md), after reading William of Moerbeke's 1268 translation of the *Elements*, was the first Latin reader to see that it came from Proclus. Porphyry's questions came through [Boethius](../02-medieval/boethius.md). His attack on Christianity gave [Augustine](../02-medieval/augustine-of-hippo.md) his main opponent, and Augustine's own Platonism owed much to Porphyry's version. Renaissance Platonists, from [Nicholas of Cusa](../02-medieval/nicholas-of-cusa.md) to Marsilio Ficino, read Proclus and Iamblichus directly. Hypatia's afterlife has been political: a figure in eighteenth-century anti-clerical writing, Victorian fiction and twentieth-century feminism.

## Criticisms and debates

- **Decline or development?** Older scholars, following Eduard Zeller and E. R. Dodds, saw Iamblichus and Proclus as a slide from Plotinus's philosophy into superstition. Recent work (Gregory Shaw, Carlos Steel, John Dillon and others) reads theurgy as a reasoned answer to a real problem about the soul. The dispute is live.
- **Was Iamblichus right about the soul?** If the soul is fully descended, how can it still know the Forms? If not, why is ritual needed? Proclus sided with Iamblichus here; the arguments on both sides are still studied.
- **What did Porphyry think about universals?** He declined to say in the *Isagoge*. His commentaries on the *Categories* suggest Aristotle's view for sensible things and Plato's for the intelligible, but scholars disagree on how firmly.
- **Who killed Hypatia, and why?** Socrates points to local politics and rumor. Damascius blames Cyril's envy directly. Christian writers since have defended or accused Cyril. Historians still disagree on how far Cyril was responsible, beyond what Socrates says about the reproach the killing brought on him and his church.

## Misreadings to avoid

- **"The Neoplatonists founded a new school."** They saw themselves as Platonists explaining Plato. "Neoplatonism" is a modern label.
- **"Porphyry solved the problem of universals."** He posed it and explicitly set it aside as too deep for a beginner's book.
- **"Theurgy is just magic."** Iamblichus insists the rites do not force the gods. Whether that distinction holds is a debate, not a settled matter.
- **"The *Liber de causis* is Aristotle's."** It is an Arabic adaptation of Proclus, as Aquinas showed.
- **"Hypatia was killed because she was a scientist."** The earliest source makes it a political murder during a feud between the prefect and the bishop.
- **"Hypatia died when the Library of Alexandria burned."** The Library was long gone by 415. That link is a modern invention.

## In their own words

> "I shall omit to speak about genera and species, as to whether they subsist (in the nature of things) or in mere conceptions only; whether also if subsistent, they are bodies or incorporeal, and whether they are separate from, or in, sensibles, and subsist about these"

— Porphyry, *Isagoge*, ch. I; trans. O. F. Owen, *The Organon* (London: Bohn, 1853), vol. II, pp. 609–610

> "I had fifty-four treatises before me: I divided them into six sets of nine, an arrangement which pleased me by the happy combination of the perfect number six with the nines"

— Porphyry, *Life of Plotinus* §24; trans. Stephen MacKenna, *Plotinus: The Ethical Treatises* (1917), p. 25

> "For a conception of the mind does not conjoin theurgists with the Gods; since, if this were the case, what would hinder those who philosophize theoretically, from having a theurgic union with the Gods?"

— Iamblichus, *On the Mysteries*, Section II, ch. XI; trans. Thomas Taylor (1821; 2nd ed. 1895), p. 109

> "Every effect remains in its cause, proceeds from it, and reverts upon it."

— Proclus, *Elements of Theology*, Prop. 35; trans. E. R. Dodds (1933), as quoted in the SEP entry "Proclus"

> "Having succeeded to the school of Plato and Plotinus, she explained the principles of philosophy to her auditors, many of whom came from a distance to receive her instructions."

— Socrates Scholasticus, *Ecclesiastical History* VII.15, on Hypatia; trans. A. C. Zenos, *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers*, 2nd series, vol. 2 (1890)

> "Yet even she fell a victim to the political jealousy which at that time prevailed."

— Socrates Scholasticus, *Ecclesiastical History* VII.15; trans. Zenos (1890)

> "mother, sister, teacher, and withal benefactress"

— Synesius of Cyrene, Letter 16, to Hypatia; trans. A. Fitzgerald, *The Letters of Synesius of Cyrene* (1926)

## Study questions

1. State Porphyry's three questions in your own words. Which answer would Plato give, and which Aristotle? Which do you find most plausible for a kind like "dog"?
2. Iamblichus argues that if thinking about the gods were enough, every philosopher would already be united with them. Is this a good argument against Plotinus?
3. Explain Proclus's "remaining, procession and return" with an example of your own. What does each step add?
4. Why might a Christian writer around 500 have wanted to present Proclus's ideas under the name of Paul's disciple? What changed when Aquinas discovered the source of the *Liber de causis*?
5. Using only Socrates Scholasticus and Synesius, write a paragraph about Hypatia. Then list three popular claims about her that your paragraph cannot support.

## What to read first

1. Porphyry, *Life of Plotinus* — short, vivid, and the door into the whole tradition.
2. Porphyry, *Isagoge*, ch. I — two pages that set the agenda for medieval logic; read it with Boethius's commentary in mind.
3. Socrates Scholasticus, *Ecclesiastical History* VII.13–15, then Synesius's Letters 10, 15 and 16 — the ancient evidence for Hypatia, before any modern retelling.
4. Iamblichus, *On the Mysteries*, Book II, then Proclus, *Elements of Theology*, Props. 1–39 — the later system and its central law.
