# Zeno of Citium & the Stoics

*Era: Ancient Philosophy (c. 600 BCE – c. 270 CE) · [Era overview](README.md) · [Master index](../../index.md)*

## At a glance
- **Dates:** Zeno c. 334–262 BCE; school c. 300 BCE–2nd century CE
- **Place:** Founded in Athens; later centred in Rome
- **School / tradition:** Stoicism, named for the *Stoa Poikilē* (Painted Porch) where Zeno taught
- **Known for:** A system of logic, physics, and ethics where virtue alone is good, the cosmos is governed by rational *logos*, and happiness is living in agreement with nature.

## Biography

Zeno of Citium, a Phoenician-Greek merchant from Cyprus, came to Athens — ancient biography tells of a shipwreck, a bookshop encounter with Socrates' life, and study under the Cynic Crates, whose self-sufficiency and contempt for convention marked him permanently. He also studied in Plato's Academy and learned logic from Megarian philosophers. Around c. 300 BCE he began teaching in the Painted Porch.

His successor Cleanthes of Assos (c. 331–232 BCE), a former boxer who hauled water at night to pay for study, is remembered for his *Hymn to Zeus*, praising Zeus — Stoic shorthand for the universe's rational order — as ruling all by law.

The third head, Chrysippus of Soli (c. 279–206 BCE), made Stoicism a system. Credited with over 700 treatises, he formalised its logic, defended it against the sceptical Academy, and developed its accounts of fate and emotion. "If there had been no Chrysippus, there would have been no Stoa," the saying went. No complete work by him, Zeno, or Cleanthes (except the *Hymn*) survives; we know them through fragments and later, often hostile, reports.

In the 2nd–1st centuries BCE Panaetius and Posidonius carried a more flexible Middle Stoa to Rome, and Cicero became its great Latin transmitter. The Roman Stoa produced the texts beginners read. Seneca (c. 4 BCE–65 CE), wealthy senator and adviser to Nero, wrote essays and letters on the ethical life from inside a corrupt court, and was forced to take his own life by Nero. Epictetus (c. 50/55–135 CE), born enslaved, freed in Rome, then teacher at Nicopolis in Greece, survives because his student Arrian took notes. Marcus Aurelius (121–180 CE, emperor 161–180) wrote his *Meditations* in Greek, on campaign, as private notes never meant for publication. Courtier, ex-slave, and emperor show Stoicism practised at every social level.

## Major theories and concepts

### The Logos and the three-part system

Stoics divided philosophy into logic (how we know), physics (what exists), and ethics (how to live), insisting each supports the others. Their physics was materialist: only bodies exist and act — including soul and God. The universe is one living, rational whole permeated by *logos* (reason, rational principle), which they identified with God, Nature, and Fate, pictured as a fiery breath (*pneuma*) shaping matter from within, following Heraclitus. Hence the cosmos is providential, not random, and periodically dissolves in fire (*ekpyrōsis*) before repeating exactly.

*Tension:* if everything is fated and material, how are choice and blame possible? That question drives Stoic ethics.

### Virtue the only good; the rest indifferents

Virtue — wisdom, courage, justice, temperance, unified as excellent reason — is the *only* good; vice is the only evil. Health, wealth, reputation, pain, and life itself are "indifferents": they do not by themselves make a life happy or miserable. Some are "preferred" (health over sickness) and normally guide action, but needing them for happiness is a mistake. Example: two people lose a business; the Stoic calls the loss a dispreferred indifferent, and misery follows only from the false judgement that a true good was lost. The virtuous person, even under torture, remains happy, because happiness is rational activity, not comfort.

*Tension:* if health is truly indifferent, why prefer it so reliably? Stoics answer that nature builds in preference for what preserves us, without making it our good.

### The passions (pathē) as false judgements

*Pathē* are not feelings in general but excessive impulses resting on false judgements: fear judges a future indifferent a true evil, grief judges a present one so. For Chrysippus a passion is, or directly follows from, such a judgement, so therapy is cognitive — correct the judgement and the passion dissolves. In their place come *eupatheiai*, "good feelings": joy, rational caution, and wishing, directed at real goods and evils. Involuntary startles or paling — "first movements" — are allowed; assent to the false evaluation is the error.

*Tension:* are emotions really this belief-like and voluntary? Aristotelian critics already doubted it.

### Fate and responsibility

Since *logos* is Fate, every event, including our choices, follows from prior causes. Yet Stoics defended responsibility — an early compatibilism, the view that responsibility is *compatible* with determinism. Chrysippus compared a person to a cylinder: a push (circumstance) starts it, but it rolls as it does because of its own shape (character). An impression pushes us; assent — the mind's "yes" — flows through our character, and is therefore "up to us" in the sense relevant to praise and blame. Epictetus opened the *Enchiridion*: "Some things are up to us, some are not" — judgements and impulses are ours; body, property, and outcomes are not fully so.

*Tension:* if character itself was fated before birth, is assent really mine? Stoics said an act is mine when it flows from my nature, not external compulsion — modern compatibilists still develop this, incompatibilists still resist it.

### Cosmopolitanism

The goal is "living in agreement with nature" — human nature, which is rational, and cosmic nature, rational through and through. Since all humans share *logos*, all are citizens of one cosmic city. Zeno's lost *Republic* imagined a community stripped of local convention; later Stoics developed duties to all humanity regardless of rank or enslavement. In practice they usually accepted existing social roles as the material of duty rather than campaigning to overturn them.

## Key works

- **Early Stoa, fragments only (c. 300–200 BCE).** No complete early Stoic work survives; Zeno's *Republic* is lost. Thought is reconstructed from Diogenes Laertius, Cicero, Plutarch, Sextus Empiricus, and others.
- **Seneca, *Letters to Lucilius* (c. 62–65 CE).** 124 letters coaching a friend on time, death, wealth, and daily practice — Roman Stoicism's most accessible sustained voice.
- **Seneca, *On the Shortness of Life* and *On Anger* (c. 41–64 CE).** Life is long if well used; anger is a temporary madness to prevent, not indulge.
- **Epictetus, *Discourses* and *Enchiridion* (c. 108 CE, recorded by Arrian).** Classroom teaching, and its 53-chapter handbook distillation opening with the dichotomy of control.
- **Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations* (c. 170–180 CE).** Twelve books of private self-exhortation: see events from the whole's perspective, accept mortality, act justly now.

## Contributions

Stoics created antiquity's most integrated system and much of its vocabulary. Chrysippus built a propositional logic — reasoning with whole statements, "if… then…" — so advanced it was only recognised in the 20th century. Their philosophy of language distinguished word, *lekton* (the "sayable," roughly meaning), and object. In ethics, *oikeiōsis* — the natural expansion of concern from self to family, community, and humanity — grounded cosmopolitan duty, and their cognitive theory of emotion was the West's first.

## Impact and legacy

Via Cicero, Stoic natural law entered Roman jurisprudence and later rights theory. Christian writers borrowed Stoic discipline and indifference to fortune, uneasily, since self-sufficiency sits awkwardly with dependence on grace. Spinoza inherited Stoic determinism and therapy of the affects; Kant's ethics of duty has Stoic ancestry. Cognitive behavioural therapy's founders credited Epictetus: we are disturbed not by events but by our judgements about them. Today's popular Stoicism is sometimes faithful, sometimes thinned into productivity advice.

## Criticisms and debates

The sceptical Academy attacked Stoic claims to certain knowledge and providence; Plutarch hunted Stoic contradictions; Aristotelians objected that calling health "indifferent" abuses language. Providence faced the problem of evil: why disease and cruelty in a rationally ordered cosmos? Stoics replied that parts must be judged by the whole and moral evil is the price of rational agency — critics found this evasive. Modern scholars still debate whether passions *are* judgements or merely follow them, whether the sage — admittedly rarer, Stoics said, than the phoenix — is a usable ideal, how radical Zeno's *Republic* was, and whether Seneca's wealth and service to Nero refute or merely embarrass his preaching.

## Misreadings to avoid

- **Not suppression of emotion:** the aim is replacing false-judgement passions with good feelings, not bottling up.
- **"Indifferent" does not mean "don't care":** preferred indifferents guide action; they just do not decide happiness.
- **Not just resilience tips:** historical Stoicism rests on its physics and determinism — a providential, material *logos* — however selectively modern readers borrow it.
- **The sage is a regulative ideal,** like a perfect circle in geometry, not a portrait of actual Stoics.

## What to read first

1. **Epictetus, *Enchiridion*** — the doorway; read a few chapters at a time.
2. **Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations* II–V** — Stoicism as private practice, in a modern translation.
3. **Seneca, *On the Shortness of Life* and selected *Letters*** (1, 5, 7, 47, 63).
4. **John Sellars, *Stoicism*** — a clear guide to the whole system.
