## At a glance

- **Dates:** Luce Irigaray (b. 1930) · Julia Kristeva (b. 1941) · Judith Butler (b. 1956) — all three living as of November 2026
- **Place:** Belgium, Paris and Italy; Bulgaria and Paris; the United States (Yale, Johns Hopkins, Berkeley)
- **School/tradition:** psychoanalytic and poststructuralist feminism; "French feminism" (an American label for Irigaray, Kristeva and Hélène Cixous); queer theory
- **Known for:** Irigaray's philosophy of **sexual difference** and her reading of Western philosophy as a culture of one sex; Kristeva's **semiotic** dimension of language and her theory of **abjection**; Butler's account of gender as **performative** and the critique of the distinction between sex and gender

**Responds to:** [Beauvoir](simone-de-beauvoir.md), [Plato](../01-ancient/plato.md), [Hegel](../04-19th-century/g-w-f-hegel.md), [Nietzsche](../04-19th-century/friedrich-nietzsche.md), [Merleau-Ponty](maurice-merleau-ponty.md), [Foucault](michel-foucault.md), [Derrida](jacques-derrida.md), [Austin](austin-and-strawson.md) · **Answered by:** [Nussbaum](martha-nussbaum.md); Seyla Benhabib, Nancy Fraser, Toril Moi and trans theorists such as Jay Prosser — outside this repo's entries

**Why they are grouped here.** The three never formed a school. Irigaray and Kristeva were rivals more than allies, and both kept their distance from the label "feminist"; Butler is an American philosopher who read them, and argued with both, in *Gender Trouble*. They belong together because they ask the question that comes after [Beauvoir](simone-de-beauvoir.md). Beauvoir showed that "woman" is made, not given. Her successors asked what that making is made *of*. For Irigaray and Kristeva the answer runs through language and the unconscious, as Freud and the psychoanalyst Jacques Lacan described them. For Butler it runs through repeated acts and the norms that govern them. Their quarrel, over whether feminism should defend women's difference or dissolve the category "women", is still the central debate of feminist theory.

## Biography

**Luce Irigaray (b. 1930)** was born on 3 May 1930 in Blaton, Belgium, according to most references; Britannica gives "1932?". She dislikes biographical questions, holding that women thinkers are too often judged by their lives rather than their ideas, so accounts of her life are thin and sometimes conflict. She studied in Louvain, then moved to Paris, where she took doctorates in linguistics and in philosophy and joined the National Centre for Scientific Research (CNRS) in 1964. She trained as a psychoanalyst in Lacan's École freudienne de Paris in the 1960s. Her second doctoral thesis, *Speculum of the Other Woman* (1974), attacked the male bias of Freud and of Lacan himself. Its publication cost her her teaching post at the University of Paris VIII at Vincennes, and the Lacanian circle shut her out. She went on writing for five decades and became closely involved with the Italian women's movement.

**Julia Kristeva (b. 1941)** was born on 24 June 1941 in Sliven, Bulgaria, and studied literature in Sofia. In 1965 a French government fellowship took her to Paris. There she worked with the literary theorist Roland Barthes, joined the avant-garde review *Tel Quel*, and married its editor, the novelist Philippe Sollers. Her thesis, *Revolution in Poetic Language* (1974), made her name. She then trained as a psychoanalyst and practised for decades alongside teaching at the University of Paris VII. In 2004 she received the first Holberg Prize. One episode is disputed. In 2018 a Bulgarian state commission announced that communist-era secret-police files listed her as an informant under a code name. Kristeva called the claim false. What the files show, and whether they record real collaboration, remains contested.

**Judith Butler (b. 1956)** was born on 24 February 1956 in Cleveland, Ohio, and took a Yale doctorate on how French philosophers read [Hegel](../04-19th-century/g-w-f-hegel.md), published as *Subjects of Desire* (1987). An essay of 1988, "Performative Acts and Gender Constitution", prepared *Gender Trouble* (1990), which became one of the most cited books in the humanities and a founding text of **queer theory** (the study of how norms of sexuality and gender are produced and resisted). Butler has taught at Berkeley since 1993 and in 2012 received Frankfurt's Adorno Prize. Butler uses both *she* and *they*; this entry mostly uses the name. Later work turned to hate speech, grief, war and non-violence, and to the global campaigns against "gender ideology" in *Who's Afraid of Gender?* (2024).

## Major theories and concepts

### Irigaray: one sex, and its other

Irigaray's starting point is a reading of Freud. In his lecture "Femininity" Freud describes the girl as a boy who discovers she lacks something. Irigaray argues that this is not an observation but a decision: the female is measured on a male model and can only appear as less. She then claims that philosophy did the same long before psychoanalysis. *Speculum* moves, broadly, backwards through history, from Freud to Plato. In its last part, "Plato's Hystera" (*hystera* is Greek for womb), she reads the [cave](../01-ancient/plato.md) as a womb. The allegory, on her reading, turns this maternal origin into a world of mere shadows that the philosopher must leave behind for the sun of the Good, a father figure on her reading. Western thought, she concludes, has had only **one sex**: the male subject, with the woman as his mirror, his matter, his "other". In that sense, she says, real sexual difference does not yet exist.

Her method is **mimesis** (or mimicry). She repeats the philosophers' images of woman, too faithfully and with a twist, so that their assumptions show. *This Sex Which Is Not One* (1977) uses the method for the title essay's best-known image. Female pleasure, unlike the male model of one organ, is plural and self-touching, figured by "two lips" in contact; woman is a sex that is "not one". From the 1980s she turns to an ethics: two sexes, each a full subject, related by **wonder** rather than possession, with distinct civil rights for each. The tension is the one critics press. Is she describing bodies, and so returning to biology? Or is she rewriting the images through which bodies are read?

### Kristeva: the semiotic and the symbolic

Kristeva's theory of language has two sides. The **symbolic** (a term from Lacan) is language as grammar, logic and reference: what a dictionary and a syntax can capture, tied to social law. The **semiotic** is the rhythm, tone and sound pattern of speech, charged with bodily drives, which comes before the infant's grasp of words and is bound up with the mother's body. She locates it in a **chora**, a word borrowed from Plato's *Timaeus*, where it names a shapeless "receptacle". Every utterance mixes the two. A lullaby is mostly semiotic, a legal contract mostly symbolic. Avant-garde poets such as Mallarmé let the semiotic break through syntax, and for Kristeva that disturbance has political force. Her claim is not that women speak semiotically and men symbolically: both capacities belong to every speaker.

### Kristeva: abjection

In *Powers of Horror* (1980) Kristeva asks why certain things, such as vomit, filth, an open wound or a corpse, provoke a revulsion stronger than dislike. Her answer is **abjection**: the violent rejection of what threatens the boundary between self and not-self. The abject is neither a subject nor an object. It is what I must expel in order to become an "I" at all, and the first thing so expelled, she argues, is the infant's fusion with the mother's body. Religions mark the abject with rules of purity; literature (her main case is the novelist Céline) and horror revisit it. The concept has travelled widely, into film studies and into accounts of racism and misogyny as the abjection of other groups.

### Kristeva: "Women's Time"

In the essay "Women's Time" (1979) Kristeva distinguishes three attitudes in feminism, which she sometimes calls generations but says can coexist. The first, the struggle for votes and equal rights, demands a place in history's linear time. The second, after 1968, insists on women's difference and distrusts that time altogether. The third, which she hopes for, treats the very opposition of man and woman as a metaphysical construction and moves the struggle inside each person's identity. The essay is often read as Kristeva's own place in the equality-versus-difference debate.

### Butler: gender as performative

Butler's target is the standard feminist distinction between **sex** (biological) and **gender** (its cultural meaning). If gender is cultural, Butler asks, what guarantees that sex is not? The famous answer is that sex may be "as culturally constructed as gender", so that the distinction "turns out to be no distinction at all" (*Gender Trouble*, ch. 1). We never meet a sexed body outside the descriptions, norms and expectations that make it intelligible.

Gender, on this view, is **performative**. The term comes from [J. L. Austin](austin-and-strawson.md), who noticed that some sentences do what they say: "I promise" makes a promise. A performative gender is one constituted by repeated acts, ways of walking, dressing, speaking and desiring, rather than expressed by an inner core. Just as [Nietzsche](../04-19th-century/friedrich-nietzsche.md) held that there is no doer behind the deed, Butler holds that there is no gender identity behind its expressions. The acts repeat norms that came before us. From [Foucault](michel-foucault.md) Butler takes the idea that power produces the subjects it seems only to regulate; the norm Butler names the **heterosexual matrix**, which expects sex, gender and desire to line up. From [Derrida](jacques-derrida.md) comes **citationality**: any sign works only by being repeatable in new contexts, and so can misfire. That gives room for **subversion**. Drag, Butler's best-known example, repeats gender with a difference and shows it to be an imitation without an original.

### Butler: *Bodies That Matter*

Readers took *Gender Trouble* to mean that gender is a costume one chooses each morning. *Bodies That Matter* (1993) corrects this. Performativity is not a theatrical performance by a sovereign chooser; it is the forced, repeated citing of norms, which forms the self that might later resist them. Nor does the book deny that bodies exist. It asks how some bodies come to "matter", in both senses: to count as real and valuable, while others are excluded.

## Key works

- Irigaray, *Speculum of the Other Woman* (1974; Eng. 1985) — Freud's theory of femininity, then the history of philosophy read backwards to Plato's cave.
- Irigaray, *This Sex Which Is Not One* (1977; Eng. 1985) — essays and interviews; the "two lips", mimicry, women as objects of exchange.
- Irigaray, *An Ethics of Sexual Difference* (1984; Eng. 1993) — lectures on Plato, Aristotle, Descartes, Spinoza, Hegel, Merleau-Ponty and [Levinas](emmanuel-levinas.md); the ethics of wonder.
- Kristeva, *Revolution in Poetic Language* (1974; abridged Eng. 1984) — the semiotic, the symbolic and the chora.
- Kristeva, "Women's Time" (1979; Eng. 1981) — the three attitudes in feminism.
- Kristeva, *Powers of Horror: An Essay on Abjection* (1980; Eng. 1982) — the abject, purity rules, Céline.
- Butler, *Gender Trouble* (1990; 2nd ed. 1999 with a new preface) — the critique of "women" as feminism's subject and of the sex/gender distinction; performativity.
- Butler, *Bodies That Matter* (1993) — performativity as citation; the materiality of bodies.

## Contributions

Irigaray turned the history of philosophy itself into a feminist question: not only what philosophers said about women, but how the shape of their concepts depends on women's exclusion. Kristeva gave theories of meaning a body, with rhythm, drive and revulsion, and supplied the concept of abjection. Butler showed how the sex/gender distinction could be questioned, built a theory of identity from speech-act theory, Foucault and Derrida, and helped found queer theory.

## Impact and legacy

Irigaray shaped feminist history of philosophy and the feminist reading of Plato, and her ethics of difference influenced Italian feminism and later feminist "new materialism". Kristeva is central to psychoanalytic literary criticism, film theory of horror, and work on exile and national identity. Butler is among the most widely read living philosophers outside philosophy: in law, literary studies, sociology, psychology and activism, and in trans and queer politics. The three are also the subject of the "French feminism" debate in America, where readers argued over how far theory written in Paris spoke to women's material lives.

## Criticisms and debates

- **Difference or equality?** Equality feminists, in Beauvoir's line, want women admitted to the full human subject. Irigaray replies that this subject is male, so equality means becoming like men; she wants two sexes, each with its own standing. Her critics answer that stressing difference risks restoring the old stereotypes.
- **Is Irigaray an essentialist?** Toril Moi and others read the "two lips" as biological essentialism. Naomi Schor, Diana Fuss and Margaret Whitford defend it as strategic mimicry. Trans philosophers have more recently objected that her two-sex ontology leaves no room for trans lives.
- **Kristeva and the maternal.** In *Gender Trouble* Butler argues that Kristeva's semiotic, presented as prior to culture, is itself a cultural construction, so her theory quietly keeps the maternal body outside history. Nancy Fraser doubted that Kristeva's theory leaves room for collective political agency.
- **Does performativity leave a subject who can act?** Seyla Benhabib's exchange with Butler in *Feminist Contentions* (1995) asked who does the subverting if the self is an effect of norms. Butler answers that agency is a capacity to repeat norms differently, not freedom from them.
- **Nussbaum's charge.** In "The Professor of Parody" (1999), [Nussbaum](martha-nussbaum.md) attacked Butler's obscure style and argued that ironic subversion replaces the legal and economic struggles real women need. That April the magazine printed an exchange of letters answering Nussbaum. The journal *Philosophy and Literature* had just given Butler first prize in its 1998 "Bad Writing Contest", and in March 1999 Butler answered that charge in a *New York Times* opinion piece, "A 'Bad Writer' Bites Back". Defenders reply that Butler's work was never meant to replace law and policy, and that Nussbaum sets up a false choice between them and the critique of norms.
- **Bodies.** Some critics, including Toril Moi and Jay Prosser, argue that Butler underrates the lived body, which Beauvoir and [Merleau-Ponty](maurice-merleau-ponty.md) put at the centre.

## Misreadings to avoid

- **"Performative means it is a performance you choose."** Butler rejects voluntarism; the norms come first and are imposed.
- **"Butler denies that bodies exist."** The claim is that our access to bodies is always shaped by norms, not that there is nothing there.
- **"For Kristeva, women speak semiotically."** Every speaker uses both registers.
- **"Irigaray wants to return women to nature."** Whether her images describe biology or rework its imagery is precisely the debate.
- **"French feminism was a movement."** It is mainly an American reading-list label; the three disagree sharply.

## In their own words

> "Sexual difference is one of the major philosophical issues, if not the issue, of our age."

— Irigaray, *An Ethics of Sexual Difference* (1984), ch. 1 "Sexual Difference", trans. Carolyn Burke and Gillian C. Gill (Cornell, 1993), p. 5

> "There looms, within abjection, one of those violent, dark revolts of being, directed against a threat that seems to emanate from an exorbitant outside or inside, ejected beyond the scope of the possible, the tolerable, the thinkable. It lies there, quite close, but it cannot be assimilated."

— Kristeva, *Powers of Horror* (1980), ch. 1 "Approaching Abjection", trans. Leon S. Roudiez (Columbia, 1982), p. 1

> "perhaps this construct called 'sex' is as culturally constructed as gender; indeed, perhaps it was always already gender, with the consequence that the distinction between sex and gender turns out to be no distinction at all."

— Butler, *Gender Trouble*, ch. 1, §ii (2nd ed., Routledge, 1999), pp. 10–11

> "a stable identity or locus of agency from which various acts follow"

— Butler, *Gender Trouble*, ch. 3, §iv (1999 ed.), p. 179, describing what gender is *not*

> "Judith Butler's hip quietism is a comprehensible response to the difficulty of realizing justice in America. But it is a bad response. It collaborates with evil."

— Nussbaum, "The Professor of Parody", *The New Republic*, 22 February 1999, §VI

## Study questions

1. Irigaray reads Plato's cave as a womb. What does this reading reveal, and what might a defender of Plato reply?
2. Can a feminist both demand equality with men and insist that women are different? How do Beauvoir, Irigaray and Kristeva's "Women's Time" each answer?
3. Pick an experience of disgust. Does Kristeva's account of abjection explain it better than a simple account of danger or hygiene?
4. Butler borrows "performative" from Austin. What changes when the idea moves from single sentences such as "I promise" to a whole gender?
5. If identities are effects of repeated norms, who resists the norms? Is Butler's answer to Benhabib convincing?
6. Is Nussbaum right that Butler's politics is quietist, or does she set up a false choice between law and culture?

## What to read first

1. Butler, *Gender Trouble*, Preface (1999) and ch. 1 — the clearest statement of the critique of sex/gender.
2. Irigaray, "This Sex Which Is Not One", in the book of that name — short, and the best way into her method.
3. Kristeva, "Women's Time" — a compact map of the equality/difference debate.
4. Kristeva, *Powers of Horror*, ch. 1; then Nussbaum's "The Professor of Parody" beside Butler, *Bodies That Matter*, Preface, to see the argument about performativity from both sides.
