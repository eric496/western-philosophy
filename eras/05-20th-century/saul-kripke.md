## At a glance

- **Dates:** 1940–2022
- **Place:** Born in Bay Shore, Long Island, New York; grew up in Omaha, Nebraska; career at Harvard, Rockefeller University, Princeton, and the City University of New York
- **School/tradition:** Analytic philosophy; logic, philosophy of language, metaphysics
- **Known for:** The possible-worlds ("Kripke") semantics for modal logic; *Naming and Necessity* (1980) — rigid designation, the causal theory of reference, and necessary truths known only a posteriori; the "Kripkenstein" skeptical paradox about rule-following and meaning

**Responds to:** [Frege](gottlob-frege.md), [Russell](bertrand-russell.md), [Wittgenstein (late)](wittgenstein-late.md), [Quine](w-v-o-quine.md) · **Answered by:** later philosophers of language, mind, and metaphysics who work in the framework he reset — no single later entry in this repo

## Biography

Saul Aaron Kripke was born in Bay Shore, on Long Island, New York, on November 13, 1940, and grew up in Omaha, Nebraska, where his father led a synagogue. He was a prodigy of a rare and verifiable kind. He taught himself modal logic as a teenager, and the paper that founded a whole technique — "A Completeness Theorem in Modal Logic," in *The Journal of Symbolic Logic* — was published in 1959, when he was eighteen and had begun the work while still a high-school student in Omaha. The result it proved, a completeness theorem for the modal system known as S5 extended with quantifiers and identity, was the seed of what everyone now calls Kripke semantics.

Kripke went to Harvard and graduated in 1962 with a B.S. in mathematics — which, the biographers like to note, was the only nonhonorary degree he ever held. He stayed on at Harvard, first as a member of the Harvard Society of Fellows and then as a lecturer, until 1968. He then taught at Rockefeller University from 1968 to 1976, and then moved to Princeton (sources give 1976 to 1978 for the move), where he was McCosh Professor of Philosophy until his retirement in 1998. Along the way he gave the John Locke Lectures at Oxford in 1973 (published decades later, in 2013, as *Reference and Existence*). In 2001 he received the Rolf Schock Prize in Logic and Philosophy from the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences, one of the field's highest honors, and in 2003 he joined the Graduate Center of the City University of New York as a distinguished professor, where the Saul Kripke Center was established around his work and papers. He died in Plainsboro, New Jersey, on September 15, 2022.

His way of working was unusual. Kripke published comparatively little, and much of his most influential material reached print as transcribed lectures rather than as books written for the page. *Naming and Necessity* began as three lectures delivered at Princeton in January 1970, spoken largely without notes; the transcript appeared in 1972 and the free-standing book, with a new preface, in 1980. Further work circulated for years in manuscript or on tape before publication, and some remains unpublished. The effect was paradoxical: a thinker who shaped the agenda of analytic philosophy for half a century did so through a small, intensely studied body of work.

## Major theories and concepts

### Possible-worlds semantics for modal logic

Modal logic is the logic of *necessity* and *possibility* — of what must be so and what might have been otherwise. Before Kripke, logicians could write modal axioms but lacked a clear, general account of what made them true. Kripke supplied one. Picture a set of **possible worlds** — complete ways things might have been — with an **accessibility relation** saying which worlds count as possible relative to which. A sentence is *necessarily* true at a world when it is true at every world accessible from it, and *possibly* true when it is true at some accessible world.

The elegant payoff is that different modal systems correspond to different structural properties of the accessibility relation. If every world can "see" itself (reflexivity), you validate the principle that whatever is necessary is actual; make the relation an equivalence relation and you get S5, the system of his 1959 paper. This framework — now standard under the name **Kripke semantics** — turned modal logic from a collection of competing axiom lists into a subject with a model theory, and it spread far beyond philosophy, into the semantics of computer programs, the logics of knowledge and belief, and the analysis of time.

### Rigid designation

In *Naming and Necessity* Kripke carried possible worlds into the theory of names. A **rigid designator** is an expression that designates the same object in every possible world in which that object exists, and never designates anything else. His claim is that ordinary proper names are rigid. "Richard Nixon" picks out Nixon in every world in which Nixon exists — including worlds in which Nixon lost the 1968 election and sold insurance instead.

Contrast a definite description. "The person who won the 1968 United States presidential election" happens to pick out Nixon in the actual world, but in other possible worlds it picks out Hubert Humphrey, or someone else entirely; it is **non-rigid**. The distinction looks small and turns out to be enormous, because it lets Kripke keep two questions apart that earlier theories had run together: the question of how a term *refers*, and the question of which of its truths are *necessary*. A name and a description can refer to the same thing in the actual world while behaving completely differently across the others.

### The causal-historical picture of reference

If names are not disguised descriptions, what fixes what a name refers to? Kripke sketches — he is careful to call it a picture, not a finished theory — a **causal-historical** account. A name is introduced for an object in an initial event, a kind of "baptism," which may point at the object directly or fix it by a description used only to get started. The name is then passed from speaker to speaker. Each new user intends to use it with the same reference as the person they picked it up from, so reference travels down a **chain of communication** linking present uses back to the original object.

The payoff is that a speaker can refer while knowing almost nothing true about the referent, and even while believing something false. Most people who talk about Richard Feynman cannot state a description that singles him out — "a famous physicist" fits many — yet they refer to Feynman, because their use is the near end of a chain reaching back to him. Reference is a matter of causal history among speakers, not of a description carried in each speaker's head.

### A posteriori necessities (and the contingent a priori)

This is the result that overturned a long-standing assumption traced to Kant: that the necessary truths are exactly the truths knowable a priori, independently of experience. Kripke pried the two distinctions apart. *Necessary* versus *contingent* is a **metaphysical** distinction, about how things must be or might have been. *A priori* versus *a posteriori* is an **epistemological** distinction, about how a truth can be known. There is no reason, he argued, to assume the two line up.

His signature cases are the **necessary a posteriori**. "Hesperus is Phosphorus" — the evening star is the morning star — was a genuine astronomical discovery, known only a posteriori. But "Hesperus" and "Phosphorus" are both rigid designators for the planet Venus, so the statement says of Venus that it is identical to itself; given that it is true, it is true in every possible world, hence necessary. The same holds for "Cicero is Tully" and, extending the idea to natural kinds, "Water is H₂O": discovered empirically, yet necessarily true once discovered. Kripke also allowed the mirror case, the **contingent a priori**: someone who fixes the reference of "one meter" by stipulating that it is the length of a particular bar can know a priori that the bar is one meter long, even though the bar might have been longer or shorter, so the truth is contingent.

### The anti-descriptivist arguments against Frege and Russell

Kripke's target was **descriptivism**, the dominant theory of names he attributed to [Frege](gottlob-frege.md), [Russell](bertrand-russell.md), and, in a cluster version, to the later [Wittgenstein](wittgenstein-late.md) and John Searle. On Frege's view a name carries a *sense* that presents its bearer; on Russell's, an ordinary name is shorthand for a description or bundle of descriptions. Either way, the name refers to whatever uniquely fits the associated description. Kripke pressed three kinds of objection.

The **modal** argument: if "Aristotle" just meant "the pupil of Plato who taught Alexander," then "Aristotle taught Alexander" would be necessary — but it is not. Aristotle could have died at age two and done none of the things we associate with him, and he would still have been Aristotle; so the name cannot be synonymous with those descriptions. The **epistemic** argument: if the name meant the description, "Aristotle, if he existed, had these properties" would be knowable a priori, which it is not — it is an empirical fact that he had them. The **semantic** argument: the description a speaker associates with a name can be false of the real bearer, or true of someone else, and reference still goes to the bearer. Kripke's much-used case is the logician Kurt Gödel: even if it turned out that a man named Schmidt really proved the incompleteness theorem and Gödel stole it, "Gödel" would still refer to Gödel — the man — and not to "whoever proved incompleteness." Together these arguments are why most philosophers of language now treat names as rigid and reference as something other than description-fitting.

### The skeptical paradox about rules and meaning ("Kripkenstein")

In *Wittgenstein on Rules and Private Language* (1982) Kripke drew a startling argument out of a few sections of [Wittgenstein's](wittgenstein-late.md) *Philosophical Investigations*, above all §201. Because the reading is as much Kripke's as Wittgenstein's — Kripke says he presents the argument "as it struck" him — commentators dubbed the resulting figure **"Kripke's Wittgenstein," or "Kripkenstein."**

The **skeptical paradox** runs like this. You have added numbers all your life, but only finitely many times. Suppose you have never added numbers as large as 68 and 57. A skeptic now asks: when you answer "125," are you following the rule for *plus*, or for a different function — call it **quus** — that agrees with plus on all the cases you have met but diverges on new ones, yielding, say, 5 for "68 quus 57"? The challenge is to point to any **fact about you** — past behavior, mental images, a felt rule, a disposition — that settles that you meant plus rather than quus. Past behavior is finite and fits both. A disposition cannot do it either: dispositions are finite too, and they describe what you *would* do, not what you *ought* to do — and meaning is **normative**, saying the correct answer is 125 whatever you are inclined to blurt out. The unsettling conclusion, if meaning had to be constituted by such a fact, is that "there can be no such thing as meaning anything by any word."

Kripke pairs the paradox with a **skeptical solution**. Rather than finding the missing fact, it changes the question. We give up asking what fact *constitutes* meaning plus, and ask instead under what conditions we are entitled to *say* someone means plus. The answer is communal: within a shared practice, we count a person as meaning plus when their use agrees, in enough cases, with the way the rest of us go on. Meaning-talk is licensed by **assertibility conditions** grounded in agreement across a community, not by truth-conditions that report private facts. This is why, on Kripke's reading, there can be **no private language**: a rule followed by one person entirely in isolation would have nothing to distinguish getting it right from merely seeming to.

## Key works

- "A Completeness Theorem in Modal Logic" (1959) — written as a teenager; the completeness proof for S5 that launched possible-worlds semantics.
- "Identity and Necessity" (1971) — an early, compact statement of the necessity of identity and of necessary a posteriori truths, given after the January 1970 lectures but in print a year before their 1972 transcript.
- *Naming and Necessity* (lectures 1970; transcript 1972; book 1980) — the masterwork: rigid designation, the causal picture of reference, the necessary a posteriori, and the case against descriptivism.
- "Outline of a Theory of Truth" (1975) — an influential formal theory of truth that handles the Liar paradox by building truth up in stages to a "fixed point," allowing some sentences to go truth-valueless.
- *Wittgenstein on Rules and Private Language: An Elementary Exposition* (1982) — the skeptical paradox about rule-following and its communal "skeptical solution."
- *Reference and Existence* (2013) — the 1973 John Locke Lectures, on fiction, empty names, and reference.

## Contributions

Kripke gave modal logic its working model theory, turning talk of necessity and possibility into a precise and portable technique used across logic, linguistics, and computer science. He supplied the theory of language with tools now simply part of the furniture: rigid designation, the causal-historical chain of reference, and the clean separation of the metaphysical (necessary/contingent) from the epistemic (a priori/a posteriori). By exhibiting necessary a posteriori and contingent a priori truths he reopened serious metaphysics — essences, natural kinds, and de re necessity — inside a tradition that logical positivism had taught to distrust it. In the philosophy of mind, his argument that a mind-brain identity would have to be necessary a posteriori, yet fails that test because pain seems conceivable without its physical correlate, set a problem physicalists are still answering. And his reading of Wittgenstein put the normativity of meaning at the center of philosophy of language.

## Impact and legacy

*Naming and Necessity* is routinely ranked among the most important philosophical works of the twentieth century: it is difficult to find a later discussion of names, reference, modality, or essence that does not position itself relative to Kripke. The "new theory of reference" he launched, alongside parallel work by Ruth Barcan Marcus, Keith Donnellan, and Hilary Putnam, displaced the descriptivism of [Frege](gottlob-frege.md) and [Russell](bertrand-russell.md) as the default view; Putnam's "Twin Earth" argument that meanings "ain't in the head" is a close cousin of it. In metaphysics the revival of essentialism and of modal realism belongs to the opening Kripke made, even where conclusions diverge sharply. "Kripkenstein" generated its own literature on meaning skepticism, and his fixed-point theory of truth reshaped formal work on the Liar. Few twentieth-century philosophers set so many research programs in motion.

## Criticisms and debates

- **Did he refute descriptivism, or only naive descriptivism?** Sophisticated descriptivists ([David Lewis](analytic-philosophy-after-quine.md), Frank Jackson, and others) replied with *rigidified* or *causal* descriptions — "the actual teacher of Alexander," or "the person at the origin of this name's use" — designed to survive the modal and semantic arguments. Whether these rescue descriptivism or quietly concede Kripke's point is a live dispute.
- **Is the causal picture a theory?** Kripke only sketched it, and critics press the "qua problem": a baptism points at an object that is also a lump of matter, a time-slice, and a member of a kind, so what fixes *which* of these the name tracks? Reference-change over time is another strain the bare chain does not obviously explain.
- **How secure are the necessary a posteriori cases?** Two-dimensional semantics (Chalmers, Jackson) accepts the examples but reinterprets them, arguing that a hidden a priori component is doing the work — which would blunt the anti-Kantian moral. Others question whether "water is H₂O" is necessary rather than a convenient stipulation.
- **Priority.** Barcan Marcus's earlier results on the necessity of identity and on names as "tags" have led some to argue that parts of the picture predate Kripke; defenders reply that the systematic synthesis, and the semantics, are his.
- **Is "Kripkenstein" Wittgenstein?** Many Wittgenstein scholars (notably Gordon Baker and Peter Hacker, and John McDowell) argue that Kripke's skeptic imports exactly the craving for a super-fact that Wittgenstein meant to dissolve, so that the "skeptical solution" misreads the *Investigations*. The debate over whether the paradox is Wittgenstein's or Kripke's own remains unsettled — which is why the hybrid name stuck.

## Misreadings to avoid

- Possible worlds are not far-off planets Kripke claims to discover. They are ways things might have been, a device for talking about necessity — "stipulated, not discovered by powerful telescopes," as he put it.
- Rigid designation does not mean a name is attached to its bearer by magic or in all circumstances of use; it means that, holding the name's actual reference fixed, we evaluate claims about other possible situations by asking about *that same object*.
- "Necessary a posteriori" does not mean a truth is known without experience after all. It means a truth that experience is needed to discover is nonetheless one that could not have been otherwise.
- Kripke's Wittgenstein is not committed to the view that meaning is nothing or that "anything goes." The skeptical solution is meant to vindicate ordinary meaning-talk by relocating its ground in communal practice.

## What to read first

1. *Naming and Necessity* — start with Lecture I and the first half of Lecture II. It is a transcript of spoken lectures, so it reads far more easily than its reputation suggests, and the core ideas are all there.
2. "Identity and Necessity" (1971) — a shorter route into the necessity of identity and the a posteriori necessary, if the book feels like a lot at once.
3. *Wittgenstein on Rules and Private Language*, Chapters 2–3 — the skeptical paradox and the skeptical solution, written with unusual clarity; read it after you have met the late Wittgenstein.
