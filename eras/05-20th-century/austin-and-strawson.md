## At a glance

- **Dates:** J. L. Austin, 1911–1960 · P. F. (Peter Frederick) Strawson, 1919–2006
- **Place:** Austin was born in Lancaster, England; Strawson in London. Both spent most of their careers at Oxford.
- **School/tradition:** Analytic philosophy; postwar Oxford "ordinary language" philosophy
- **Known for:** Austin: speech act theory (*How to Do Things with Words*) and the attack on sense-data theory. Strawson: the critique of Russell's theory of descriptions ("On Referring"), "descriptive metaphysics" (*Individuals*), and the reactive attitudes ("Freedom and Resentment").

**Responds to:** [Gilbert Ryle](gilbert-ryle.md), [Wittgenstein (late)](wittgenstein-late.md), [Bertrand Russell](bertrand-russell.md), [G. E. Moore](g-e-moore.md), [Immanuel Kant](../03-early-modern/immanuel-kant.md) (Strawson) · **Answered by:** [W. V. O. Quine](w-v-o-quine.md) and, on speech acts and reference, later philosophers of language with no entry in this repo (Grice, Searle, Kripke)

## Why these two are grouped

After [Gilbert Ryle](gilbert-ryle.md), Austin and Strawson did most to define and then move beyond "ordinary language philosophy": looking carefully at how we actually talk in order to clear up, or reframe, traditional problems. Where [Frege](gottlob-frege.md), [Russell](bertrand-russell.md) and the early Wittgenstein wanted a perfect artificial language, the ordinary-language philosophers, with the [late Wittgenstein](wittgenstein-late.md), treated natural language as an autonomous object of study whose apparent imperfections signal expressive richness. Ryle's entry covers the mind; this one covers speech and truth (Austin) and reference, the structure of thought and responsibility (Strawson). Scholars warn that the label is loose: it is unclear what beyond attention to everyday speech it commits anyone to.

There is also a historical reason. In the early 1950s Strawson, the younger man, publicly challenged Austin, the hitherto acknowledged leader of Oxford philosophy, over truth, and then spent decades steering Oxford toward metaphysics, Kant and the self. They shared a method and disagreed about where it should lead.

## Biography

### J. L. Austin

John Langshaw Austin was born in Lancaster on March 26, 1911. He studied classics at Balliol College, Oxford, from 1929, took a First in Literae Humaniores (classics and philosophy) in 1933, and was elected a fellow of All Souls that year; in 1935 he became a fellow and tutor at Magdalen. Aristotle, Kant, Leibniz and Plato were early interests, and the Oxford realists John Cook Wilson and H. A. Prichard, with G. E. Moore, plausibly shaped his habit of trusting specific, careful judgments over sweeping general ones.

In the Second World War Austin served in the Intelligence Corps and left the army in 1945 as a lieutenant colonel, honored with an OBE, the French Croix de Guerre and the American Legion of Merit. After the war he returned to Oxford and became White's Professor of Moral Philosophy in 1952. He translated Frege's *Grundlagen* (1950), gave the William James Lectures at Harvard in 1955, and was president of the Aristotelian Society in 1956–57.

Austin published very little in his lifetime (seven articles by one count); Oxford had a tradition of not publishing while alive. His influence ran through teaching and "Saturday morning" discussion meetings where philosophers and lawyers worked through texts such as Frege's *Grundlagen* and Wittgenstein's *Philosophical Investigations*. He died in Oxford on February 8, 1960. His main books are posthumous reconstructions from his lecture notes.

### P. F. Strawson

Peter Frederick Strawson was born in London on November 23, 1919, and studied Philosophy, Politics and Economics at St John's College, Oxford, from 1937 to 1940. His tutors included J. D. Mabbott and H. P. Grice. After war service he took a post at Bangor, won Oxford's John Locke Prize, and in 1948 became a fellow of University College, Oxford. "On Referring" and his debate with Austin made him internationally known in 1950.

He remained in Oxford: first at University College, and from 1968 as Ryle's successor in the Waynflete Chair of Metaphysical Philosophy at Magdalen. He was elected to the British Academy in 1960 and knighted in 1977. Unlike Austin, he believed in publication and wrote steadily on logic, metaphysics, Kant, grammar, skepticism and the nature of philosophy. He died in Oxford on February 13, 2006.

## Major theories and concepts

### Ordinary language as "the first word" (Austin)

Austin demanded that philosophers meet the ordinary standards of truth and accuracy that apply to any serious subject. His reasons to study how we speak: words are tools, so we should "use clean tools"; words are not the things they describe, so prying them off the world shows their limits; and the common stock of words embodies distinctions people have found worth making over many generations, which is likely sounder, for practical matters, than anything invented in an armchair.

His example is responsibility. Before arguing whether an act was "free," look at what we say when we excuse ourselves: it was an accident, a mistake, unintentional, due to clumsiness. In "A Plea for Excuses" he argues that each excuse picks out a different part of the "machinery of action," so a survey of excuses shows what an action is made of.

Ordinary language is "not the last word," he said, but "the first word." He called his style "linguistic phenomenology": lists of the expressions used in a domain and thought experiments about odd cases. He also cared about the world, not just words: "It takes two to make a truth," and "fact is richer than diction," since language cannot be forearmed against every case it may need to describe.

*Tension:* critics call the method pedantic or limited, since everyday language lacks some distinctions an inquiry needs.

### Performatives and speech acts (Austin)

Philosophers had often assumed that a declarative sentence's job is to describe, so that it is true or false. Austin points to cases where saying something is *doing* something: "I bet you sixpence it will rain tomorrow," a marriage vow, the words that name a ship, a clause in a will. The speaker is not reporting a bet but making one. He first calls such utterances **performatives** and contrasts them with **constatives**, which state facts.

Performatives fail differently from statements: not by being false but by **infelicity**. In a *misfire* the act does not come off (you are already married, or the officiant lacks authority). In an *abuse* it comes off insincerely (a promise you never meant to keep).

Then Austin dismantles his own distinction. Statements can also misfire (a claim about "the king of France" when there is none) or be insincere, and "I state that…" looks like a performative but makes a statement. He replaces it with the idea that every utterance involves several acts at once:

- A **locutionary act** is saying something with a definite content.
- An **illocutionary act** is what you do *in* saying it, with a certain *force*: stating, warning, promising, ordering. Promising and stating that you'll be home for dinner share content but differ in force.
- A **perlocutionary act** is what you bring about *by* saying it: persuading, encouraging. Warning that the ice is thin may persuade someone to stay off it.

Austin's main interest was the illocutionary act. Judging an utterance "true" is, he held, a way of judging it a right and proper thing to say in these circumstances, to this audience, for these purposes. Hence his attack on "the true/false fetish" and "the value/fact fetish": sorting utterances into fact-stating and value-expressing is too crude. The three-way distinction is now widely accepted and is usually counted his central contribution.

*Tension:* because Austin calls his early sections provisional, readers differ on whether he means to defend the performative/constative distinction or to show it fails. A strong reading, on which content cannot be detached from force, faces the Frege–Geach problem of explaining logical connections among speech acts of different force.

### Perception and knowledge (Austin)

*Sense and Sensibilia* attacks the doctrine that we never directly perceive material things, only private "sense-data," chiefly as defended by A. J. Ayer. The usual route is the **argument from illusion**: a straight stick in water looks bent; so what we are aware of must be something bent, not the stick; and since good and illusory experiences can't be told apart from the inside, we are always aware only of such inner items.

Austin objects to nearly every step. Terms like "real," "directly" and "material thing" are never clearly explained. There is no single kind of thing we perceive (rainbows, shadows and flames are seen, but are not "material objects") and no single way of being deceived. An illusion (the bent-looking stick) differs from a delusion (pink rats), and a bar of soap that looks just like a lemon shows that experiences can be indistinguishable by introspection without sharing an object-type. How the stick *looks* is available to other perceivers; the error lies in the *judgment* we make on that basis.

His paper "Other Minds" (1946) holds that knowing is a basic way of apprehending how things are, arising from judgmental acumen exercised in favorable circumstances. Because those capacities are fallible, there are no specially infallible foundations. This places him in the "Oxford realist" line from Cook Wilson and Prichard to John McDowell and Timothy Williamson.

### Truth: where they collided

Austin's "Truth" (1950) defends a refined correspondence theory. A statement is true when the particular situation it concerns is of the type its sentence describes; *descriptive* conventions link sentences to types of situation, and *demonstrative* conventions link a particular statement to the situation it is about. Strawson replied that the theory never says what the conventions connect to, that facts are not things in the world ("It is a fact that P" adds nothing beyond P), that saying something is true is not a remark about linguistic conventions, and that the account cannot handle "There are no unicorns." He favored Ramsey's idea that "P is true" amounts to "P." Most people judged that Strawson had fatally wounded Austin's account.

### Reference and presupposition: "On Referring" (Strawson)

Russell's theory of descriptions (1905) analyzes "The present King of France is bald" as claiming that there is a king of France, that there is only one, and that he is bald. France has no king, so the sentence is false. Strawson's "On Referring" (1950) says this misdescribes what we do. Standing in front of the family car and saying "The car won't start," you are not telling your family that there is a car; you are picking out one they can already identify and saying something about it. This is **identifying reference**.

The key distinction is between a *sentence* and a *use* of it. Whether a speaker succeeds in referring depends on the circumstances. When there is no king, the speaker has not said something false: the existence of a king was **presupposed**, not asserted. The natural reply is "What do you mean?", not "That's false," so the use is neither true nor false (a "truth-value gap"). Presupposition, developed further in *Introduction to Logical Theory* (1952), has had a colossal influence on linguists. Strawson claimed only that there are uses of "the" Russell's theory cannot capture.

*Tension:* Russell replied in "Mr. Strawson on Referring" (1957) that an avowed atheist who exploited Strawson's doctrine to say he did not hold false the proposition that the Ruler of the Universe is wise would strike people as shifty. Neale later argued the issue can be settled in Russell's favor, though Strawson himself came to doubt that any brisk formal argument could settle it.

### Descriptive metaphysics and persons (Strawson)

*Individuals* (1959) helped move Oxford away from the anti-metaphysical stance of Ayer and Austin. Strawson contrasts **revisionary metaphysics**, which tries to improve how we think, with **descriptive metaphysics**, which describes the conceptual scheme we have and cannot do without. There is, he writes, "a massive central core of human thinking which has no history." The idea is recognizably Kantian, and *The Bounds of Sense* (1966) is his reading of Kant's *Critique of Pure Reason*.

The argument starts from reference. To think about particular things we must identify them, and that ties them to one public spatio-temporal framework centered on what we perceive. Material bodies turn out to be the basic particulars.

In the chapter on persons he argues that the concept of a person is **primitive**. We do not refer to a pure mind attached to a body (Descartes's picture), nor to nothing at all; we refer to one kind of thing to which both mental and bodily properties are ascribed. Since we can ascribe experiences to ourselves only if we can ascribe them to others, the criteria we use for others must be adequate, so there is no genuine "problem of other minds."

*Tension:* these are "transcendental arguments," which try to show that what the skeptic doubts is required for the skeptic's own thinking. Barry Stroud and others pressed hard on whether they work, and Strawson later agreed they are problematic as anti-skeptical devices.

### Freedom, resentment and the reactive attitudes (Strawson)

"Freedom and Resentment" (1962) is perhaps Strawson's most famous paper, although he wrote very little moral philosophy and joked he would turn to it only when his powers were waning. The old argument says: if determinism is true, nobody could have done otherwise, so nobody is truly responsible. Strawson asks what holding people responsible actually consists of. His answer is the **reactive attitudes**: gratitude, anger, resentment, forgiveness, love and the like, our natural responses to others' goodwill, ill will or indifference toward us. What matters is the "quality of others' wills towards us," which is why you resent someone who injures you from contempt in a way you do not resent someone who injures you by accident.

We can step back from these attitudes in two ways. An *excuse* (you were pushed) shows that the person's regard for us was not lacking. An *exemption* (a child, someone severely disturbed) removes a person from the full range of attitudes, and we take the **objective attitude**, seeing them as something "to be managed or handled or cured or trained."  Gary Watson later named the excuse/exemption contrast.

Strawson's central claim is that the truth of determinism would not show that everyone is excused or exempt, and that our commitment to ordinary relationships is "too thoroughgoing and deeply rooted" to be given up on the strength of a theoretical thesis. The paper aims to dissolve the problem of determinism and responsibility, not answer it on its own terms.

## Key works

**Austin:** "Other Minds" (1946), on knowledge; "Truth" (1950), the theory Strawson attacked; "A Plea for Excuses" (1956–57), the method applied to responsibility; *Philosophical Papers* (1961), edited by J. O. Urmson and G. J. Warnock; *Sense and Sensibilia* (1962), lectures given at Oxford and Berkeley between 1947 and 1958, edited by Warnock; *How to Do Things with Words* (1962), the 1955 William James Lectures, edited by Urmson (second edition with Marina Sbisà, 1975).

**Strawson:** "On Referring" (*Mind*, 1950); *Introduction to Logical Theory* (1952), on formal logic and ordinary language; *Individuals* (1959), on descriptive metaphysics and persons; "Freedom and Resentment" (1962); *The Bounds of Sense* (1966), on Kant's first *Critique*; *Skepticism and Naturalism* (1985), a "relaxed" realism; *Analysis and Metaphysics* (1992), his mature view of philosophical method.

## Contributions

Austin gave philosophy of language a new unit of analysis, the act performed in speaking rather than the bare sentence. Strawson argued that a theory of reference can be wrong because it misdescribes what we do when we refer, brought presupposition into philosophy of language, led Oxford back to metaphysics, and gave responsibility theory its central concept, the reactive attitudes.

## Impact and legacy

Austin's speech act theory was developed into a systematic account by John Searle, who was shaped as a philosopher in the Oxford of Austin, Ryle and Strawson; Searle's *Speech Acts* (1969) is the best-known successor. It reached linguistics, the philosophy of law, and debates about free speech. Ordinary-language method fell out of fashion as a program, partly under pressure from Grice's account of conversation as governed by cooperation.

Strawson's presupposition spread through linguistics. *Individuals* inspired later essays in descriptive metaphysics, including Gareth Evans's *The Varieties of Reference*, and *The Bounds of Sense* gave transcendental arguments their modern career. His relaxed realism, defending the reality of color, mind and meaning without reducing them to physics, shaped a generation of British philosophers, many his students. "Freedom and Resentment" is the starting point for contemporary work on moral responsibility.

## Criticisms and debates

- **Did Austin ignore conversational norms?** Grice-inspired critics say ordinary-language philosophers mistook what is inappropriate to say (because misleading) for what is false or meaningless. Defenders reply that Austin drew finer distinctions between false, nonsensical and merely impolite uses.
- **Serious and non-serious speech.** Austin treated stage acting and mock assertion as derivative of serious uses. Jacques Derrida challenged that priority and Searle responded, an exchange that became a point of contact between analytic and Continental approaches.
- **Russell versus Strawson.** Strawson held the contrast between identifying a thing and asserting that it exists to be undeniable; Russell and later Neale resisted. Whether the debate is a swap of intuitions is itself disputed.
- **Transcendental arguments.** Strawson's later reply to skepticism was that skeptical doubts are not serious doubts, since no one is persuaded by them. Critics say this shows how we behave, not why the skeptic is wrong.
- **What do the reactive attitudes ground?** Watson asked whether Strawson's view can explain our attitudes to extreme evil; others dispute whether Strawson meant the attitudes to constitute responsibility.

## Misreadings to avoid

- Austin did not say ordinary language is always right; it is "the first word," not the last.
- Performatives are not limited to explicit first-person formulas like "I promise." "Close the door!" can order as well as "I order you to close the door." Austin concluded that no grammatical test marks off performatives.
- "On Referring" does not say Russell was wrong about every use of "the," and offers no full replacement theory.
- Strawson does not deny that determinism could be true. His point is that the attitudes making up responsibility are not something a theoretical thesis could rationally require us to give up.

## What to read first

1. Austin, "A Plea for Excuses" (1956–57): the method in miniature.
2. Austin, *How to Do Things with Words* (1962): performatives and the three-way distinction among acts.
3. Strawson, "On Referring" (1950), alongside Russell's "On Denoting" (1905) in the [Russell entry](bertrand-russell.md).
4. Strawson, "Freedom and Resentment" (1962): short and still fresh.
5. For the larger arguments: Austin's *Sense and Sensibilia* (1962), then chapters 1–3 of Strawson's *Individuals* (1959).
