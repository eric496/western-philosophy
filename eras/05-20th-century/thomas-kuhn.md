# Thomas Kuhn

*Era: 20th Century to Present (1900 – ) · [Era overview](README.md) · [Master index](../../index.md)*

## At a glance

Thomas Kuhn (1922–1996), an American physicist turned historian and philosopher of science, is known for *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions* (1962) and its claims that science develops through paradigms, normal science, crises, and revolutionary shifts — not by steady accumulation of facts.

## Biography

Thomas Samuel Kuhn was born in Cincinnati in 1922 and raised largely in New York. He studied physics at Harvard, completing his doctorate in 1949, and seemed set for a career as a physicist. The turn came as a graduate student, when he helped teach science to humanities undergraduates using historical cases. Reading Aristotle’s *Physics*, he at first found it bafflingly bad as failed Newtonian mechanics. Then it suddenly made sense on its own terms: Aristotle was not a bungling Newton; he was answering different questions with different concepts. That experience shaped everything he later wrote about scientists in different eras inhabiting different conceptual worlds.

Kuhn never returned to research physics. He became a historian of science, teaching at Harvard, Berkeley, Princeton, and finally MIT. His first book, *The Copernican Revolution* (1957), studied how astronomy, cosmology, philosophy, and theology shifted together around Copernicus. *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions* (1962) began modestly, as a monograph for the *International Encyclopedia of Unified Science* — ironically, the Vienna Circle positivists’ own series, whose picture of science Kuhn was about to undermine.

The book became one of the most cited academic works of the twentieth century, and “paradigm shift” escaped into everyday talk, often meaning little more than a big change. Kuhn spent much of his later career clarifying his claims — notably in the Postscript to the second edition (1970) — and insisting he was not a relativist. A celebrated 1965 London colloquium pitting Kuhn against Karl Popper (published as *Criticism and the Growth of Knowledge*, 1970) fixed the two as opposites, though each later acknowledged more common ground than the legend suggests. Kuhn died in Cambridge, Massachusetts, in 1996.

## Major theories and concepts

### Paradigms and normal science

Kuhn’s key term is *paradigm*: in his clarified sense, a concrete scientific achievement — Newton’s *Principia* is the standard example — that is sufficiently unprecedented to attract a lasting group of adherents and sufficiently open-ended to leave them plenty of work to do. Around such an exemplar grows what Kuhn later called a *disciplinary matrix*: shared symbolic generalisations, models, values such as accuracy and simplicity, and standard examples learned in training.

Most science, most of the time, is *normal science*: puzzle-solving within a paradigm. The paradigm supplies the problems, the methods, and the criteria for an acceptable solution, much as a jigsaw’s picture on the box tells you what a solution looks like — except the scientist, Kuhn noted, is far more confident the puzzle *has* a solution. Failure normally reflects on the scientist, not the paradigm. This is not an insult. Kuhn argued that this focused, even dogmatic phase is what lets a field accumulate precise, deep results: without commitment to a framework, every experiment would be a dispute about foundations.

### Anomaly, crisis, and revolution

Sometimes a puzzle persistently resists solution and touches something fundamental: an *anomaly*. The orbit of Uranus was an anomaly handled inside Newtonian astronomy by discovering Neptune. Others pile up. Ptolemaic astronomy, patching discrepancies with ever more complex devices, is Kuhn’s picture of a field approaching *crisis* — a period of insecurity in which rules loosen, rival approaches proliferate, and scientists become willing to consider what previously looked unthinkable.

A *scientific revolution* occurs when an old paradigm is replaced, wholly or in part, by an incompatible new one: Copernican for Ptolemaic astronomy, Einsteinian for Newtonian mechanics, Lavoisier’s oxygen chemistry for phlogiston theory. Kuhn deliberately used a political metaphor. Revolutions are not produced by proof alone, because the standards for what counts as a good proof are partly supplied by the paradigm under dispute. Converts often describe something like a gestalt switch — the same duck-rabbit drawing suddenly seen the other way — and Kuhn compared conversion, persuasively or not, to a change of world: scientists after a revolution work with different concepts in a differently structured field of problems.

### Incommensurability

The most contested claim is that rival paradigms can be *incommensurable*. Kuhn meant several connected things. First, there is no neutral algorithm — no mechanical decision procedure of logic plus observation — that compels every rational scientist to choose the same paradigm. Second, key terms change meaning across a shift: “mass” in Newtonian physics is conserved and constant, while in Einsteinian physics it varies with velocity and relates to energy, so the same word does not pick out quite the same concept. Third, proponents of rival paradigms may partly talk past each other, because they disagree about which problems matter and what a solution must look like.

Incommensurability does *not* mean scientists cannot understand each other at all, or that choice is irrational, or that “anything goes” — that slogan belongs to Paul Feyerabend, not Kuhn. Kuhn insisted paradigm choice is guided by shared values — accuracy, consistency, scope, simplicity, fruitfulness — but argued these values can be weighted differently by reasonable people. In later work he narrowed the claim considerably, to local failures of translation between taxonomies of terms, and described himself as a firm believer in scientific progress — progress *from* earlier paradigms, if not straightforward progress *toward* one final true description.

## Key works

- *The Copernican Revolution* (1957) — an accessible case study of a revolution spanning science, philosophy, and theology.
- *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions* (1962; 2nd ed. 1970) — the paradigm book; the 1970 Postscript, clarifying “paradigm” and answering critics, is essential reading.
- *The Essential Tension* (1977) — essays on the history of science, including his account of reading Aristotle and his replies in the Popper debate.

## Contributions

Kuhn re-founded the philosophy of science on its history. The dominant positivist image had treated science as logic plus observation, ignoring how scientists are trained, how fields cohere, and how concepts change; Kuhn showed those facts were philosophically central. He gave scholars — and then the wider culture — a vocabulary: paradigm, normal science, anomaly, crisis, incommensurability, paradigm shift, and helped create modern history and sociology of science.

## Impact and legacy

Inside philosophy, Kuhn made a purely cumulative, purely logical picture of science hard to defend and set the agenda Popper, Lakatos, and Feyerabend had to answer. In the social sciences, paradigm language was eagerly borrowed — sometimes fruitfully, sometimes loosely. In public life, “paradigm shift” became a cliché of business writing, usually shorn of Kuhn’s meaning. Critics in the 1990s “science wars” blamed Kuhnian ideas for relativism about science — a charge Kuhn rejected to the end.

## Criticisms and debates

Margaret Masterman famously counted more than twenty senses of “paradigm” in *Structure*, a vagueness Kuhn tried to repair in the Postscript. Popperians object that normal science, as Kuhn describes it, celebrates the dogmatism good science should resist, and that crisis-and-conversion makes theory change look irrational. Philosophers such as Donald Davidson argued that the very idea of mutually untranslatable conceptual schemes is incoherent, since recognising a scheme as a language already requires largely understanding it. Historians have questioned whether Kuhn’s pattern fits biology or the social sciences as well as physics and chemistry, and whether revolutions are really as abrupt and total as his early rhetoric suggested. Kuhn conceded ground on several points across his career; whether the concessions leave a strong thesis or a modest one is itself a live debate.

## Misreadings to avoid

- A paradigm is not a vibe, a worldview in the loose sense, or any large change. It is anchored in concrete exemplary achievements and shared practice.
- Incommensurability does not mean total incomprehension or irrationality, and Kuhn was not saying all theories are equally good.
- “Paradigm shift” does not mean a routine upgrade, rebrand, or new product. In Kuhn’s sense it involves incompatible frameworks and changed standards.
- Kuhn was not Feyerabend. Epistemological anarchism — “anything goes” — was Feyerabend’s provocation, not Kuhn’s position.

## What to read first

1. *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions* itself — unusually, the primary text *is* the beginner text; use the 1970 or later edition.
2. The 1970 Postscript — read immediately after the main chapters; it is Kuhn’s own repair kit for his key terms.
3. *The Copernican Revolution*, selections — if you want to see the method at work on one detailed case before the theory.
