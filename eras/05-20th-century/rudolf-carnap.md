## At a glance

- **Dates:** 1891–1970
- **Place:** Born in Ronsdorf (now part of Wuppertal), Germany; worked in Vienna, Prague, Chicago and Los Angeles
- **School/tradition:** Analytic philosophy; logical empiricism, as the leading theorist of the [Vienna Circle](vienna-circle-logical-positivism.md)
- **Known for:** Building concepts out of experience with modern logic, declaring metaphysics meaningless, the principle of tolerance, the split between internal and external questions, and a logic of probability for science

**Responds to:** [Frege](gottlob-frege.md), [Russell](bertrand-russell.md), [Wittgenstein (early)](wittgenstein-early.md), [Kant](../03-early-modern/immanuel-kant.md) · **Answered by:** [Quine](w-v-o-quine.md), [Popper](karl-popper.md), [Kuhn](thomas-kuhn.md)

## Biography

Carnap was born on 18 May 1891 in Ronsdorf, an industrial town near the Ruhr. His mother, a schoolteacher, taught him at home until secondary school. He was raised religious, with the stress on ethics more than doctrine, and he later said that giving up belief in God cost him no crisis of conscience. In 1908 the family moved to Jena, where he went on to study philosophy, physics and mathematics.

Two things at Jena mattered later. One was the Youth Movement, whose idea that social forms could be freely invented rather than inherited runs through his whole philosophy. The other was a reclusive mathematics professor, [Gottlob Frege](gottlob-frege.md). Carnap took every course Frege offered, and his notes survive. He also studied Kant with the neo-Kantian Bruno Bauch.

The First World War interrupted everything. Carnap spent most of 1914–17 at the front, was wounded, and was then posted to a radio research unit in Berlin. There he became a pacifist and a socialist and joined the anti-war Independent Socialist Party. His dissertation, *Der Raum* (*Space*, 1922), was written under Bauch and still had a Kantian frame.

Reading [Russell](bertrand-russell.md) showed Carnap how Frege's logic could rebuild philosophy. Hans Reichenbach introduced him to Moritz Schlick, and in 1926 he moved to Vienna as a lecturer, submitting the draft of *Der logische Aufbau der Welt* as his qualifying thesis. He quickly became the leading theorist of Schlick's discussion group, soon known as the Vienna Circle, though he never liked the label "logical positivism." With Hahn and Neurath he wrote the Circle's 1929 manifesto.

In March 1929 Carnap attended the university course at Davos, Switzerland, best known for the debate between Ernst Cassirer and [Martin Heidegger](martin-heidegger.md) over how to read Kant. Carnap's diary records walks and talks with Heidegger and a respectful first impression, and he read *Being and Time* the next year. Three years later he made Heidegger his chief example of meaningless metaphysics. Michael Friedman's *A Parting of the Ways* (2000) treats Davos as the moment analytic and continental philosophy began to separate.

In 1931 Carnap became professor at the German University in Prague, where he wrote *The Logical Syntax of Language* (1934). As the Nazis tightened their grip, he left for the United States in 1935 with help from Charles Morris and the young [W. V. O. Quine](w-v-o-quine.md). He taught at the University of Chicago from 1936 to 1952, became an American citizen in 1941, spent two years at the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton, and then joined the University of California, Los Angeles. He died in Santa Monica on 14 September 1970.

Carnap wrote long, technical books and kept extensive diaries. His English prose never had the force of his German, and much of the wider purpose behind his technical work was lost on his American readers. A volume on his philosophy in the Library of Living Philosophers, with his intellectual autobiography, appeared in 1963.

## Major theories and concepts

### The *Aufbau*: building the world out of experience

*Der logische Aufbau der Welt* (1928; English *The Logical Structure of the World*) tries to show how all the concepts of science could be defined, step by step, inside one logical system. Carnap calls it a **constitution system**: each new concept is "constituted," that is, defined from earlier ones. He chose as the base one's own experiences, cut into **elementary experiences**, slices of the whole stream of consciousness at a moment. The only undefined relation is the **recollection of similarity**: remembering that one experience resembled another in some respect. From that single relation, using Russell's logic, Carnap defines sense qualities, then the visual field, physical things, other minds and finally cultural objects.

The point was not that the world is made of sensations. Carnap said a system could also start from physical objects. What he wanted was objectivity: if every concept can be stated as a position in a structure, science can be shared even though experiences are private. Internal tensions are real, though. Some steps work only roughly, and the move from experience to physical things relies on rules that are not strict definitions.

### "Overcoming metaphysics": meaning and pseudo-statements

The 1932 essay "Überwindung der Metaphysik durch logische Analyse der Sprache," translated by Arthur Pap as "The Elimination of Metaphysics through Logical Analysis of Language," is the Circle's sharpest polemic. A word has meaning, Carnap argues, only if we can say how simple sentences containing it would be verified. A sentence can fail in two ways. It can use a word with no such conditions, like "the Absolute." Or it can combine meaningful words in a way logic forbids, even when ordinary grammar allows it.

His main example is Heidegger's 1929 lecture *What Is Metaphysics?* Heidegger treats "nothing" as a name for something and then says "Das Nichts selbst nichtet," which Pap rendered "The Nothing itself nothings" and others "nihilates." In logic, Carnap replies, "nothing" is not a name but a way of forming a negative existential statement, one that denies that something exists. To take an example of our own, "nothing is in the box" means "there is no x such that x is in the box." Treat it as a noun, and you get sentences that look deep but say nothing. Such sentences are not false, they are **pseudo-statements**.

Carnap did not think metaphysicians were simply foolish. Metaphysics, he says, expresses an attitude toward life, the job art does better. Hence his line that metaphysicians are musicians without musical ability.

### Logical syntax and the principle of tolerance

In *The Logical Syntax of Language* Carnap set out to treat a language purely by its rules: which strings count as sentences, and which follow from which. Kurt Gödel's work showed that such rules could themselves be described precisely. Carnap built two sample languages, Language I and Language II, and refused to name either the correct one.

That refusal is the **principle of tolerance**. Rival positions in logic and mathematics are not true or false claims about one reality. They are proposals for languages, judged by how well they serve a purpose. "It is not our business to set up prohibitions," Carnap wrote, "but to arrive at conventions." Anyone may build a language; the only demand is to state its rules clearly. From 1935, persuaded by Alfred Tarski, Carnap added semantics, the theory of truth and reference, to syntax. Tolerance stayed for the rest of his career.

### Internal and external questions

"Empiricism, Semantics, and Ontology" (1950) applies tolerance to existence. To talk about a new kind of thing, such as numbers, we adopt a **linguistic framework**, a set of words and rules. Then two kinds of question arise. **Internal questions** are asked inside the framework: "Is there a prime between 10 and 20?" is answered by arithmetic, and "Is there a white paper on my desk?" by looking. **External questions** ask whether the whole system of entities is real, apart from any framework: "Do numbers really exist?" Taken literally, Carnap says, these have no cognitive content. What makes sense is the practical question behind them: should we adopt this framework, and is it useful? He wrote the paper in reply to critics of his semantics, Ryle among them, who accused him of committing to abstract objects.

### Inductive logic and probability

From 1941 Carnap tried to make confirmation exact. He separated two meanings of "probability": **probability₂**, the relative frequency of something in a long run, which is a fact about the world; and **probability₁**, the degree to which evidence supports a hypothesis, which he treated as a logical relation. In *Logical Foundations of Probability* (1950) and *The Continuum of Inductive Methods* (1952) he defined measures of degree of confirmation inside formal languages and found a whole family of them, governed by a parameter for how fast we learn from experience. True to tolerance, he did not claim one method is the right one. A known difficulty is that his early measures give universal laws a degree of confirmation of zero in an infinite world.

## Key works

- *Der Raum* (1922): His dissertation on formal, intuitive and physical space.
- *Der logische Aufbau der Welt* (1928): The constitution system that builds science from elementary experiences.
- "Overcoming Metaphysics through Logical Analysis of Language" (1932): The attack on Heidegger; the clearest statement of meaning-criticism.
- *Logische Syntax der Sprache* (1934; English 1937): Languages as rule systems; the principle of tolerance (§17).
- "Testability and Meaning" (1936–37): Loosens verification into confirmation, using "reduction sentences."
- *Meaning and Necessity* (1947): Semantics of extension and intension; modal logic.
- "Empiricism, Semantics, and Ontology" (1950): Linguistic frameworks; internal vs external questions.
- *Logical Foundations of Probability* (1950): Logical probability and the method of **explication**, replacing a vague concept with an exact one fit for a purpose.

## In their own words

> "In logic, there are no morals. Everyone is at liberty to build up his own logic, i.e., his own form of language, as he wishes."

— *The Logical Syntax of Language*, §17 (trans. Amethe Smeaton, 1937)

> "The Nothing itself nothings."

— Heidegger, *What Is Metaphysics?* (1929), as quoted in Carnap, "The Elimination of Metaphysics through Logical Analysis of Language," §5 (trans. Arthur Pap, 1959)

> "Metaphysicians are musicians without musical ability."

— Carnap, "The Elimination of Metaphysics through Logical Analysis of Language," §7 (trans. Arthur Pap, 1959)

> "questions of the existence of certain entities of the new kind within the framework; we call them internal questions; and second, questions concerning the existence or reality of the system of entities as a whole, called external questions."

— "Empiricism, Semantics, and Ontology," §2 (1950)

> "Let us be cautious in making assertions and critical in examining them, but tolerant in permitting linguistic forms."

— "Empiricism, Semantics, and Ontology," §5 (1950; p. 221 of the 1956 reprint)

> "Our statements about the external world face the tribunal of sense experience not individually, but only as a corporate body."

— Quine, "Two Dogmas of Empiricism," §5, in *From a Logical Point of View* (1953), p. 41

## Contributions

Carnap turned logic into a working tool for philosophy of science. He gave rational reconstruction its most ambitious test in the *Aufbau*, made language choice a matter of explicit rules and practical goals, and founded the modern study of inductive logic. His internal/external distinction is the starting point of today's metaontology, the study of what existence questions mean.

## Impact and legacy

In America, Carnap trained or shaped Quine, Nelson Goodman, Hilary Putnam and Richard Jeffrey, and logical empiricism dominated philosophy of science for a generation. His inductive logic fed into Bayesian epistemology and work on machine learning. Kuhn's *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions* (1962) appeared in the International Encyclopedia of Unified Science, a series Carnap co-edited. By his death the field had moved on. Since the 1980s, though, scholars such as Michael Friedman and Richard Creath have revived his work, and "neo-Carnapian" deflationists such as Amie Thomasson build on his view of ontology.

## Criticisms and debates

- **Quine's "Two Dogmas" (1951).** [Quine](w-v-o-quine.md) argued that "analytic" (true by meaning alone) cannot be defined without circularity, and that the *Aufbau*'s reductionism fails because sentences meet experience only as a whole. That holism undercuts Carnap's split between choosing a framework and testing claims within it. The current scholarly mainstream, following Creath, holds that the two partly talked past each other: Carnap had dropped the *Aufbau*'s reductionism and defined analyticity only for constructed languages, never for natural ones. Whether Quine's worry still hits those definitions is debated.
- **Heidegger.** Heidegger did not reply in public and held that logic is not the highest court for thinking. Readers still dispute whether Carnap refuted Heidegger or misread a lecture never meant as a set of factual claims.
- **Popper.** [Popper](karl-popper.md) rejected both verification as a test of meaning and the very idea of inductive logic. He argued that scientific laws have zero probability and that science advances by bold guesses and attempted refutation.
- **Kuhn.** [Kuhn](thomas-kuhn.md)'s history of paradigm shifts challenged the picture of science as logically reconstructable. How far Kuhn's view conflicts with Carnap's own tolerance of frameworks is debated.
- **Goodman and the *Aufbau*.** Goodman's *The Structure of Appearance* (1951) showed technical gaps in the construction; Friedman later argued it was never a phenomenalist reduction at all.
- **Is tolerance arbitrary?** Critics ask what rational ground there can be for choosing frameworks, if every standard sits inside one. Defenders point to practical aims as the guide.

## Misreadings to avoid

- **"Carnap thought metaphysics was false."** He held it was empty of factual content, which is a different charge.
- **"The *Aufbau* says the world is built out of sensations."** It shows one possible way to define concepts; Carnap allowed a physical basis too.
- **"Tolerance means anything goes."** Every language must have explicitly stated rules, and frameworks are judged by usefulness.
- **"External questions are banned."** They make sense as practical questions about which framework to use.
- **"Quine simply refuted Carnap."** Many historians now treat the debate as unresolved and partly a misunderstanding.

## What to read first

1. "Empiricism, Semantics, and Ontology" (1950): short, clear, and the best entry to his mature view.
2. "The Elimination of Metaphysics through Logical Analysis of Language" (1932): read with Heidegger's *What Is Metaphysics?* beside it.
3. *The Logical Syntax of Language*, Foreword and §17: the principle of tolerance in Carnap's own words.
4. Quine, "Two Dogmas of Empiricism," then Carnap's "Intellectual Autobiography" in the Schilpp volume (1963).
5. The [Stanford Encyclopedia](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/carnap/) article on Carnap for the *Aufbau* and inductive logic.

## Study questions

1. Explain how Carnap uses the analysis of "nothing" as a denial of existence to argue that "The Nothing itself nothings" is a pseudo-statement. Could Heidegger reply that he was not making a factual claim at all?
2. What is the difference between asking "Is there a prime between 10 and 20?" and "Do numbers exist?" in Carnap's terms? What does he think the second question is really asking?
3. State the principle of tolerance. Does it leave any room for saying that one logic is better than another?
4. Why does Quine's holism threaten the line between choosing a framework and testing claims inside it?
5. Carnap built many confirmation measures rather than one. Is that a strength of his inductive logic or a sign that the project failed?
6. Davos is often called the start of the analytic/continental split. Using this entry and [Heidegger's](martin-heidegger.md), say what the two men disagreed about.
