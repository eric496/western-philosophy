## At a glance

- **Dates:** 1938–2002
- **Place:** United States; Harvard, where he held the Joseph Pellegrino University Professorship
- **School/tradition:** Analytic philosophy; libertarian political philosophy; counterfactual epistemology
- **Known for:** *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* (1974) — side constraints, the minimal state, the entitlement theory, the Wilt Chamberlain argument, and the experience machine; later, the tracking account of knowledge in *Philosophical Explanations* (1981)

**Responds to:** [Locke](../03-early-modern/john-locke.md), [Kant](../03-early-modern/immanuel-kant.md), [Rawls](john-rawls.md) · **Answered by:** [Rawls](john-rawls.md) (in his later work), the left-libertarian response of Hillel Steiner, Peter Vallentyne and Michael Otsuka, and G. ## Biography

Robert Nozick was born in Brooklyn, New York, on November 16, 1938, into a Russian Jewish immigrant family. He went through the Brooklyn public schools, then Columbia, where he graduated summa cum laude in 1959 after studying with Sidney Morgenbesser. He took a PhD at Princeton in 1963 under Carl Hempel, spent 1963–64 at Oxford as a Fulbright Scholar, and after early appointments at Princeton, Harvard and Rockefeller University returned to Harvard as a full professor in 1969. He stayed there for the rest of his life, holding the Joseph Pellegrino University Professorship and serving as president of the American Philosophical Association.

Nozick began on the left — founder of Columbia's chapter of the Student League for Industrial Democracy, which later became the campus chapter of Students for a Democratic Society. His early socialism, he later said, rested on an entitlement conception of economic justice combined with the empirical belief that workers were the sole producers of wealth. Reading Hayek and Mises, and long conversations at Princeton with Bruce Goldberg, who introduced him to Murray Rothbard, changed the empirical picture, not the moral premise: he kept the entitlement framework and let it carry him into libertarianism. He said he had been "pulled into libertarianism reluctantly" when he could not answer the arguments on the other side.

*Anarchy, State, and Utopia* appeared in 1974 and won the National Book Award for Philosophy and Religion the following year. It grew out of a 1971 Harvard course on capitalism and socialism that Nozick co-taught with Michael Walzer as a debate; Walzer's side later became *Spheres of Justice* (1983). Nozick never wrote a sequel, joking in *Socratic Puzzles* that he had no interest in "Son of *Anarchy, State, and Utopia*". His later books ranged widely: *Philosophical Explanations* (1981), *The Examined Life* (1989), *The Nature of Rationality* (1993), *Socratic Puzzles* (1997) and *Invariances* (2001). He married the poet Gjertrud Schnackenberg late in life. First treated for stomach cancer in 1994, he died from it in Cambridge, Massachusetts, on January 23, 2002.

## Major theories and concepts

### Rights as side constraints

*Anarchy, State, and Utopia* opens with the sentence that sets the whole project: individuals have rights, and there are things no person or group may do to them without violating those rights. The book's question is how much room such rights leave for a state.

Nozick calls these rights *side constraints* on action rather than goals to be promoted. The distinction matters. A theory that treats rights as goals — a social value whose violations should be minimized — can in principle authorize violating one person's rights to prevent two violations elsewhere. A side-constraint theory refuses that trade: it marks off certain actions as impermissible, inside whose boundary each person is to be left alone. The content is Lockean (life, liberty, property, freedom of contract); the justification is Kantian — to treat persons as ends in themselves rather than merely as means is to accept that one person's loss is not automatically compensated by another's gain, because they are not the same person. This is Rawls's "separateness of persons" theme, used here against utilitarianism and, as we will see, against Rawls's own difference principle.

### The invisible-hand route to the minimal state

Nozick's first task is not Rawls, but the anarchist. If rights are as strong as he says, how can any state be legitimate — given that every state claims a monopoly of coercive force within its territory?

His answer is an *invisible-hand explanation*: a story in which a state could arise from a rights-respecting state of nature through a series of permissible steps that no one intends as state-building. Individuals buy protection from competing *mutual protection agencies*; economies of scale and common dispute-resolution procedures drive clients toward whichever agency grows strongest, until a single *dominant protective association* emerges. In enforcing fair procedures against its competitors and against "independents" who try to punish its clients using unreliable methods, that association becomes an *ultra-minimal state*, and a full *minimal state* when it extends protection to the independents it has disadvantaged, as compensation. Each step is permissible; so the minimal state as a whole can be. It is the "night-watchman" state of classical liberal theory: protection against force, fraud and theft, enforcement of contracts, and the administration of courts. Anything more extensive — redistributive taxation, paternalism, laws making people moral — must, Nozick argues, violate rights.

### The entitlement theory of justice

Part II of *ASU* turns to distributive justice. Nozick's own theory is a *historical entitlement* theory with three principles: justice in acquisition (how previously unowned things become justly held), justice in transfer (how justly held things can change hands), and rectification (what to do when the first two have been violated). A holding is just when it has arisen through repeated applications of the first two, corrected where necessary by the third; the resulting distribution — however unequal — is just because of how it came about, not because it fits any pattern.

That is the key contrast. *End-state* theories (strict equality, average utility) judge a distribution by its shape at a time; *patterned* theories (need, moral desert, Rawls's difference principle) judge it by whether holdings track some feature of persons. Historical theories look at the processes that produced them. For Nozick, only they take seriously what the others ignore: the same income matrix can be a free society or a slave society, depending on how the numbers got there.

### The Wilt Chamberlain argument

The most famous argument in the book is the Wilt Chamberlain example, aimed at every patterned principle at once. Suppose holdings have been distributed exactly as your favorite patterned principle requires — call that *D1*. Chamberlain is a great basketball player; a million fans each cheerfully pay him 25 cents on top of the ticket price to see him play. By season's end he has an extra $250,000. The new distribution, *D2*, no longer fits the pattern.

Nozick asks the question that does the work: if the people were entitled to their shares in *D1*, were they not entitled to spend some of them on Chamberlain? And if so, how can the outcome be unjust? The patterned theorist must either forbid the free exchanges — treating people as unable to dispose of what was by hypothesis justly theirs — or accept the inequality, abandoning the pattern. Nozick concludes: "no end-state principle or distributional patterned principle of justice can be continuously realized without continuous interference with people's lives" (*ASU* 163). Enforcing a pattern, moreover, requires taxing people on what they earn through their labor, and "taxation of earnings from labor is on a par with forced labor" (*ASU* 169) — not an equation with chattel slavery, but the thought that seizing the output of someone's labor is seizing a stretch of time during which that person worked for someone else's purposes.

The reply to Rawls runs through this argument. Rawls's difference principle is for Nozick just one more patterned principle. Rawlsian defenders answer that the principles govern the *basic structure* — the institutional background against which transactions happen — not each exchange, so background taxation and voluntary trade with Chamberlain are compatible. G. A. Cohen pressed a different line: the ex ante justice of *D1* does not survive if *D2* can only be reached through choices (like Chamberlain's demand for extra payment) that the egalitarian ethos of a just society would restrain.

### The Lockean proviso

Nozick's theory of initial acquisition stays close to [Locke](../03-early-modern/john-locke.md) without endorsing Locke's labor-mixing argument. What he keeps is Locke's qualifier that an appropriation must leave "enough, and as good" for others, reinterpreted: an acquisition is permissible so long as it does not leave others on net *worse off than they would have been* with the resource in common. The baseline is a counterfactual about welfare, not a stock of unowned nature. With that reinterpretation the proviso is easy to meet, since market appropriation generally raises others' prospects; it governs edge cases (someone owning the only water source) rather than everyday property. Critics argue the baseline is pitched so low the proviso has almost no bite. Later left-libertarians (Steiner, Vallentyne, Otsuka) accept self-ownership but combine it with a demanding, egalitarian reading of the proviso on *external* resources, using Nozick's starting materials to reach something close to the opposite conclusion.

### The experience machine

Nozick's most famous thought experiment outside politics appears in a short chapter of *ASU* (pp. 42–45). Imagine a machine that can give you, for the rest of your life, any experiences you want; once plugged in you cannot tell you are not actually living them. Would you plug in?

The target is *hedonism* — the view, central to classical utilitarianism, that pleasurable experience is the only thing good in itself. If hedonism were true, we should see no reason not to plug in: from the inside the machine would be at least as good as any real life. But most people hesitate. Nozick gives three reasons: we want to *do* things, not merely have the experience of doing them; we want to be a certain sort of person — "someone floating in a tank is an indeterminate blob"; and plugging in cuts us off from "any deeper reality". If any of these matters to us independently of felt experience, hedonism is false.

### Tracking knowledge

Seven years after *ASU*, Nozick turned to epistemology in *Philosophical Explanations* (1981). The book is sprawling — knowledge, free will, personal identity, the mind–body problem, the meaning of life — but its most influential proposal is a counterfactual, or *tracking*, account of knowledge (pp. 172–187). The classical analysis defined knowledge as justified true belief, which Edmund Gettier's 1963 cases had shown to be insufficient. Nozick's replacement adds two subjunctive conditions. *S* knows that *p* if and only if: (1) *p* is true, (2) *S* believes *p*, (3) if *p* were true, *S* would believe *p* (*adherence*), and (4) if *p* were not true, *S* would not believe *p* (*sensitivity*).

Nozick's image was a tracking missile: when the target moves, the missile moves with it. A belief *tracks* the truth when it would move with the fact. Nozick's own test case is a grandmother who sees that her grandson is well; had he been ill, the family would have kept him away and told her he was well, so her belief fails condition 4 as stated, yet she seems to know. The conditions are relativized to the *method* by which the belief is formed, so that a careful enquirer is not faulted for what she would believe using a different method.

The striking consequence is that *epistemic closure* can fail. Closure says: if you know *p* and know that *p* entails *q*, you know *q*. On Nozick's account you can know you have hands (sensitivity to that fact is high in ordinary life) without knowing you are not a brain in a vat (in the vat world you would still believe you have hands — sensitivity fails). Nozick embraces this: skeptical arguments trade on closure, and dropping it lets us keep ordinary knowledge without refuting the skeptic. Ernest Sosa later pressed that sensitivity should be replaced by *safety* (close worlds in which you believe *p* are worlds in which *p* is true), which preserves closure. The sensitivity-vs-safety debate is now a standard topic in epistemology, with Nozick's chapter the point of departure.

### Later revisions

In *The Examined Life* (1989), a book of philosophical meditations rather than an argumentative treatise, Nozick stepped back from the strong libertarian conclusions of *ASU*. He called the position "seriously inadequate", saying it did not "fully knit the humane considerations and joint cooperative activities it left room for more closely into its fabric", and had not taken sufficient account of the "symbolic" importance of collective action. He offered no worked-out alternative. In a 2001 interview he added that reports of his "deviation (or apostasy!) from libertarianism were much exaggerated"; in the ethics chapter of *Invariances* (2001) he argued that only one layer of ethics — the "ethics of respect", a set of negative rights — may be made mandatory in any society. The position is still within the libertarian family, if looser than the natural-rights framework of *ASU*. How to read the arc of his later work is itself one of the live Nozick debates.

## Key works

- *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* (1974) — the libertarian masterwork: side constraints, the invisible-hand defense of the minimal state, the entitlement theory, Wilt Chamberlain, the experience machine, the framework for utopia.
- *Philosophical Explanations* (1981) — on knowledge, personal identity, free will and meaning. Contains the tracking account and the "closest continuer" theory of personal identity. Winner of Phi Beta Kappa's Ralph Waldo Emerson Award.
- *The Examined Life* (1989) — twenty-seven philosophical meditations; the first book in which Nozick distances himself from parts of his earlier libertarianism.
- *The Nature of Rationality* (1993) — on decision theory, principles of rational choice, and the role of symbolic value in reasons for action.
- *Socratic Puzzles* (1997) — collected papers, including "Why Do Intellectuals Oppose Capitalism?"
- *Invariances* (2001) — on objectivity, truth and consciousness; its ethics chapter is where Nozick restates a loosened but still libertarian core.

## Contributions

Nozick made serious libertarian political philosophy a mainstream subject in Anglophone philosophy. Before *ASU*, the position was mostly an outside tradition carried by economists and polemicists; Nozick gave it the sustained, high-level treatment philosophy departments had to engage with. He introduced tools and examples now part of the discipline's standard kit: side constraints, the historical/patterned/end-state taxonomy, the Wilt Chamberlain argument, the experience machine, the utility monster, and the invisible-hand style of political explanation. In epistemology his tracking account reframed the Gettier debate as a question about subjunctive conditions; his "closest continuer" theory of personal identity is widely taught alongside Parfit's work. He defended an essayistic style, aimed, as *Philosophical Explanations* puts it, at *explanation* rather than *proof*.

## Impact and legacy

Reaction to *ASU* was large and immediate. On the right Nozick became the public face of a philosophically respectable libertarianism alongside the very different figures of Rothbard and Ayn Rand; on the left, almost every major political philosopher of the next generation wrote against him. G. A. Cohen's *Self-Ownership, Freedom, and Equality* (1995) is the most sustained critique, prising self-ownership apart from the private-property conclusions Nozick draws. Left-libertarians combined self-ownership with egalitarian ownership of external resources; communitarians such as Michael Sandel pressed the opposite worry, that Nozick's picture of the person is thinly voluntarist. In epistemology the tracking account spawned a long literature on counterfactual knowledge and closure. Outside philosophy, Wilt Chamberlain is a staple of political-theory textbooks; the experience machine turns up wherever hedonism, well-being research, or debates about virtual reality are discussed.

## Criticisms and debates

- *The bare assertion of rights.* Nozick spends more time on the shape of rights than on their foundation. Critics argue the whole system rests on an undefended premise; his gestural reply — that rights trace back to the capacity for a meaningful life — is a sketch, not a derivation.
- *The invisible-hand defense.* Anarchist critics (Rothbard above all) answered that no actual state has developed by Nozick's story, and that real histories include conquest and expropriation. Nozick replies that an invisible-hand *possibility* already undercuts the claim that every state is necessarily illegitimate.
- *The Wilt Chamberlain argument.* The most-pressed objection is that it assumes the pattern must be reimposed transaction by transaction, when the Rawlsian aim is a background basic structure that leaves individual exchanges alone — Chamberlain can be paid and taxed.
- *Rectification.* Nozick concedes actual holdings are shot through with past injustices, and that past injustices "might be so great as to make necessary in the short run a more extensive state in order to rectify them" (*ASU* 231). What the correct principle of rectification is remains open in his wake.
- *Tracking.* The main debate is whether sensitivity or safety is the right modal condition on knowledge, and whether closure must go. Vogel's lemonade example makes trouble for sensitivity; Nozick's defenders argue closure-friendly accounts cannot explain our ordinary refusals to grant knowledge of radical anti-skeptical claims.

## Misreadings to avoid

- The minimal state is not anarchy. Nozick spends the first part of *ASU* arguing *against* individualist anarchism; he thinks the night-watchman state can be justified, even if nothing more extensive can.
- Side constraints are not goals. Reading Nozick as a utilitarian of rights (minimize total violations) collapses the view he is defending.
- The Wilt Chamberlain argument is not primarily about inequality. It is an argument that *continually enforcing any pattern* requires continual interference with liberty.
- The experience machine is not an argument that pleasure is bad, only that it is not the *only* thing that matters.
- "Taxation of earnings from labor is on a par with forced labor" is a claim about structure (seizing a share of your labor's product is seizing a share of your time), not an equation of a modern tax system with chattel slavery.
- Nozick did not renounce libertarianism in later life so much as hold it at a different distance; the 2001 Sanchez interview is his clearest word on the matter.

## What to read first

1. *Anarchy, State, and Utopia*, Part II (chapters 7–8) — the entitlement theory, Wilt Chamberlain, and the critique of Rawls. The easiest entry into the book's style.
2. The experience-machine passage in Part I (pp. 42–45) — three pages that have launched a thousand ethics seminars.
3. *Philosophical Explanations*, pp. 172–187 — the tracking account of knowledge in Nozick's own words.
4. Jonathan Wolff, *Robert Nozick: Property, Justice, and the Minimal State* (1991) — the standard introduction.
5. G. A. Cohen, *Self-Ownership, Freedom, and Equality* (1995), chapters 1 and 3 — the most sustained critical reply.
