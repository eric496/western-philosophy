# Albert Camus

## At a glance

- **Dates:** 1913–1960
- **Place:** Born in Mondovi, French Algeria; raised in Algiers; later lived in Paris and Lourmarin, France
- **School/tradition:** Philosophy of the absurd and of revolt; routinely grouped with existentialism, a label he spent his career rejecting
- **Known for:** *The Myth of Sisyphus* (1942) and the philosophy of the absurd; the novels *The Stranger* (1942) and *The Plague* (1947); *The Rebel* (1951) and the 1952 break with Sartre it provoked; the 1957 Nobel Prize in Literature

**Responds to:** [Nietzsche](../04-19th-century/friedrich-nietzsche.md), [Kierkegaard](../04-19th-century/soren-kierkegaard.md) · **Answered by:** [Sartre](jean-paul-sartre.md), [Beauvoir](simone-de-beauvoir.md)

## Biography

Albert Camus was born on November 7, 1913, in Mondovi, a village near the port city of Bône (today Annaba) in northeastern French Algeria. His father, Lucien Auguste Camus, a wine-shipping clerk and army veteran, was recalled to service at the outbreak of the First World War and died of wounds from the first Battle of the Marne on October 11, 1914, when Camus was not yet a year old. His mother, Catherine Hélène Camus, was illiterate, partially deaf, and afflicted with a speech difficulty; after her husband's death she moved with her two sons to Belcourt, a poor district of Algiers, where she worked as a cleaner and factory hand to support the family in her own mother's cramped apartment. Camus later described this childhood of hunger, heat, and silence, relieved by the sea and the sun, in his unfinished autobiographical novel *The First Man*.

Teachers at his local primary school recognized his ability and steered him toward a scholarship to the Grand Lycée, near the Kasbah, where he met the Muslim community of Algiers at close range — an early brush with the experience of the outsider that runs through his later work. He read widely and, at about fifteen, suffered his first attack of tuberculosis, a disease that recurred throughout his life and later kept him out of military service. After his Baccalauréat in 1932 he enrolled at the University of Algiers to study philosophy, supporting himself with odd jobs. In the mid-1930s he joined, and was then expelled from, the Algerian branch of the French Communist Party, which wanted colonial grievances in Algeria played down for the sake of Popular Front unity; he also co-founded a left-wing theater troupe, the Théâtre du Travail, writing and acting for it. In 1936 he completed a graduate thesis on the influence of Plotinus and Neoplatonism on Augustine — a telling choice, since Augustine, another North African who wrote his way through guilt and time, stayed with Camus all his life.

From 1938 Camus worked as a journalist for *Alger républicain*, and in June 1939 he wrote some of the first detailed reports by a European Algerian on the famine and poverty of Kabylie, the Berber highlands. When the paper folded he edited its successor, *Le Soir républicain*, opposing France's entry into the Second World War as a pacifist. Unemployed and often penniless, he married his second wife, Francine Faure, in December 1940 (his first marriage, to Simone Hié, had already ended in divorce) and took a teaching post in Oran while finishing the novel that would make him famous. *The Stranger* appeared in June 1942, followed in October by the essay *The Myth of Sisyphus* — the two halves of a planned triptych on the absurd that also included the play *Caligula*. Sartre's admiring review of *The Stranger* helped launch Camus's reputation in Paris just as the two men were about to meet.

Camus returned to occupied France, joined the Resistance, and in 1943–44 published two clandestine "Letters to a German Friend" weighing whether violence against the occupier could be justified. In March 1944 he succeeded his mentor Pascal Pia as editor of *Combat*, the underground newspaper of the non-Communist left, a post that placed him at the center of Parisian intellectual life alongside Sartre and Simone de Beauvoir after the Liberation. *The Plague*, begun during the war and read at the time as an allegory of the Resistance against Nazi occupation, appeared in 1947 and made him a figure of international stature. In 1951 he published *The Rebel*, a philosophical history of revolt and revolution that turned, in its final pages, into an attack on Marxism and on the intellectuals — Sartre chief among them — who excused Communist violence in the name of history. Sartre had the book reviewed with calculated harshness by Francis Jeanson in *Les Temps Modernes* in May 1952; Camus replied with an open letter addressed not to Jeanson but to "Monsieur le Directeur," meaning Sartre himself, and Sartre's "Réponse à Albert Camus," published in the same journal that August, ended the friendship for good.

The 1950s also forced Camus to confront the deepening crisis in his native Algeria, where he hoped, against mounting evidence, for accommodation between the French and Arab populations rather than a war of independence. He published his last completed novel, *The Fall*, in 1956, and in 1958 gathered two decades of journalism on the "Algerian question" into *Algerian Chronicles*, written partly to answer critics who accused him of moral cowardice for never joining the formal denunciations of French colonialism that other intellectuals signed. In October 1957, shortly after the story collection *Exile and the Kingdom*, Camus learned he had won the Nobel Prize in Literature "for his important literary production, which with clear-sighted earnestness illuminates the problems of the human conscience in our times." He accepted it uneasily, telling his Stockholm audience he felt too young and unfinished for such an honor while his own country was "going through unending misery." He did not live to finish much more: on January 4, 1960, he died in a car accident near Sens, as a passenger driven by his friend and publisher Michel Gallimard, who was also killed. He was buried at Lourmarin in Provence, where his family had made their home. Sartre's eulogy, printed within days, praised the "stubborn humanism" Camus had brought to bear against the "massive and deformed events of the day" — from the friend with whom he had broken, and never reconciled, eight years before.

## Major theories and concepts

### The absurd

Camus's starting point is a mismatch: the human mind demands clarity, order, and a reason for things, and the universe answers with silence. "The absurd is not in man nor in the world," he writes, "but in their presence together... it is the only bond uniting them." This is a narrower claim than the word's everyday use suggests — not that life is chaotic or confusing in general, but that a specific collision, between our hunger for meaning and a world that offers none, defines the human situation once religious answers are set aside.

*The Myth of Sisyphus* opens by taking this collision to its sharpest point: "There is only one really serious philosophical problem, and that is suicide." If life has no inherent meaning, why go on living it? Camus considers two tempting exits and rejects both. The first is literal suicide, which he treats as a surrender rather than a solution — it removes the questioner instead of answering the question. The second he calls "philosophical suicide": any leap to a transcendent guarantee of meaning, whether religious faith or a secular substitute, that quiets the absurd by inventing an answer the evidence does not support. Camus's own target here includes philosophers he otherwise respects — Kierkegaard among them — whom he accuses of first exposing the absurd unflinchingly and then escaping it through a leap of faith he considers a kind of reasoned-away despair.

His own answer is to hold the collision open rather than resolve it: live, with full consciousness of the absurd and without consoling it away. His emblem is Sisyphus, condemned by the gods to roll a boulder up a mountain forever, watching it roll back down every time he nears the top. Most readers see only futility in the myth; Camus reads it instead from the moment of the descent, when Sisyphus walks back down knowing exactly what is coming and keeps going anyway. "The struggle itself toward the heights is enough to fill a man's heart," Camus writes. "One must imagine Sisyphus happy." This is not cheerful optimism but a hard-won, lucid defiance — and critics have long asked whether Camus ever shows that such happiness follows logically from absurdity, rather than being an image the artist asserts where the philosopher would need an argument.

### Revolt

*The Rebel* (1951) extends the logic of the absurd from the question of suicide to the question of murder. If nothing has inherent value, Camus notes, then killing becomes "a matter of indifference" — exactly the nihilism he wants to block. His way out is to treat rebellion itself as a starting fact of human experience, parallel to Descartes's cogito: the rebel who says "thus far, and no further" is, in that act, asserting that something — their own dignity, and by extension everyone's — is worth protecting. "I revolt, therefore we are," Camus writes, turning an apparently individual protest into a claim about a shared human community. From this he draws a conclusion many of his contemporaries found provocative: that an "analysis of rebellion leads at least to the suspicion that... a human nature does exist, as the Greeks believed" — a direct rejection of the idea, central to Sartre's existentialism, that human beings have no fixed nature and simply make themselves through their choices.

Revolt, for Camus, has to carry its own limits, because the thing it defends — human solidarity — is violated the moment revolt turns into unlimited violence against other humans. His central historical argument is that modern revolutionary movements, Communism above all, abandon this limit: believing history guarantees justice at the end, they accept any amount of present killing as the price of that future, producing what Camus calls "logical crime" — murder planned, foreseen, and rationally defended by doctrine. The argument is sweeping rather than closely documented, and one-sided in a way contemporaries noticed at once: Camus spends far more energy on Communist violence than on Nazism, says almost nothing about the atomic bombing of Japan (which he had himself protested in 1945), and barely mentions French colonialism as a war over Algeria's independence approached. It was this charge — of turning a philosophy of revolt into a Cold War weapon trained almost solely on the left — that Sartre's camp pressed hardest in 1952, and that still shapes how *The Rebel* is read.

### The absurd made fiction: Meursault and the outsider

Camus's philosophy and his novels were conceived together, and *The Stranger* is best read as the absurd worked out in a life rather than argued in an essay. Meursault, its narrator, kills a man on an Algiers beach in what the novel presents as almost an accident of heat and glare, and is condemned less for the killing itself than for his failure to perform the expected emotions at his mother's funeral and his own trial. He is Camus's "outsider": someone who observes his own life, and eventually his own death sentence, with the same detached clarity Camus asks us to turn on the universe itself. Only awaiting execution does he become fully conscious, dying — like Sisyphus walking back down the mountain — without illusion and, in Camus's terms, triumphant for it.

The novel's treatment of its nameless Arab victim has become one of the most contested points in Camus studies. The man Meursault kills is never named and barely speaks; critics led by Edward Said have read this as symptomatic of a wider erasure of Arab presence and voice across Camus's fiction, including the uncounted Algerian dead who have no role in *The Plague*. The Algerian novelist Kamel Daoud answered this directly in *The Meursault Investigation*, retelling *The Stranger* from the perspective of the murdered man's brother — a literary rebuke that is also, as its own reviewers note, a tribute to the original's enduring hold.

### The Plague and the ethics of limits

*The Plague* (1947) takes the individual lucidity of *The Myth of Sisyphus* and gives it a social form, anticipating *The Rebel*. An epidemic seals off the Algerian city of Oran; the only response available to its characters, short of denial or the theology of collective guilt voiced by the priest Paneloux, is to organize unglamorous "sanitary squads" and do the limited, specific work in front of them. Camus's narrator-physician, Rieux, states the moral directly: "there's no question of heroism in all this. It's a matter of common decency." The refusal to stage the plague as a drama of heroes and villains is deliberate — Camus wanted a model of resistance to catastrophe that needed no grand meaning, exactly as *The Myth of Sisyphus* had asked. Written with the Nazi occupation in view and read again during the COVID-19 pandemic, the book endures because its answer — act, with others, without fanfare, wherever you happen to be — asks for no metaphysics at all.

### Why Camus rejected the existentialist label

Camus was close to Sartre, shared his vocabulary of the absurd and of choice, and is shelved beside him in almost every survey — yet he denied all his life that he was an existentialist, and the Swedish Academy's own description of him as representing "the philosophical movement called Existentialism" is exactly the kind of labeling he resisted. His reasons were not merely personal. First, he had little interest in the systematic ontology that occupies Sartre and Heidegger; where they built philosophical systems, he worked in images. Second, he treated existentialism as an all-or-nothing doctrine — to be a real existentialist, in his view, meant accepting its whole apparatus, not borrowing a mood from it. Third, and most sharply, he rejected the thesis that "existence precedes essence": against Sartre's view that human beings have no fixed nature and invent themselves through choice alone, Camus's argument for revolt depends on there being a shared human nature worth defending in the first place. Sartre, for his part, accepted the label Camus refused and built from the cogito "an essay on phenomenological ontology" — exactly the foundation Camus's absurdism declines to lay.

## Key works

- *L'envers et l'endroit* (Betwixt and Between, 1937) — Camus's first book, early autobiographical and philosophical sketches.
- *Noces* (Nuptials, 1939) — lyrical essays on the Algerian landscape that state his starting point: no afterlife, no salvation, only the physical present.
- *L'étranger* (The Stranger, 1942) — the novel of Meursault's killing, trial, and execution; the absurd lived rather than argued.
- *Le mythe de Sisyphe* (The Myth of Sisyphus, 1942) — the philosophical essay on suicide, the absurd, and Sisyphus as its emblem.
- *Caligula* (written earlier, published 1944) and *Le malentendu* (The Misunderstanding, 1944) — plays completing the "cycle of the absurd."
- *La peste* (The Plague, 1947) — the Oran epidemic novel; collective resistance to catastrophe without heroics.
- *L'homme révolté* (The Rebel, 1951) — the philosophical history of revolt and revolution that provoked the break with Sartre.
- *La chute* (The Fall, 1956) — a confessional monologue on guilt by a self-styled "judge-penitent"; Camus's last completed novel.
- *L'exil et le royaume* (Exile and the Kingdom, 1957) — short fiction published just before the Nobel announcement.
- *Actuelles III: Chronique algérienne* (Algerian Chronicles, 1958) — two decades of journalism on Algeria, gathered partly in self-defense.
- *Le Premier homme* (The First Man, published posthumously 1994) — the unfinished autobiographical novel found in the car at his death.

## Contributions

Camus gave twentieth-century thought a working vocabulary — the absurd, revolt, the outsider — precise enough to argue with and vivid enough to escape the seminar room, so that "absurd" and "Sisyphean" now circulate in everyday speech carrying something of his specific meaning. He is one of the few writers to use the novel as a philosophical instrument without turning fiction into illustrated doctrine: *The Stranger* argues nothing explicitly and demonstrates everything. And in *The Rebel* he made one of the first sustained attempts by a writer of the non-Communist left to ask why revolutions aimed at liberation so often end in terror — a question that outlived its Cold War context, including a post-9/11 afterlife in arguments, Paul Berman's among them, over whether Camus's distinctions between legitimate and illegitimate political killing speak to contemporary terrorism.

## Impact and legacy

The Sartre–Camus rupture of 1952 became, as much as either man's books, the symbol of a divided Cold War left: Sartre and the pro-Communist intelligentsia on one side, Camus and an anti-totalitarian left on the other. That split still shapes how each is read. *The Plague* has had its own second life — read as Resistance allegory on publication, and read again, in huge new print runs, during the COVID-19 pandemic of 2020 for its account of fear, denial, and the discipline of "common decency." The existentialist label he fought stuck anyway in popular usage, if not among most specialists; meanwhile, Daoud's *The Meursault Investigation* and Said's postcolonial criticism have made Camus's treatment of Algeria and its people a live, unresolved question rather than a settled one. Camus's lifelong opposition to capital punishment, pressed in essays and in *The Stranger* and *The Plague* alike, is often cited in later European abolitionist arguments. And the 1957 Nobel Prize, awarded when he was forty-four, confirmed in his lifetime what his death three years later could not take away: a reputation as the moralist of a damaged century who refused, in his own words, both the lie and the silence of resignation.

## Criticisms and debates

- **Does the happiness follow from the argument, or only from the writing?** Camus's move from "life is absurd" to "one must imagine Sisyphus happy" is less a deduction than an image urged on the reader; even sympathetic commentators note that at exactly this step the philosopher gives way to the artist, and ask whether the conclusion — that absurd life is worth living intensely — is established rather than displayed.
- **Is The Rebel's indictment of Communism bought at the price of everything else?** Camus's near-silence on Nazism, the Hiroshima and Nagasaki bombings, and French colonialism, against his evident awareness of all three, has led critics to see the book less as even-handed philosophy than as a document of its Cold War moment — a reading Sartre's side pressed in 1952 and that recent scholarship largely sustains.
- **The existentialist label, again.** Some commentators treat Camus's denial as a symptom of his quarrel with Sartre rather than a sound distinction, arguing his passionate individualism makes him, if anything, a truer existentialist than the label's inventor. Others hold the denial well founded, pointing to his rejection of "existence precedes essence" and his lack of interest in ontology. Both positions remain current.
- **Algeria and the missing Arab voice.** Camus's refusal to join anti-colonial manifestos, and the near-total absence of named, speaking Arab characters in his fiction, have drawn sustained criticism from Said and others; Conor Cruise O'Brien's reading of *The Fall* as Camus's own confession of complicity is one of several efforts, not universally accepted, to recover a more self-critical Camus from this record.
- **Is revolt's "human nature" argued or assumed?** Camus treats a common human nature as something rebellion reveals, but offers this more as an intuition confirmed by the slave's defiance than as a developed argument, open to the same charge of assertion-over-argument made against the Sisyphus essay.

## Misreadings to avoid

- The absurd is not a vague sense that life is confusing or chaotic. It names a specific structural mismatch — between the mind's demand for meaning and a universe that offers none — that exists only in the encounter between the two, never in either alone.
- Camus did not counsel suicide, and did not counsel resignation either; *The Myth of Sisyphus* explicitly rules out both physical and "philosophical" suicide in favor of lucid, continued living.
- Rebellion in Camus's sense is not a license for violence; *The Rebel*'s entire argument is that revolt must respect limits, precisely because unlimited revolt against oppression collapses into the very murder it set out to oppose.
- Camus was not indifferent to Algeria's suffering out of comfort or evasion; *Algerian Chronicles* records two decades of personal anguish over a conflict in which he hoped, unsuccessfully, for a future that did not require either side's destruction. That he judged silence on some points the lesser evil does not settle whether the silence was right — critics and defenders still disagree.
- Being grouped with Sartre as an "existentialist" is a matter of shelving convenience, not of settled agreement between the two men; Camus gave specific philosophical reasons, not only personal ones, for refusing the term.

## What to read first

1. *The Myth of Sisyphus* — read the opening chapter on suicide and the final chapter on Sisyphus first; they contain the whole argument in miniature.
2. *The Stranger* — short, and the clearest case of Camus's philosophy lived out as fiction rather than argued as doctrine.
3. *The Plague* — the social extension of the absurd, and the best introduction to why Camus prized "common decency" over heroism.
4. *The Rebel*, selectively — the opening chapters on absurdity and murder, and the closing pages on Sartre and "the cult of history," are the parts most directly relevant to the 1952 break; the historical middle chapters can be skimmed on a first pass.
