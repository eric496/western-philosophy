## At a glance

- **Dates:** Wilfrid Sellars (1912–1989) · Donald Davidson (1917–2003) · Hilary Putnam (1926–2016) · David Lewis (1941–2001)
- **Place:** Minnesota, Yale, Pittsburgh, Stanford, Berkeley, Harvard, UCLA, Princeton
- **School/tradition:** Analytic philosophy of language, knowledge and metaphysics after logical empiricism
- **Known for:** the Myth of the Given and the space of reasons; truth-conditional semantics, radical interpretation and the principle of charity; Twin Earth and semantic externalism; the brain in a vat; counterfactuals, conventions and the plurality of worlds

**Responds to:** [Quine](w-v-o-quine.md), [Vienna Circle](vienna-circle-logical-positivism.md), [Frege](gottlob-frege.md), [Wittgenstein (late)](wittgenstein-late.md) · **Answered by:** [Kripke](saul-kripke.md) (on names, necessity and counterpart theory), and the Pittsburgh neo-Sellarsians (Richard Rorty, John McDowell, Robert Brandom), who have no entry in this repo

**Why they are grouped here.** These four were not a school, and they disagreed sharply. They belong together because each took over the ground [Quine](w-v-o-quine.md) had cleared. "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" (1951) had denied that meanings could sort truths into analytic and synthetic, and that each statement meets experience alone. That left three questions. If knowledge has no foundation of bare sense data, what makes it knowledge? If there are no meanings in Quine's old sense, what is a theory of meaning? And if philosophy is continuous with science, may it still do metaphysics? Sellars answered the first, Davidson the second, Putnam pressed all three, and Lewis answered the third with a confident yes. Putnam's functionalism belongs to the story of the mind and is told in [Philosophy of Mind after Ryle](philosophy-of-mind-after-ryle.md); this entry covers his work on meaning and realism.

## Biography

**Wilfrid Stalker Sellars (1912–1989)** was born in Ann Arbor, Michigan, on 20 May 1912. His father, Roy Wood Sellars, was a Michigan professor and a founder of American Critical Realism. Wilfrid went to school partly in Paris, where at the Lycée Louis le Grand he first met philosophy, as a young French Marxist. He studied at Michigan and Buffalo and then at Oriel College, Oxford, as a Rhodes Scholar, but he never finished a doctorate, at Oxford or later at Harvard. Herbert Feigl, a former member of the [Vienna Circle](vienna-circle-logical-positivism.md), hired him at Iowa in 1938. After Navy service from 1943 to 1946 he cured years of writer's block by writing up to ten hours a day. At Minnesota he and Feigl edited *Readings in Philosophical Analysis* (1949) and founded the journal *Philosophical Studies*. He moved to Yale in 1959 and to Pittsburgh in 1963, where he stayed until his death on 2 July 1989. His prose is famously dense, and he trained many graduate students who carried his ideas on.

**Donald Herbert Davidson (1917–2003)** was born in Springfield, Massachusetts, on 6 March 1917. As a Harvard undergraduate he studied literature and classics and fell under the influence of Alfred North Whitehead. He met [Quine](w-v-o-quine.md) there in 1939–40, and after Navy service in the Mediterranean he finished a 1949 dissertation on Plato's *Philebus*, by then already moving towards Quine's analytic style. He taught at Stanford (1951–1967), Princeton, Rockefeller and Chicago, and from 1981 at Berkeley. He wrote essays, not treatises; they were gathered in *Essays on Actions and Events* (1980) and *Inquiries into Truth and Interpretation* (1984). He died suddenly in Berkeley on 30 August 2003, after knee surgery, leaving a book on predication to be published posthumously.

**Hilary Putnam (1926–2016)** began as a critic of logical positivism and a scientific realist, and also did serious mathematics, including work on Hilbert's tenth problem. He taught at Harvard from 1965. His career is a sequence of public changes of mind: he invented functionalism and then attacked it, defended a strong realism, then *internal realism* from 1976, then gave that up too. His moral and political concerns showed first in political activism and from the late 1970s in his philosophy, which came to include books on ethics and on Jewish philosophy.

**David Kellogg Lewis (1941–2001)** was born in Oberlin, Ohio, to two academics. At Swarthmore he spent a year at Oxford, where he heard J. L. Austin's last lectures and was tutored by Iris Murdoch. He took his Ph.D. at Harvard under Quine; the dissertation became his first book, *Convention* (1969). He taught at UCLA from 1966 and at Princeton from 1970 until his death in 2001, and he visited Australia almost every year from 1979, where he worked closely with philosophers there. He wrote mostly in short, very clear papers, and nearly all of them fit one large system.

## Major theories and concepts

### Sellars: the Myth of the Given

Empiricists had long held that knowledge rests on a *given*: some items, such as sense impressions, that we know directly, without inference and without needing to know anything else first. They are supposed to be the foundation on which everything else is built. In "Empiricism and the Philosophy of Mind" (1956), Sellars argued that this picture asks one thing to do two jobs that cannot be combined. To support other beliefs, the given must be the kind of thing that can be a reason, which means it has to be something like a claim that could be true or false. But then it is no longer bare and free of concepts, and knowing it needs other knowledge after all. His way of putting the point became a slogan. Calling something knowledge is not describing an inner event; it is "placing it in the logical space of reasons, of justifying and being able to justify what one says." That space is *normative*: it concerns what follows from what and what counts as good evidence, and Sellars held that such facts could never be analysed without remainder into facts about sensations or behaviour.

His example is a necktie salesman. To know a tie's colour just by looking, he must already know that under the shop's new electric lighting colours are not what they seem, and that looking is a reliable guide only in standard conditions. Even seeing a colour, as a piece of knowledge, draws on a network of other things one knows. Sellars did not swap foundations for a circle. He rejected both the elephant on a tortoise and the snake swallowing its tail. Empirical knowledge is rational "not because it has a foundation but because it is a self-correcting enterprise which can put any claim in jeopardy, though not all at once." The tension critics press is how anyone ever gets into the space of reasons. Sellars's answer is training: we pick up habits by causal means until, in enough number, they earn the normative description.

### Sellars: the manifest and the scientific image

In "Philosophy and the Scientific Image of Man" (1962), Sellars defined the aim of philosophy as understanding "how things in the broadest possible sense of the term hang together in the broadest possible sense of the term." The great clash, he thought, is between two pictures. The *manifest image* is the framework of persons and things in which we first understood ourselves, with people who think, choose and act for reasons. The *scientific image* describes the same world in terms of the theoretical entities of physics and neuroscience. Sellars was a firm scientific realist: in describing and explaining the world, the scientific image has the last word. But it has no room of its own for norms and intentions. So the task is a *stereoscopic* view that keeps the language of persons and reasons, which is a language of what we *ought* to do, alongside a science that tells us what there is. His *Myth of Jones* belongs here: a story in which our ancestors' talk of inner thoughts began as a theory, modelled on outer speech, to explain behaviour.

### Davidson: truth, meaning and radical interpretation

In "Truth and Meaning" (1967), Davidson proposed that a theory of meaning for a language can take the form of a theory of truth. Building on [Frege's](gottlob-frege.md) idea that we grasp a sentence when we grasp its truth condition, and on Alfred Tarski's definition of truth, the theory would produce, for every sentence, a theorem such as: "Schnee ist weiss" is true if and only if snow is white. A finite set of axioms about words and ways of combining them would yield such a *T-sentence* for each of infinitely many sentences. That would explain how finite speakers can understand new sentences, without positing the entities called meanings that [Quine](w-v-o-quine.md) distrusted.

How could anyone know such a theory is right for a stranger's language? Davidson's answer is *radical interpretation*, his version of Quine's field linguist. The interpreter sees which sentences a speaker holds true and in what circumstances, and must work out belief and meaning together, since either can be adjusted to fit the other. The only way to break the circle is the *principle of charity*: assume the speaker's beliefs are mostly true and consistent by our lights. In his words, "Charity is forced on us; whether we like it or not, if we want to understand others, we must count them right in most matters." Charity is not politeness. It is a condition of treating the noises as language at all, and it leaves room for disagreement, since disagreement only makes sense against a large background of agreement.

### Davidson: against conceptual schemes

From radical interpretation Davidson drew a striking conclusion in "On the Very Idea of a Conceptual Scheme" (1974). Many writers, from Benjamin Lee Whorf to Thomas Kuhn, had spoken of languages or theories as different *conceptual schemes* organising the same neutral experience, perhaps so different as to be untranslatable. Davidson argued that the very distinction between an organising scheme and the content waiting to be organised is "a dogma of empiricism, the third dogma", the one Quine kept after rejecting the other two. If a language could not be translated at all, we would have no grounds to call it a language. If it can be partly translated, charity makes differences look like differences of belief within shared concepts. So we can make no clear sense of radically different schemes, and for the same reason no sense of saying we all share one. The payoff, he argued, is not relativism but direct contact with the world: without the dogma, "we do not give up the world".

### Davidson: anomalous monism

In "Mental Events" (1970), Davidson tried to reconcile three claims that seem to clash. Mental events, such as a decision, cause physical events and are caused by them. Wherever there is causation there is a strict law. But there are no strict laws linking mental and physical descriptions, because ascribing beliefs and desires is governed by charity and rationality, which have no echo in physics. His answer is that every mental event *is* a physical event, since it must fall under a strict physical law to be a cause, but mental *kinds* do not reduce to physical kinds. This is a monism, one kind of event, which is *anomalous*, free of psychophysical laws. Its companion, from "Actions, Reasons, and Causes" (1963), is that the reasons for which we act are causes of our actions. Critics such as Jaegwon Kim objected that if only physical properties do causal work, mental properties become idle, a charge of epiphenomenalism that is still debated.

### Putnam: Twin Earth and semantic externalism

In "The Meaning of 'Meaning'" (1975), Putnam attacked two assumptions about meaning: that knowing a word's meaning is being in a certain psychological state, and that meaning fixes what the word refers to. Imagine Twin Earth, identical to Earth except that the clear liquid in its rivers and rain is not H₂O but a different compound, XYZ. In 1750, before chemistry could tell them apart, Oscar on Earth and his molecule-for-molecule twin on Twin Earth were in the same psychological state when they said "water". Yet Oscar's word referred to H₂O and his twin's to XYZ. So what is in the head does not fix reference. His conclusion: "Cut the pie any way you like, 'meanings' just ain't in the head!" Two further ideas fill this out. Natural-kind words like "water" and "gold" work like pointing: they apply to whatever has the same underlying nature as *this* stuff here, whatever we happen to know about it. And there is a *division of linguistic labour*: Putnam admits he cannot tell an elm from a beech, yet his word "elm" refers to elms, because the community includes experts whose skills fix the reference for everyone. These views converged with [Kripke's](saul-kripke.md) work on names. The tension, pressed by Jerry Fodor and others, is whether psychology needs a *narrow* notion of content that twins share.

### Putnam: internal realism and the brain in a vat

From 1976 Putnam attacked *metaphysical realism*, the view that there is one true and complete description of a world of objects fixed independently of any conceptual framework, and that truth is correspondence to it. He argued that nothing in our use of words could single out one such correspondence among countless possible ones. In its place he proposed *internal realism*: objects and truth make sense only within a framework, and truth is idealised rational acceptability rather than a view from God's eye.

The brain in a vat, in chapter 1 of *Reason, Truth and History* (1981), applies externalism to skepticism. Suppose we were, and always had been, brains in a vat, fed experiences by a computer. Then our word "vat" would never have been causally connected to real vats; it would refer at most to features of the computer's signals. So the sentence "we are brains in a vat", said by such brains, would be false. Putnam concluded that the supposition is self-refuting. Critics have asked what the argument really proves. At best it shows that we cannot truly *say* we are envatted; it may not show that we are not. Putnam himself later gave up internal realism for a more direct realism.

### Lewis: conventions and counterfactuals

Quine had asked how language could rest on convention when no one ever met to agree. In *Convention* (1969), Lewis answered with game theory, following the economist Thomas Schelling. A convention is a regularity that solves a *coordination problem*, a situation where everyone wants to act as the others do, such as which side of the road to drive on. Each conforms because they expect the others to, and each knows the others know this. No agreement is needed. Lewis applied this to language: a community uses a language by conventions of truthfulness and trust in it. The book gave philosophers and economists an early account of *common knowledge*.

*Counterfactuals* (1973) analysed conditionals contrary to fact, such as the claim that if kangaroos had no tails they would topple over. Such a statement is true, roughly, if in the possible worlds most similar to ours where kangaroos lack tails, they topple over. Ranking worlds by similarity lets Lewis explain why counterfactuals do not obey rules that ordinary conditionals obey. He later used counterfactuals to analyse causation: one event causes another if the second depends counterfactually on the first, or is linked to it by a chain of such dependence.

### Lewis: modal realism

Those analyses talk freely of possible worlds, and Lewis meant the talk literally. "I believe, and so do you," he wrote, that things could have been different, and ordinary language paraphrases this as saying that "there are many ways things could have been besides the way they actually are." He took the paraphrase at face value. Other possible worlds are as real and concrete as ours, each spatiotemporally sealed off from the others. "Actual" works like "here": it picks out the world we are in, not a special status. Things exist in only one world; what is possible for me depends on my *counterparts*, people in other worlds who resemble me closely. This is *modal realism*, defended at length in *On the Plurality of Worlds* (1986). Lewis argued that the theory earns its keep, giving unified accounts of possibility, properties, propositions and counterfactuals. He granted that it "does disagree, to an extreme extent, with firm common sense opinion about what there is". He treated the usual reaction, the *incredulous stare*, as a real cost to be weighed against those benefits rather than as a refutation. [Kripke](saul-kripke.md) objected that counterpart theory changes the subject. When I say Humphrey might have won the election, I care about Humphrey himself, not about someone else in another world who resembles him.

- Davidson, "Actions, Reasons, and Causes" (1963) and "Mental Events" (1970) — reasons as causes, and anomalous monism; collected in *Essays on Actions and Events* (1980).

- Putnam, "The Meaning of 'Meaning'" (1975) — Twin Earth and the division of linguistic labour; in *Mind, Language and Reality* (1975).
- Putnam, *Reason, Truth and History* (1981) — the brain in a vat and internal realism.
- Lewis, *Convention* (1969) — convention and language explained without agreement.
- Lewis, *Counterfactuals* (1973) — the similarity semantics for counterfactuals.
- Lewis, *On the Plurality of Worlds* (1986) — the full defence of modal realism.

## Contributions

Sellars gave the strongest argument against foundationalism built on sense data, and the phrase "space of reasons" that later epistemology still uses. Davidson turned the theory of meaning into a precise project with truth at its centre, and made interpretation, charity and holism central to the philosophy of mind. Putnam showed that meaning and reference depend on the world and on other people, and tied that point to old questions about realism and skepticism. Lewis showed that systematic metaphysics could return in analytic dress, with conventions, counterfactuals, causation and possibility analysed in one connected system.

## Impact and legacy

Each opened a lasting line of work. Sellars's heirs in the so-called Pittsburgh school, Richard Rorty, John McDowell and Robert Brandom, built on the Myth of the Given and the space of reasons, and his manifest and scientific images frame debates about whether science leaves room for persons. Davidson's program shaped formal semantics and the philosophy of action, and his attack on conceptual schemes became a standard reply to relativism. Putnam's Twin Earth, with Tyler Burge's arguments about social content, made *externalism* a central position in philosophy of mind; his brain in a vat is a standard case in epistemology courses. Lewis's similarity semantics for counterfactuals is the standard account, his analysis of convention influenced economics and linguistics, and even philosophers who reject modal realism usually set out their own views against his.

## Criticisms and debates

These remain live disputes. Against Sellars: critics doubt whether rejecting the given leaves experience any role in justifying belief; McDowell argued that experience must itself be conceptual to do so, which reopens the problem in a new form. Against Davidson: whether charity is a necessary condition of interpretation or an assumption that might fail, and whether anomalous monism leaves mental properties without causal work. Against Putnam: whether narrow content is needed for psychology, whether the brain-in-a-vat argument refutes skepticism or only reshapes it, and whether internal realism avoids the relativism Putnam rejected. Against Lewis: the incredulous stare at concrete worlds; Kripke's Humphrey objection to counterparts; and the worry that if other worlds are cut off from ours, we could not know anything about them.

## Misreadings to avoid

- Sellars does not deny that we have sensations or that perception gives non-inferential knowledge. He denies that such knowledge stands apart from all other knowledge as a self-sufficient foundation.
- Davidson's charity is not the claim that everyone is right. It is the claim that massive disagreement is unintelligible, so meaningful error needs a background of agreement.
- Anomalous monism is not dualism. Every mental event is a physical event; only the laws are denied.
- Putnam's Twin Earth slogan is not a claim that people have no thoughts. It is a claim that what our words refer to depends partly on our surroundings and our community.
- Putnam's internal realism did not deny that there is a world. It denied that we can describe it from no point of view; he later dropped the view himself.
- Lewis's possible worlds are not distant planets or parallel universes reachable by physics. They are not spatially or causally connected to us at all.

## What to read first

1. Putnam, "The Meaning of 'Meaning'", the Twin Earth sections — vivid, and the easiest way in.
2. Putnam, *Reason, Truth and History*, chapter 1, "Brains in a Vat".
3. Davidson, "On the Very Idea of a Conceptual Scheme" — short, and it shows charity at work.
4. Lewis, *Counterfactuals*, chapter 1, then *On the Plurality of Worlds*, chapter 1.
5. Sellars, "Philosophy and the Scientific Image of Man", then "Empiricism and the Philosophy of Mind" with a guide such as Robert Brandom's study guide in the 1997 Harvard edition.

## Study questions

1. Sellars says knowing is not an inner event but a position in "the logical space of reasons". Use the necktie salesman to explain why even seeing a colour, as knowledge, needs other knowledge. Does this make knowledge circular?
2. Davidson holds that we must count others "right in most matters" to interpret them at all. Could there be a language whose speakers were mostly wrong by our lights? What would count as evidence?
3. Reconstruct the Twin Earth argument as numbered premises. Which premise would someone who believes in narrow content reject?
4. If you were a brain in a vat, what would your word "vat" refer to? Does Putnam's argument show you are not envatted, or only that you cannot truly say you are?
5. Lewis claims that "actual" works like "here". Is our world special in any way on his view? Weigh his theory's explanatory benefits against the incredulous stare.
