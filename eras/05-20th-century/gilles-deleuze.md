# Gilles Deleuze

## At a glance

- **Dates:** 1925–1995
- **Place:** Born and lived in Paris; taught in lycées and provincial universities and, from 1969 to 1987, at the experimental University of Paris VIII (Vincennes, later Saint-Denis)
- **School/tradition:** French post-war philosophy, usually grouped with poststructuralism; a "philosophy of difference" and of immanence
- **Known for:** Freeing difference from identity, the virtual and the actual, and, with Félix Guattari, desiring-production, the rhizome and deterritorialization; later, philosophy as the creation of concepts

**Responds to:** [Hume](../03-early-modern/david-hume.md), [Spinoza](../03-early-modern/baruch-spinoza.md), [Leibniz](../03-early-modern/gottfried-wilhelm-leibniz.md), [Kant](../03-early-modern/immanuel-kant.md), [Hegel](../04-19th-century/g-w-f-hegel.md), [Nietzsche](../04-19th-century/friedrich-nietzsche.md), [Marx](../04-19th-century/karl-marx.md), [Bergson](henri-bergson.md), [Heidegger](martin-heidegger.md) · **Answered by:** [Foucault](michel-foucault.md), [Derrida](jacques-derrida.md), Alain Badiou, later feminist and "new materialist" philosophy

## Biography

Gilles Deleuze was born in Paris on 18 January 1925 to a conservative middle-class family, and apart from a year in Normandy during the German occupation he lived in the same part of Paris all his life. His older brother was arrested by the Nazis for resistance work and died on the train to Auschwitz. Deleuze dated his turn to literature and philosophy to a teacher he met in Deauville in 1940–41, Pierre Halbwachs. After the Liberation he studied at the Lycée Henri IV and then the history of philosophy at the Sorbonne, under Jean Hyppolite, Ferdinand Alquié, Georges Canguilhem and Maurice de Gandillac. Like his generation he also read Sartre closely.

His first book, *Empiricism and Subjectivity* (1953), was on Hume. That was a provocation: his peers at the École Normale Supérieure, Foucault and Derrida among them, were reading "the three H's", Hegel, Husserl and Heidegger. Deleuze then published little for nearly a decade, teaching in provincial lycées and universities, and he later called this period a hole in his life. In these years he also caught the respiratory illness that troubled him for the rest of his life.

*Nietzsche and Philosophy* (1962) made his name. Studies of Kant (1963), Proust (1964) and Bergson (1966) followed. In 1968 he defended *Difference and Repetition* as his main doctoral thesis, with a study of Spinoza as the secondary thesis. In 1969 he took up the post at Vincennes, published *Logic of Sense*, and met Félix Guattari (died 1992), a radical psychoanalyst and political militant. Their *Anti-Oedipus* (1972) became a bestseller and a scandal. In the 1970s Deleuze joined Foucault's Prison Information Group (GIP) and supported gay rights and the Palestinian cause. The two men later grew apart, but Deleuze wrote a book on Foucault after his death (1986). He disliked conferences and travel, and he held that thinking happens in writing rather than in debate. His seminars at Vincennes, though, were famous, open to anyone, and many were recorded. Married from 1956 to Fanny (Denise Paul) Grandjouan, a translator of D. H. Lawrence, he had two children. The 1980s brought solo books on Francis Bacon, cinema, Foucault and Leibniz; *What Is Philosophy?* (1991) was his last book with Guattari. By then his lung disease made writing hard. He published one last short essay, "Immanence: A Life", in 1995, and took his own life in Paris on 4 November 1995.

## Major theories and concepts

### Readings of other philosophers

Before writing "in his own name", Deleuze wrote monographs on figures he treated as allies against what he saw as the main line of philosophy, which runs through Plato, Descartes, Kant and Hegel and is organized around identity. He described the history of philosophy as a kind of portraiture: a likeness, but one the portraitist has to produce.

- **Hume.** Deleuze reads Hume as asking how a mind, at first a mere collection of impressions, becomes a subject through habit and association. The subject is produced, not given. Kant asked how the given is given to a subject; Hume asked how a subject is formed in the given.
- **Nietzsche.** *Nietzsche and Philosophy* reads Nietzsche against Hegel. Reality is made of forces, active or reactive, and the will to power is the principle that decides their relations. Genealogy asks what kind of force a value expresses. The eternal return is read as a test that only the different, never the same, comes back.
- **Bergson.** From Bergson Deleuze takes the idea that there are two kinds of multiplicity, one of separate units in space and one of continuous change in duration, and a distinction between the virtual and the possible (below).
- **Spinoza.** Deleuze called Spinoza the "prince" of philosophers. He takes from him **univocity**, the idea that being is said in one and the same sense of everything that is (a term from Duns Scotus; compare the [Scotus entry](../02-medieval/john-duns-scotus.md)), and a definition of a body by what it can do: its power to affect and be affected.

### Difference in itself, against representation

*Difference and Repetition* is his main book. Normally we think of difference as a relation between two things that already have their own identity: x differs from y. Deleuze reverses the order. Identities are produced by prior differences, as a flash of lightning is produced by a difference in electrical potential between cloud and ground. He calls this **difference in itself**.

His target is **representation**, the way of thinking that only lets difference in when it is tied to identity: to a concept, an analogy, an opposition or a resemblance. A difference in representation is always a difference between items of a kind we already recognize. In chapter 3 he lists the assumptions of what he calls the "dogmatic image of thought", such as that thinking naturally aims at truth and that its model is recognition ("that is a table"). Against it, he says real thought begins only in an encounter with something we cannot recognize, which forces us to think. **Repetition** is treated the same way. True repetition is not the recurrence of the same item but the return of difference, which he links to Nietzsche's eternal return.

### The virtual and the actual

To explain how differences produce things, Deleuze uses a pair of terms from Bergson. The **possible** is usually pictured as a ready-made copy of the real, waiting to be realized; Deleuze thinks this explains nothing, because it simply projects the finished thing backwards. The **virtual**, by contrast, is real but not actual. It is a field of differential relations and "singular points", the thresholds at which a system changes its behaviour. Things become actual by **actualization**, a process that never resembles the virtual it comes from.

A hurricane is a standard example in the secondary literature. There is no central command and no blueprint; differences of temperature and pressure in air and water reach thresholds and organize themselves. The structure of the hurricane's possible behaviour is the virtual; the storm on the map is the actual. Deleuze drew such models from calculus, thermodynamics and embryology. Whether he used these sciences as genuine sources or as suggestive images is one of the debates below.

### Sense and the event: Logic of Sense

*Logic of Sense* (1969) combines the Stoics with Lewis Carroll. A proposition, Deleuze argues, does more than refer to things, express a speaker and signify concepts; it also expresses a **sense**, which is neither a thing nor a mental item. Following the Stoics ([Zeno of Citium and the Stoics](../01-ancient/zeno-of-citium-and-the-stoics.md)), he treats sense as an incorporeal **event**. In a battle, bodies and weapons collide, but "the battle" is an effect hovering over those mixtures, which we name in language. Sense sits on the surface between language and bodies. Carroll's paradoxes of nonsense are used to show how this surface works.

### Capitalism and Schizophrenia: desiring-production

*Anti-Oedipus* (1972), the first volume of *Capitalism and Schizophrenia*, attacks psychoanalysis, the Oedipus complex in particular. For Deleuze and Guattari, desire is not lack, not a longing for a missing object, and not a family drama. It is **desiring-production**: a productive, social process that makes reality by connecting flows (of food, money, words, labour). Subjects are a product of these connections, not their source. They call their alternative to psychoanalysis "schizoanalysis". They tell a "universal history" of how societies capture these flows. Tribal societies code them onto the earth; empires overcode them onto the body of the despot; capitalism decodes them, freeing labour and money, but then recaptures them through private property and by folding desire back into the family triangle of mummy, daddy and me. Their use of schizophrenia names a process, not a lifestyle; they say the clinical schizophrenic is someone whose process has broken down.

### A Thousand Plateaus: rhizome and deterritorialization

*A Thousand Plateaus* (1980) is built as a set of "plateaus" meant to be read in any order. Its opening plateau contrasts two images of thought. A **tree** has a root, a trunk and branches: a hierarchy with one origin, like a family tree or a classification. A **rhizome**, like crabgrass or a ginger root, has no centre, and any point can connect to any other. The book itself is meant to be a rhizome. Its key process terms are **territorialization**, **deterritorialization** and **reterritorialization**. A territory is a stable arrangement, such as a bird's song marking its patch, a set of habits or a national labour market. To deterritorialize is to break such an arrangement open, as capitalism does when it tears workers from the land; reterritorialization is the new arrangement that captures the freed flows. Other concepts in the book include the **assemblage** (a working arrangement of heterogeneous parts), the "body without organs" (roughly, a body's reservoir of unformed potentials), "becoming", the "war machine" and the contrast of "smooth" and "striated" space.

### Cinema 1 and 2

*Cinema 1: The Movement-Image* (1983) and *Cinema 2: The Time-Image* (1985) read film as doing philosophy with images. Deleuze draws on Bergson's *Matter and Memory* and on Peirce's classification of signs. In classical cinema, the **movement-image** links perception to action, and time is shown indirectly through movement. After the Second World War, in Italian neorealism and the French New Wave, this sensory-motor link breaks: characters see situations they cannot act on, and the **time-image** shows time directly. Deleuze said these were books of philosophy, not film criticism.

### The societies of control

The late essay "Postscript on the Societies of Control" (1990) extends Foucault. Foucault had described **disciplinary societies** (18th to early 20th century) that shape individuals by moving them from one enclosure to the next: family, school, barracks, factory, hospital, prison. Deleuze argues these are in crisis and are being replaced by **societies of control**, a name he takes from William Burroughs. Control works through continuous modulation instead of enclosure: lifelong training instead of school, the corporation and the bonus instead of the factory and the wage, passwords and codes that grant or deny access, and debt. Individuals become "dividuals", bundles of data. The essay, five pages long, is now a standard reference in surveillance and digital media studies.

### What Is Philosophy?

In their last book, Deleuze and Guattari answer the title question directly: philosophy creates concepts. A concept is not a general idea but a construction with several components, built in response to a problem, laid out on a "plane of immanence" and voiced by "conceptual personae", such as Descartes's doubter or Nietzsche's Zarathustra. Science, by contrast, creates functions, and art creates blocs of sensation, compounds of what they call percepts and affects. None of the three serves the others. They also reject the idea that philosophy is mainly contemplation, reflection or communication.

## Key works

- *Empiricism and Subjectivity* (1953), *Nietzsche and Philosophy* (1962), *Bergsonism* (1966) — Hume and how a subject is formed; Nietzsche's forces and eternal return read against Hegel; duration, multiplicity and the virtual.
- *Difference and Repetition* (1968) — The main work: difference in itself, the image of thought, the virtual.
- *Expressionism in Philosophy: Spinoza* (1968) and *Spinoza: Practical Philosophy* (1970; enlarged 1981) — Univocity, expression and an ethics of power.
- *Logic of Sense* (1969) — Sense, events, the Stoics and Lewis Carroll.
- With Guattari, *Anti-Oedipus* (1972) and *A Thousand Plateaus* (1980) — *Capitalism and Schizophrenia*: desire, history, rhizome, deterritorialization.
- *Cinema 1* (1983) and *Cinema 2* (1985) — The movement-image and the time-image.
- *Foucault* (1986), *The Fold: Leibniz and the Baroque* (1988), and "Postscript on the Societies of Control" (1990), collected in *Negotiations* (1990).
- With Guattari, *What Is Philosophy?* (1991) — Philosophy, science and art compared.

## Contributions

Rejecting Heidegger's notion of the end of metaphysics (SEP), Deleuze built a metaphysics in which difference, process and event come before identity, substance and essence. He gave the distinction between the virtual and the possible a central role in ontology, and he rebuilt a minor tradition, Lucretius, Spinoza, Hume, Nietzsche and Bergson, as an alternative history of philosophy. With Guattari he produced a theory of desire as social production and a vocabulary (rhizome, assemblage, deterritorialization) that spread far outside philosophy. He also wrote the most ambitious philosophy of film of the century, and named the "society of control".

## Impact and legacy

Foucault's essay "Theatrum Philosophicum" (1970) was an early, admiring review of *Difference and Repetition* and *Logic of Sense*. Deleuze's English reception came first through literary and cultural studies, then architecture, geography, film studies and art theory, where "rhizome" and "assemblage" became standard words. Manuel DeLanda and Brian Massumi linked his ontology to complexity and dynamical-systems theory; Rosi Braidotti, Elizabeth Grosz and others built a "corporeal" feminism on his account of bodies. The "new materialism" and assemblage theory in the social sciences draw heavily on *A Thousand Plateaus*. The "Postscript" became a founding text for thinking about surveillance, platforms and data. Since the 2000s philosophers have also read *Difference and Repetition* as serious metaphysics, comparable with process thought ([Whitehead](alfred-north-whitehead.md)). His relation to [Derrida](jacques-derrida.md) was cordial but distant: both attack the priority of identity, but Deleuze builds a positive metaphysics, while Derrida works by reading texts against themselves.

## Criticisms and debates

- **Badiou's reading.** In *Deleuze: The Clamor of Being* (1997), Alain Badiou argued that Deleuze, against his reputation, is a philosopher of the One rather than of the multiple, and that his vitalism falls short of the rigour Badiou demands, which for him comes from mathematical set theory. Defenders such as Daniel W. Smith reply that Deleuze favours a "problematic" mathematics (the calculus of problems and solutions) over Badiou's axiomatic one. Peter Hallward (2005) offered a related reading of Deleuze as a thinker of creation who points away from the actual world.
- **Obscurity and the use of science.** Many readers, sympathetic or not, find the books hard, full of coined terms and borrowed technical vocabulary. Alan Sokal and Jean Bricmont's *Intellectual Impostures* (1997; English *Fashionable Nonsense*, 1998) accused Deleuze and Guattari of using mathematical and physical terms without understanding them. Defenders reply that Deleuze knew the history of the calculus well and was building philosophical concepts from it, not doing mathematics; the dispute is live.
- **Feminist critiques.** Alice Jardine (1985) and [Luce Irigaray](feminist-philosophy-after-beauvoir.md) objected that "becoming-woman" in *A Thousand Plateaus* uses women as a figure of change for everyone and undercuts women's hard-won claim to be subjects. Later feminists, such as Braidotti and Grosz, have instead found the work useful.
- **Politics.** Slavoj Žižek (2003) argued that the Guattari collaborations betray the better Deleuze of *Logic of Sense*. Others worry that a philosophy celebrating flows and deterritorialization sits too comfortably with deregulated capitalism; defenders point to the "Postscript", which treats the new flexible order as a danger.
- **History of philosophy as portraiture.** Specialists in Hume, Spinoza and Bergson often find his readings creative but unreliable. Deleuze did not claim neutral scholarship: on his own account, a portrait has to be produced, not copied.

## Misreadings to avoid

- **"Deleuze praises schizophrenia."** He and Guattari treat the clinical schizophrenic as ill and needing help; "schizo" names a process of desire, not a model to imitate.
- **"The virtual means the unreal or the digital."** For Deleuze the virtual is fully real; it is opposed to the actual, not to the real.
- **"Rhizome means anything goes."** The rhizome is a model of connection without a centre, set against tree-like hierarchies; the book still argues.
- **"Deleuze is a postmodern relativist."** He calls himself a metaphysician and builds a systematic ontology.

## What to read first

1. "Postscript on the Societies of Control" (1990), five pages, in *Negotiations*.
2. *Negotiations* (1990), the interviews, for his own account of his books.
3. *Nietzsche and Philosophy* (1962), chapters 1–2, or *Spinoza: Practical Philosophy*, chapter 2.
4. *A Thousand Plateaus*, plateau 1, "Introduction: Rhizome".
5. *What Is Philosophy?*, Introduction and chapter 1.
6. *Difference and Repetition*, chapter 3, "The Image of Thought", with a guide such as Smith and Protevi's SEP entry.

## Study questions

1. What does it mean to say identity is produced by difference rather than the other way round? Use the lightning example.
2. How does Deleuze's "virtual" differ from the "possible"? Why does he think the possible explains nothing?
3. Deleuze reads Hume as asking how a subject is formed. How does that change the question Kant asked?
4. Is desire lack or production? What would follow, for politics, if Deleuze and Guattari are right?
5. Compare Foucault's disciplinary societies with Deleuze's societies of control. Which describes your own school or workplace better?
6. Is Badiou right that Deleuze is really a philosopher of the One? What would Deleuze's univocity of being suggest?
