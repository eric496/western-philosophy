## At a glance

- **Dates:** U. T. Place (1924–2000) · J. J. C. Smart (1920–2012) · Hilary Putnam (1926–2016) · John Searle (1932–2025) · Thomas Nagel (b. 1937) · Daniel Dennett (1942–2024) · David Chalmers (b. 1966)
- **Place:** Adelaide, Leeds, Harvard, Berkeley, New York, Tufts
- **School/tradition:** Analytic philosophy of mind; physicalism and its critics
- **Known for:** the moves that replaced Ryle's dissolution of the mind–body problem — the mind/brain identity theory, functionalism and multiple realizability, the Chinese Room, "What is it like to be a bat?", the hard problem of consciousness, and Dennett's deflationary naturalism

**Responds to:** [Ryle](gilbert-ryle.md), [Descartes](../03-early-modern/rene-descartes.md), [Wittgenstein (late)](wittgenstein-late.md) · **Answered by:** no single later entry in this repo — the debate these figures opened is still open

**Why they are grouped here.** These seven formed no school; several spent careers attacking one another. They belong in one entry because they are consecutive moves in a single argument. [Ryle's](gilbert-ryle.md) *The Concept of Mind* (1949) had declared [Descartes's](../03-early-modern/rene-descartes.md) inner theater a category mistake: mind-talk concerns abilities and tendencies, not a ghost steering a machine. That left the question Ryle had not answered. If the mind is not a second thing, what is it? Each figure below is one answer, or one objection to the answer before it.

## Biography

**Ullin Thomas Place (1924–2000)** and **John Jamieson Carswell "Jack" Smart (1920–2012)** met in Australia. Smart, a Scot whose father was an eminent astronomer, had studied at Oxford, where Ryle's behaviorist approach to mind made a deep impression. In 1950, strikingly young for the post, he became Professor of Philosophy at Adelaide, split psychology off from his department, and appointed Place to head the new unit. The two spent years arguing over their dissatisfaction with behaviorism, looking — in the phrase of Graham Nerlich, one of Smart's first Adelaide students — for a mind that was inner without being spooky. Place published first, in a psychology journal, in 1956; Smart's version for philosophers followed in 1959, and Smart always credited Place as the originator. The view came to be known affectionately as the Australian heresy. Place spent his later career in clinical practice and teaching, mainly at Leeds, and bequeathed his brain to the University of Adelaide, where it is displayed with the label *Did this Brain Contain the Consciousness of U. T. Place?*

**Hilary Whitehall Putnam (1926–2016)** was the century's most conspicuous changer of mind. He joined Harvard in 1965 and ended as Cogan University Professor there, inventing the dominant theory of mind of his generation and then becoming its most damaging critic. He died in Arlington, Massachusetts, aged 89.

**Thomas Nagel (b. 4 July 1937, Belgrade)** became a United States citizen in 1944 and studied at Cornell, Oxford and Harvard, taking his Ph.D. in 1963. After teaching at Berkeley (1963–66) and Princeton (1966–80), he moved to New York University as University Professor and Professor of both Philosophy and Law, and won the Balzan Prize in moral philosophy in 2008.

**John Rogers Searle (1932–2025)** was born in Denver and took his Oxford doctorate as a Rhodes Scholar under J. L. Austin and Peter Strawson, whose interest in what speech *does* shaped his own speech-act theory. He joined the University of California, Berkeley in 1959 and stayed for the rest of his career as Slusser Professor of Philosophy of Mind and Language. In 2019, after allegations first raised in a 2017 lawsuit, Berkeley found he had violated its sexual-harassment policies, revoked his emeritus status and ended his teaching; his legacy is accordingly discussed as a divided one.

**Daniel Clement Dennett III (1942–2024)** was born in Boston to a diplomat and a professor and spent part of his childhood in Beirut. He took his B.A. at Harvard in 1963, studying with [Quine](w-v-o-quine.md), and his D.Phil. at Oxford in 1965 under Ryle himself, with a thesis he developed into his 1969 book *Content and Consciousness* — which makes him the one figure here who received the problem from the hands that posed it. After six years at UC Irvine he came to Tufts in 1971 and stayed more than fifty years, directing its Center for Cognitive Studies from its founding in 1985, and published 21 books and over 560 articles.

**David John Chalmers (b. 20 April 1966, Sydney)** studied mathematics at Adelaide and reached Oxford in 1987, by which point, in his own account, his obsession with consciousness had got out of hand. He took a Ph.D. at Indiana in 1993 in Douglas Hofstadter's research center, and is now Professor of Philosophy at New York University, co-directing its Center for Mind, Brain, and Consciousness.

## Major theories and concepts

### The mind/brain identity theory

The claim is blunt: conscious experiences *are* brain processes, identical with them rather than merely correlated. Place offered this in 1956 as a scientific hypothesis, not a conceptual truth. The obvious objection is that "sensation" does not *mean* "brain process," so the two cannot be the same. Place's reply is that this proves nothing: "lightning" does not mean "motion of electric charges," yet the pair picks out one thing. As Place put it in 1956, the claim that consciousness is a process in the brain "is neither self-contradictory nor self-evident; it is a reasonable scientific hypothesis, in the way that the statement, 'Lightning is a motion of electric charges,' is a reasonable scientific hypothesis." Smart and Herbert Feigl recast the point as the distinction between meaning and reference.

The positive case is economy. Feigl's name for the dualist's mind–body correlations was *nomological danglers*: laws hanging off the net of physical science, explaining nothing. Smart added Ockham's Razor and the *topic-neutral* analysis, a term he adapted from Ryle. To report a sensation as something going on in me like what goes on when I really see an orange in good light describes a state without saying what it is made of, leaving the identity for science to settle. The tension is immediate: if the report is genuinely topic-neutral, has anything been explained or only relabeled? Place answered with his charge of the *phenomenological fallacy*, treating a mental image as a ghostly picture postcard that must be located somewhere; critics have never agreed the charge settles the matter.

### Functionalism and multiple realizability

Putnam introduced multiple realizability into philosophy of mind, and with it the objection that broke the identity theory in its strongest form. Challenging the "brain state theorists" in 1967, he noted how many creatures look capable of pain: primates, other mammals, birds, reptiles, amphibians, even mollusks such as the octopus. For the brain-state theory to hold, one physical-chemical kind would have to be common to all of them and exactly correlated with each occurrence of pain. But neuroanatomy differs sharply across these groups, and convergent evolution routinely builds the same function from unrelated structures. The hypothesis was, Putnam granted, ambitious — and he thought it almost certainly false. Whatever is multiply realizable is not identical to any one of its realizers.

Putnam's replacement was *functionalism*: a mental state is defined by its place in a pattern of causes and effects, not by its material. Pain is the state typically caused by tissue damage that in turn produces the belief that one is in pain, the desire to be rid of it, and behavior such as crying out or nursing the injury. Whatever occupies that role is pain — neurons, silicon, or the stock philosopher's green slime. Ned Block and Jerry Fodor noted that ordinary functional kinds behave the same way: mousetraps and valve lifters are multiply realizable, and nobody thinks a valve lifter is a kind of metal. The deepest objection was Putnam's own: in *Representation and Reality* (1988) he argued that mental kinds are also *computationally* plastic, since two people can share a belief without being in the same computational state. Defining mental states by computational role therefore fails for the reason brain-state theory failed.

### Searle's Chinese Room

Searle's 1980 paper argued that running the right program could never suffice for understanding. In his own later summary: a native English speaker who knows no Chinese is shut in a room with boxes of Chinese symbols (the data base) and a book of instructions for manipulating them (the program). Questions in Chinese come in; following the instructions, he passes out correct Chinese answers. The system passes the Turing Test, "but he does not understand a word of Chinese." A computer running a program has nothing the man lacks, so no computer understands Chinese merely by running one.

The target is narrower than the fame suggests. Searle attacks *Strong AI*, the claim that a suitably programmed computer thereby understands, not *weak AI*, the modest claim that computers are useful tools for psychology and linguistics; and he does not hold that machines cannot think — brains are machines, he says, and brains think. What he denies is that formal symbol manipulation is enough: computation is defined purely syntactically while minds have semantic content, "and we cannot get from syntactical to the semantic just by having the syntactical operations and nothing else." [Leibniz's](../03-early-modern/gottfried-wilhelm-leibniz.md) Mill, in §17 of the *Monadology*, is the ancestor — walk inside a thinking machine and find only parts pushing parts.

The Systems Reply is the standard objection, and Searle's answer is where the argument stands or falls. The reply grants that the man does not understand Chinese but notes that he is only the processor in a larger system, and that the *system* understands. Searle's response is to internalize everything: memorize the program and database, do it in his head, walk out of the room. He is now the whole system and still would not know the Chinese word for hamburger. Critics including Margaret Boden answer that this is itself a category mistake in Ryle's sense, treating the implementer as the bearer of intelligence rather than its causal basis.

### Nagel: what it is like to be a bat

Nagel's 1974 paper opens by naming the obstacle: "Consciousness is what makes the mind-body problem really intractable." His criterion is deliberately minimal. An organism has conscious mental states if and only if there is something it is like to be that organism — something it is like *for* the organism. He calls this the *subjective character of experience*, and notes at once that it is not analyzable in terms of functional or intentional states, since those could be ascribed to robots that behaved exactly like people.

The bat is the example because it is close enough to be plainly conscious and strange enough to defeat imagination. Microchiroptera perceive mostly by echolocation, detecting reflections of their own rapid, subtly modulated, high-frequency shrieks, which supports discriminations as fine as ours by vision. We know all this objectively, yet bat sonar resembles no sense we have, and there is no reason to think it is subjectively like anything we can imagine. Nagel tries the obvious move and rejects it: imagining webbed arms, poor eyesight, insects caught in the mouth and days hanging upside down "tells me only what it would be like for me to behave as a bat behaves. But that is not the question. I want to know what it is like for a bat to be a bat."

Behind the example is a collision of standpoints. Physical facts are objective facts par excellence, observable from many points of view; facts about experience are available from one point of view only, so a method that abstracts away from every standpoint cannot capture them. The conclusion is modest: Nagel does not argue that physicalism is false, only that we have no present understanding of how it could be true. Frank Jackson pushed the intuition further in 1982 with the case of Mary, who learns all the physics of color vision inside a black-and-white room and then sees red — and who, Jackson concluded, learns something new, so physicalism is false.

### Chalmers and the hard problem

Chalmers's contribution is partly a piece of terminology that reorganized a field. In 1995 he separated the *easy problems* from the *hard problem*. The easy problems are what standard cognitive science can attack: discriminating and reacting to stimuli, integrating information, reporting mental states, attention, the difference between waking and sleep. "Easy" is relative — getting the details right, he says, will probably take a century or two of difficult empirical work — but we know what an answer would look like.

"The really hard problem of consciousness is the problem of experience." Explaining the mechanism that makes information available for report tells you nothing about why a felt quality of deep blue, or of middle C, accompanies the processing. "Why should physical processing give rise to a rich inner life at all? It seems objectively unreasonable that it should, and yet it does."

His positive proposal, in *The Conscious Mind* (1996), is to treat conscious experience as a fundamental feature of the world and look for fundamental laws linking it to physical processes, built on principles of structural coherence and organizational invariance. The *zombie* thought experiment does much of the argumentative work: conceive a being physically identical to you with no experience at all, and if that is genuinely conceivable, experience is not fixed by the physical facts. Organizational invariance has a consequence Chalmers accepts cheerfully — a silicon system with the right organization would be conscious, so the book defends a form of strong artificial intelligence while denying materialism. The internal tension is every dualism's: if experience is fundamental and distinct, what work does it do? Critics also press whether conceivability shows anything about possibility, and Robert Kirk has argued that a creature with all our behavioral and neural properties would be conscious, so the zombie is incoherent.

### Dennett: the intentional stance, heterophenomenology, and multiple drafts

Dennett spent fifty years arguing that the residue Nagel, Chalmers and Searle point to is an artifact of how we are looking.

The *intentional stance* is the first move. Ascribing beliefs and desires is adopting a predictive strategy, not reporting inner items. Treat a chess program, a thermostat or a person as a rational agent pursuing goals given its information, and you predict behavior quickly and well — which is the whole point and the whole justification. Such ascriptions are, on his account, irredeemably indeterminate: they pick out real patterns, but patterns no more real than numbers or a center of gravity. Beliefs are thus neither fictions nor inner sentences.

*Heterophenomenology* is the method of *Consciousness Explained* (1991): take people's reports about their own experience entirely seriously as data to be explained rather than as evidence about an inner realm. Explain why a subject sincerely says what they say, without granting in advance that the saying reports anything accurately.

The *multiple drafts model* is the positive theory. There is no place in the brain where it all comes together for a show, no *Cartesian theater* with one audience and one moment at which a content becomes conscious; there are many parallel processes fixing content at various places and times, revising and overwriting each other, with no privileged edition. The demonstrations come from timing anomalies in perception, where asking what the subject *really* experienced, and when, has no determinate answer — evidence, for Dennett, that the question presupposes a theater that is not there. He draws the conclusion in a chapter titled "Qualia Disqualified," and later defended outright illusionism: qualia, he wrote in 2020, are like Santa Claus and the Easter Bunny. Hence the objection his opponents never let go — a theory that explains why you are convinced you have experiences, then declines to explain them, can look like changing the subject.

## Key works

- Place, "Is Consciousness a Brain Process?" (1956), *British Journal of Psychology* 47: 44–50 — the founding paper, and still the clearest statement of why the identity claim is empirical.
- Feigl, "The 'Mental' and the 'Physical'" (1958) — the companion piece from a member of the [Vienna Circle](vienna-circle-logical-positivism.md); source of "nomological danglers."
- Smart, "Sensations and Brain Processes" (1959), *The Philosophical Review* 68(2) — the version for philosophers.
- Putnam, "Psychological Predicates" (1967), in Capitan and Merrill, eds., *Art, Mind, and Religion*, 37–48 — reprinted as "The Nature of Mental States."
- Nagel, "What Is It Like to Be a Bat?" (1974), *The Philosophical Review* 83(4): 435–450 — sixteen pages that set the agenda for everything after.
- Searle, "Minds, Brains, and Programs" (1980), *The Behavioral and Brain Sciences* — the Chinese Room, printed with 27 commentaries and Searle's replies; restated in *Minds, Brains and Science* (1984).
- Jackson, "Epiphenomenal Qualia" (1982) — the knowledge argument in canonical form.
- Putnam, *Representation and Reality* (1988) — the author's own case against functionalism.
- Dennett, *The Intentional Stance* (1987) and *Consciousness Explained* (1991) — the method, then the theory.
- Chalmers, "Facing Up to the Problem of Consciousness" (1995), *Journal of Consciousness Studies* 2(3): 200–219, and *The Conscious Mind* (1996).

## Contributions

Place and Smart made materialism about the mind an empirical hypothesis rather than a conceptual thesis, which is why the identity theory, unlike behaviorism, could be refuted by evidence. Putnam gave psychology a principled reason to be a science in its own right rather than a branch of neurology awaiting absorption. Searle produced the strongest argument that computation is not comprehension, when few in artificial intelligence thought the distinction mattered. Nagel and Chalmers established subjective experience as a datum any theory must answer to. Dennett built the fullest naturalistic alternative, and taught philosophers to argue with experimental results in hand.

## Impact and legacy

The field as now taught is organized around these moves. Functionalism became the default working assumption of cognitive science, and multiple realizability remains the standard argument against reducing psychology to neuroscience. Chalmers's distinction reshaped consciousness studies, and the Association for the Scientific Study of Consciousness, which he helped found, exists partly because of it. Searle's Chinese Room became the most widely discussed philosophical argument in cognitive science since the Turing Test — by 1991 the computer scientist Pat Hayes could define cognitive science, half-jokingly, as the ongoing project of refuting it — and it is argued over again whenever a language model produces fluent text. A January 1990 *Scientific American* exchange, where Searle's piece ran beside a reply from Paul and Patricia Churchland, carried the dispute to a general audience.

## Criticisms and debates

These are live disputes, not settled scores. Against the identity theory: does the topic-neutral analysis explain experience or merely postpone the question, and did Kripke's argument that true identities hold necessarily undermine the contingent identity the Australians wanted? Against functionalism: a system with the right causal roles and no inner life — Block's China-sized network of people passing signals in the firing pattern of a brain in pain — comes out conscious, which many take as a reductio. Against the Chinese Room: the Systems, Robot, Brain Simulator and Virtual Mind replies all contend that Searle locates understanding at the wrong level. Against Nagel and Chalmers: that conceivability is a weak guide to possibility, and that an unexplainable residue is what you would expect from a brain modeling itself rather than evidence of a new fundamental property. Against Dennett: that he explains the reports and not the experience.

## Misreadings to avoid

- The identity theory is not behaviorism with brains. Place and Smart were reacting *against* Ryle's treatment of inner experience, which they found inadequate.
- Multiple realizability does not show the mind is non-physical. It shows mental kinds do not line up with physical kinds, which is why most functionalists remain physicalists.
- Searle does not argue that machines cannot think. He argues that running a program is not sufficient, and holds that brains are thinking machines.
- Nagel's paper is not an argument that materialism is false. It argues that we do not yet understand how it could be true.
- Chalmers's hard problem is not a claim that consciousness is unexplainable, and his position is compatible with conscious computers.
- Dennett does not deny that people have experiences in the ordinary sense. He denies a particular philosophical account of what experience consists in — a narrower claim than the slogans suggest.

## What to read first

1. Nagel, "What Is It Like to Be a Bat?" — short, non-technical, the hinge of the whole debate.
2. Smart, "Sensations and Brain Processes," then Putnam's "Psychological Predicates" — the rise and fall of the identity theory in about forty pages.
3. Searle, "Minds, Brains, and Programs" — the argument, then two of the commentaries printed with it and Searle's answers.
4. Chalmers, "Facing Up to the Problem of Consciousness" — the clearest statement of the modern problem.
5. Dennett, *Consciousness Explained*, the chapters "A Method for Phenomenology" and "Multiple Drafts versus the Cartesian Theater" — read last, as the reply to everything above.
