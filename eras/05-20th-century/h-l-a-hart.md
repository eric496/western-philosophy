## At a glance

- **Dates:** 1907–1992
- **Place:** Born in Harrogate, Yorkshire; a barrister in London; Professor of Jurisprudence at Oxford (1952–1968)
- **School/tradition:** Analytic philosophy of law; legal positivism; Oxford ordinary language philosophy
- **Known for:** *The Concept of Law* (1961): law as a union of primary and secondary rules resting on a social "rule of recognition", and the defence of the separation of law and morals against Fuller, Devlin and Dworkin

**Responds to:** [Hobbes](../03-early-modern/thomas-hobbes.md), [Bentham](../04-19th-century/jeremy-bentham.md), [Mill](../04-19th-century/john-stuart-mill.md), [J. L. Austin](austin-and-strawson.md), [Wittgenstein](wittgenstein-late.md) · **Answered by:** [Rawls](john-rawls.md), Ronald Dworkin, Joseph Raz, John Finnis

A warning about names before anything else. The "Austin" whose theory of law Hart demolishes is the jurist **John Austin (1790–1859)**, Bentham's follower and author of *The Province of Jurisprudence Determined* (1832). He is not **J. L. Austin (1911–1960)**, the Oxford philosopher of language and Hart's friend and colleague, whose methods Hart borrowed. Hart criticised the first Austin with the tools of the second.

## Biography

Herbert Lionel Adolphus Hart was born on 18 July 1907 in Harrogate, the son of Jewish parents who ran a tailoring and dressmaking business. He studied classics and philosophy ("Greats") at New College, Oxford, taking a First in 1929, then qualified as a barrister and practised at the Chancery bar in London through the 1930s. In 1941 he married Jenifer Williams, a civil servant and later an Oxford historian. During the Second World War he worked for MI5, the British security service, where he renewed Oxford friendships with philosophers such as Gilbert Ryle and Stuart Hampshire.

After the war he did not go back to the bar. He took a philosophy fellowship at New College and joined the circle around [J. L. Austin](austin-and-strawson.md), whose patient attention to how words are actually used is called **ordinary language philosophy**. From 1948 Hart and Austin taught a joint seminar on legal and moral responsibility. Hart also absorbed the later [Wittgenstein](wittgenstein-late.md)'s idea that following a rule is a social practice, and [Ryle](gilbert-ryle.md)'s suspicion of hidden mental or metaphysical entities.

In 1952, having published only a few essays, he was elected Professor of Jurisprudence at Oxford. His inaugural lecture, *Definition and Theory in Jurisprudence* (1953), argued that legal words like "right" or "corporation" should be explained by the sentences they work in, not by hunting for the thing they name. In 1957 he gave the Holmes Lecture at Harvard, "Positivism and the Separation of Law and Morals" (published 1958), which provoked a reply from Lon Fuller and began the **Hart-Fuller debate**. *Causation in the Law*, written with Tony Honoré, appeared in 1959 and *The Concept of Law* in 1961. Lectures at Stanford became *Law, Liberty and Morality* (1963), his side of the **Hart-Devlin debate**.

Hart resigned the chair in 1968, and Ronald Dworkin succeeded him in 1969. Hart then worked on the Bentham Project's edition of [Bentham](../04-19th-century/jeremy-bentham.md)'s writings, edited Bentham's *Of Laws in General* (1970), and was Principal of Brasenose College, Oxford, from 1973 to 1978. He spent his last years drafting a reply to Dworkin. He died in Oxford on 19 December 1992. The reply was left unfinished and was published, edited by Penelope Bulloch and Joseph Raz, as the **Postscript** to the second edition of *The Concept of Law* (1994).

Nicola Lacey's *A Life of H. L. A. Hart: The Nightmare and the Noble Dream* (2004), based on his diaries and letters, changed the public picture of him. It shows a man full of self-doubt, troubled by his Jewish identity and his sexuality within a difficult marriage, and devastated in 1983 by a newspaper's insinuation that he had been a spy, which led to a breakdown. Reviewers have debated how far such private material should shape how we read the philosophy.

## Major theories and concepts

### The critique of John Austin's command theory

The jurist John Austin defined law as the **command** of a **sovereign**: an order backed by a threat of punishment, issued by a person or body that is habitually obeyed by most of society and obeys no one else. Hart opens *The Concept of Law* by testing this model against a simple case. A gunman orders a bank clerk to hand over the money or be shot. If Austin is right, law is just this scene enlarged: the "gunman situation writ large" (Hart's phrase, as quoted in the SEP; see below).

Hart argues that the model fails in several ways. The gunman makes us *obliged* to comply, from fear, but we would not say the clerk had an *obligation* to comply; law, by contrast, creates obligations. Many laws are not orders at all: the rules for making a valid will, contract or marriage confer **powers**, and failing to follow them is not punished but simply does not produce a valid result. Laws bind the legislators who make them, which a command to others cannot explain. And the idea of a "habit of obedience" cannot explain why a new monarch's first law is valid on the day of accession, before anyone has acquired a habit of obeying him. What does that is a *rule* specifying who is entitled to succeed.

### Primary and secondary rules

Hart's alternative is to see law as a **union of primary and secondary rules**. **Primary rules** tell people what to do or not do: do not steal, pay your taxes. Hart imagines a simple society governed only by such customary rules, and finds three defects in it. It is *uncertain*, since there is no way to settle what the rules are; *static*, since there is no way to change them deliberately; and *inefficient*, since there is no official way to settle disputes about whether a rule was broken.

Each defect is cured by a kind of **secondary rule**, a rule about the primary rules. **Rules of change** say how rules can be made or repealed (for example by a vote in Parliament). **Rules of adjudication** set up courts and give them power to decide cases. The **rule of recognition** gives the criteria for identifying which rules belong to the system. Hart says this step from a pre-legal to a legal world is the key to understanding law, though he presents it as a thought experiment, not as history.

### The rule of recognition

The rule of recognition is the bedrock of a legal system. In the United Kingdom, roughly, it says that whatever the Queen in Parliament enacts is law. Every other rule is valid because it passes the test this rule sets. But the rule of recognition itself is not valid because some higher rule says so. It simply **exists as a social fact**: it is the rule that judges and other officials actually use, and criticise each other for departing from, when they decide what counts as law. Unlike Kelsen's "basic norm", which must be presupposed, Hart's rule of recognition is an observable practice among officials. Ordinary citizens need only generally obey the valid rules; it is the officials who must accept the rule of recognition.

### The internal point of view

To explain what it is to *accept* a rule, Hart distinguishes two ways of relating to it. From the **external point of view**, an observer just records regularities: drivers usually stop when the light turns red, and people who do not stop are often fined. From the **internal point of view**, participants use the rule as a standard. They say "you ought to have stopped", criticise others who do not, and treat a breach as a *reason* for criticism, not merely a predictor of it. A rule exists, for Hart, when a group's regular behaviour is combined with this critical, reflective attitude. Accepting a rule in this sense need not mean believing it is morally good; an official may accept the rule of recognition from habit, self-interest or loyalty to colleagues.

### Open texture and judicial discretion

Rules are written in general words, and general words have a settled core and a fuzzy edge. Hart called this the **open texture** of law. His famous example (from the 1958 essay) is a rule forbidding vehicles in a public park. A car is clearly covered. But what about a bicycle, roller skates, a toy car, or a disused army truck set up as a war memorial? In such **penumbral** cases the rule's words do not decide the case, and the judge must use **discretion**, in effect making new law with an eye to the rule's aims. Hart presented this as a middle path between "formalism", which pretends rules always decide cases mechanically, and the American "rule-scepticism" which treats rules as mere predictions of what judges will do. He later called these extremes the nightmare and the noble dream of American jurisprudence, the phrase Lacey took for her title.

### Legal positivism and the separation of law and morals

**Legal positivism** is the view that what the law *is* depends on social facts (what has been enacted, decided or customarily accepted), not on whether it is morally good. Hart's formula is that it is in no sense a necessary truth that law reproduces the demands of morality, although it often does (quoted below). This is the separation of the question "what is the law?" from the question "is it good law, and should I obey it?", a distinction he took from Bentham and John Austin.

Hart insisted the separation serves morality. If we say "an evil law is not law", we blur the moral question that matters most. Better to say clearly: this is law, and it is too wicked to be obeyed. He also allowed that any viable legal system must contain a **minimum content of natural law**, basic rules against violence and theft and in favour of keeping promises, given plain facts about human beings such as their vulnerability and limited altruism.

### The Hart-Fuller debate

Lon Fuller of Harvard replied in "Positivism and Fidelity to Law" (1958). The test case was the postwar German "grudge informer" problem: a woman who had denounced her husband to the Nazi authorities was later prosecuted, and a court treated the Nazi statute she relied on as no law because of its gross immorality. Hart argued it would be more honest to admit the statute was law and pass a retrospective law to punish her, openly accepting the moral cost. Fuller answered that law has an **inner morality**: rules must be general, public, prospective, clear, consistent and possible to obey, and a regime that ignores these demands does not produce law at all. Hart replied in *The Concept of Law* that these are principles of efficient rule-making, which a very wicked regime could also follow.

### The Hart-Devlin debate

In 1957 the Wolfenden Report recommended decriminalising homosexual acts between consenting adults in private. In 1959 the judge Patrick Devlin argued that a society may use the criminal law to enforce its shared morality, because that shared morality holds it together; the test was the reaction of the ordinary reasonable person. Hart's *Law, Liberty and Morality* (1963) asked whether the mere immorality of conduct is enough to justify punishing it, and answered no. He defended a modified version of [Mill](../04-19th-century/john-stuart-mill.md)'s harm principle, allowing some legal **paternalism** (protecting people from themselves) but rejecting **legal moralism**, the enforcement of morality as such. He separated **positive morality** (the morality a society actually holds) from **critical morality** (the standards by which any society's morality can be judged), and argued that Devlin's claim that private immorality threatens social survival had no evidence behind it. The debate replayed Mill's argument with James Fitzjames Stephen a century earlier.

### Dworkin and the Postscript

Dworkin's "The Model of Rules" (1967, reprinted in *Taking Rights Seriously*, 1977) argued that judges in hard cases rely not only on rules but on **principles**, such as "no one may profit from their own wrong", which are binding in law because of their moral weight, not because a rule of recognition picked them out. So, Dworkin said, there is no judicial discretion of Hart's kind, and no clean separation of law from morality. In *Law's Empire* (1986) he went further: identifying the law is always an interpretive, moral task.

In the Postscript Hart replied that his theory was descriptive and general, not a justification of any particular legal system. He accepted that a rule of recognition *may* include moral tests, as when a constitution forbids "cruel and unusual punishment", a position he called **soft positivism** and that is now usually called **inclusive positivism**. And he held on to discretion: where the law runs out, judges make law, within limits.

## Key works

- "Positivism and the Separation of Law and Morals" (1958, *Harvard Law Review*) — The Holmes Lecture; defends Bentham's and John Austin's separation of law and morals while rejecting the command theory.
- *Causation in the Law* (1959, with Tony Honoré) — How law and common sense attribute causes and responsibility.
- *The Concept of Law* (1961; 2nd ed. with Postscript 1994; 3rd ed. 2012) — The main work: the critique of John Austin, primary and secondary rules, the rule of recognition, open texture.
- *Law, Liberty and Morality* (1963) — The case against Devlin's legal moralism.
- *Punishment and Responsibility* (1968), *Essays on Bentham* (1982), *Essays in Jurisprudence and Philosophy* (1983) — Collected essays on punishment, Bentham, and the "nightmare and noble dream".

## In their own words

> the "gunman situation writ large"

— Hart's characterisation of John Austin's command model, *The Concept of Law*, ch. 2 (2nd ed. 1994, p. 19, as cited and quoted in the SEP entry "The Nature of Law")

> those rules of behavior which are valid according to the system's ultimate criteria of validity must be generally obeyed, and…its rules of recognition specifying the criteria of legal validity and its rules of change and adjudication must be effectively accepted as common public standards of official behavior by its officials.

— *The Concept of Law*, ch. 6 (3rd ed. 2012, p. 116, as quoted in Leslie Green, SEP "Legal Positivism"). Note the two audiences: the public obeys, the officials accept.

> the simple contention that it is [in] no sense a necessary truth that laws reproduce or satisfy certain demands of morality, though in fact they have often done so.

— *The Concept of Law*, ch. 9 (3rd ed. 2012, pp. 185–186, as quoted in SEP "Legal Positivism", which prints "it is no sense"; the bracketed word is supplied)

> an enormous overvaluation of the importance of the bare fact that a rule may be said to be a valid rule of law, as if this, once declared, was conclusive of the final moral question: "Ought this law to be obeyed?"

— "Positivism and the Separation of Law and Morals" (1958), p. 75 as cited in SEP "Legal Positivism". Hart is describing the danger he finds in theories that fuse law and morals.

> sufficient to justify making that conduct punishable by law?

— The question of *Law, Liberty and Morality* (1963; 1982 printing, p. 4, as quoted in SEP "The Limits of Law"): is the fact that conduct is immoral enough to justify punishing it?

## Contributions

Hart refounded the philosophy of law within analytic philosophy. He replaced the command theory with a rule-based account, introduced the distinction between primary and secondary rules, the rule of recognition and the internal point of view, and gave positivism a non-reductive form: law rests on social facts, but those facts include people's normative attitudes. He also set the modern terms for debating the legal enforcement of morality.

## Impact and legacy

Almost all later legal philosophy in English starts from *The Concept of Law*, whether to build on it or attack it. His students and successors divided his inheritance: Joseph Raz developed **exclusive positivism** (law is fixed by social sources alone), Jules Coleman and Wil Waluchow inclusive positivism, John Finnis a revived natural law theory, and Dworkin interpretivism. Scott Shapiro's planning theory (2011) and much work on the internal point of view still argue within Hart's framework. His exchange with Devlin shaped liberal arguments over the criminal law on sexuality, drugs and paternalism. He influenced the young [John Rawls](john-rawls.md), who studied at Oxford in 1952–53, and Thomas Nagel credits Hart, with Rawls, for starting the analytic tradition of rigorous argument on public moral issues.

## Criticisms and debates

- **Dworkin's challenge.** Do principles enter law by their moral weight, defeating positivism, or by being recognised in official practice, as inclusive positivists say? Exclusive positivists such as Raz think Hart's soft positivism concedes too much. The debate is unresolved.
- **Fuller's inner morality.** Is there a necessary moral element in legality itself? Positivists reply that the rule of law's virtues can serve evil ends, for example a clear and consistently applied system of racial segregation.
- **Is from ought.** Critics ask how the bare fact that officials accept a rule can make it binding. Some (Shapiro) recast law as social planning; others say Hart left the internal point of view underexplained.
- **Descriptive jurisprudence.** Can one describe law neutrally at all, or does choosing what is important already involve moral judgment (Finnis, Dworkin)?
- **Devlin's defenders.** Self-described legal moralists such as Michael Moore and Antony Duff argue that wrongdoing can be a reason to criminalise; critics answer that Devlin's "reasonable person" test ratifies prejudice.

## Misreadings to avoid

- **"Hart attacked J. L. Austin."** He attacked the jurist John Austin (1790–1859). J. L. Austin was his ally.
- **"Positivists think law must always be obeyed."** Hart held the opposite: calling something law settles nothing about whether to obey it.
- **"Positivism says law has nothing to do with morality."** Hart accepted many links, including a minimum content of natural law; he denied only that legal validity *necessarily* depends on moral merit.
- **"Open texture means judges can decide however they like."** Most cases fall in the settled core; discretion arises only at the edges and is bounded by the rule's aims.
- **"Hart against Devlin was a defence of total liberty."** He accepted some paternalism; his target was punishing immorality as such.

## What to read first

1. *The Concept of Law*, chapters 1–6 — the gunman, the critique of John Austin, primary and secondary rules, the rule of recognition.
2. "Positivism and the Separation of Law and Morals" (1958), then Fuller's reply "Positivism and Fidelity to Law" in the same volume of the *Harvard Law Review*.
3. *The Concept of Law*, chapters 7 and 9 — open texture, and law and morals.
4. *Law, Liberty and Morality* — three short lectures; read alongside Mill's *On Liberty*, ch. 1.
5. Dworkin, "The Model of Rules", then Hart's Postscript; finally Nicola Lacey, *A Life of H. L. A. Hart* (2004), for the man and his Oxford.

## Study questions

1. List the ways Hart says the gunman's order differs from a law. Which difference do you think does most damage to John Austin's theory?
2. Why can the rule of recognition not itself be valid in the way ordinary laws are? Is "it exists because officials practise it" a satisfying answer?
3. Take the "no vehicles in the park" rule. Sort ten objects into core and penumbra, and say what a judge should consider in the hard ones.
4. In the grudge informer case, was Hart's plan (admit the Nazi statute was law, then legislate retrospectively) more honest than the court's, or just more awkward?
5. Devlin says a shared morality holds society together. What evidence would Hart demand, and what would count as meeting it?
6. Does accepting "soft positivism" in the Postscript save Hart's theory from Dworkin or give up its core?
