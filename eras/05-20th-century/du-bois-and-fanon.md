## At a glance

- **Dates:** W. E. B. Du Bois (1868–1963) · Frantz Fanon (1925–1961)
- **Place:** Du Bois: Great Barrington (Massachusetts), Harvard, Berlin, Atlanta, New York, Accra · Fanon: Martinique, Lyon, Blida (Algeria), Tunis
- **School/tradition:** Africana philosophy; social science and pragmatism (Du Bois); existential phenomenology, psychiatry and anti-colonial thought (Fanon)
- **Known for:** Du Bois's double consciousness, the veil and the color line; Fanon's account of being fixed by the white gaze, and his theory of decolonization, including its much-disputed treatment of violence

**Responds to:** [Hegel](../04-19th-century/g-w-f-hegel.md), [William James](../04-19th-century/william-james.md), [Sartre](jean-paul-sartre.md), [Merleau-Ponty](maurice-merleau-ponty.md) · **Answered by:** [Arendt](hannah-arendt.md), [Sartre](jean-paul-sartre.md); in dialogue with [Beauvoir](simone-de-beauvoir.md)

**Why they are grouped here.** Du Bois and Fanon never met, wrote on different continents and in different languages, and belong to different generations. They share one question: what does it do to a person's consciousness to be seen, from childhood, as a problem? Du Bois answered from inside American segregation in 1903 with the idea of a divided self. Half a century later Fanon, a psychiatrist trained in France, answered with the tools of phenomenology (the description of experience from the inside) and then carried the question into the politics of empire. Read together, they show how a philosophy of race moved from the self to the colonial world.

## Biography

**William Edward Burghardt Du Bois (1868–1963)** was born on 23 February 1868 in Great Barrington, Massachusetts. After a BA at Fisk University in Nashville, his first experience of the segregated South, he entered Harvard as a junior in 1888 and took philosophy courses in 1889–90 with [William James](../04-19th-century/william-james.md), George Santayana and F. G. Peabody, graduating in philosophy in 1890. A grant took him in 1892 to the Friedrich Wilhelm University in Berlin, where he worked closely with the economist Gustav von Schmoller of the German Historical School, which treated economic life as part of a people's history. Money ran out before he could meet the residency rule for a German doctorate, and in 1895 he became the first Black person to receive a Harvard PhD.

His *Philadelphia Negro* (1899) was a pioneering empirical study of a Black urban community. At Atlanta University he edited the Atlanta University Studies, and in 1903 he published *The Souls of Black Folk*, which included his public break with Booker T. Washington's strategy of accommodation. In 1910 he joined the new NAACP and edited its magazine, *The Crisis*, until 1934. He organized Pan-African congresses from 1919, and wrote history, novels and the autobiography *Dusk of Dawn* (1940). In 1951 he was indicted as an "unregistered foreign agent" for his peace activism and acquitted. In 1961 he accepted Kwame Nkrumah's invitation to Ghana, became a Ghanaian citizen, and died in Accra on 27 August 1963, the eve of the March on Washington.

**Frantz Fanon (1925–1961)** was born on 20 July 1925 in Martinique, then a French colony, into a middle-class family that, like many of its class, aimed at assimilation into French culture. At school he was taught by the poet Aimé Césaire, a founder of *Négritude*, the movement affirming Black and African culture against colonial contempt. In 1943 he left to fight with the Free French forces. After the war he studied medicine and psychiatry in Lyon, where he met a cruder anti-Black racism than the Caribbean's gradations of shade; the anger it caused fed the essay that became *Black Skin, White Masks* (1952). In 1953 he became head of a psychiatric service at the Blida-Joinville hospital in Algeria. The Algerian war of independence began the next year, and Fanon found himself treating both French officers who tortured and the Algerians they tortured. Already secretly aiding the National Liberation Front (FLN), he resigned in 1956, was expelled, and worked for it openly from Tunisia: he trained nurses, edited its paper *El Moudjahid*, wrote for Sartre's journal *Les Temps Modernes*, and served the provisional Algerian government as its ambassador in Ghana. Back in Tunis in late 1960 he learned he had leukemia. He wrote *The Wretched of the Earth* in his last months; it appeared in 1961 with a preface by [Sartre](jean-paul-sartre.md). Fanon died at a US government hospital in Bethesda, Maryland, on 6 December 1961, and was buried in Algeria.

## Major theories and concepts

### Double consciousness and the veil (Du Bois)

*The Souls of Black Folk* opens with a question white acquaintances never quite ask: "How does it feel to be a problem?" Du Bois's answer is a description of a split self. America gives the Black American "no true self-consciousness" but only lets him see himself "through the revelation of the other world." This is **double consciousness**: looking at oneself through the eyes of people who look on with contempt or pity, and so feeling a "twoness," American and Negro, "two warring ideals in one dark body."

The **veil** is his image for the barrier that produces this. He first felt it, he writes, when a white classmate in a New England schoolhouse refused his visiting card "with a glance." The veil separates two worlds, but it also gives "second-sight": those behind it see white America more clearly than it sees itself. The goal Du Bois names is not to choose one side, but to make it possible "to be both a Negro and an American" without being cursed for it. There is a tension inside the idea. Double consciousness is a harm, yet second-sight is a gift; and the hoped-for merger into "a better and truer self" can sound either like healing or like an ideal Du Bois himself doubted America would allow.

### The color line

"The problem of the Twentieth Century is the problem of the color line," Du Bois wrote in the book's forethought, and in chapter II he widened it to "the relation of the darker to the lighter races of men in Asia and Africa, in America and the islands of the sea." The **color line** is the global division of rights, work and dignity by race. The phrase makes racism a structural and international problem, not a matter of individual prejudice alone, which is why Du Bois moved from American civil rights to Pan-Africanism and anti-colonialism.

### What is a race? ("The Conservation of Races", 1897)

In an address to the American Negro Academy, Du Bois asked what a race is, and answered that race is less a biological category than a historical one: a "vast family" held together "always" by common history, traditions and impulses, and striving together toward ideals of life. On this view each race has a distinctive "message" for humanity, and Black Americans had a duty to cultivate theirs rather than dissolve into white America. Whether this definition escapes biology is one of the best-known debates about him (below).

### Whiteness and *Black Reconstruction* (1935)

In *Black Reconstruction in America* Du Bois retold the period after the Civil War as a struggle over Black labor, reading the wartime flight of enslaved workers from the plantations as a "general strike" (chapter 4). He argued that white workers were paid partly in a public and psychological wage of status, which bound them to employers against Black workers. Political theorists such as Joel Olson have built on this to treat whiteness as a social position that buys loyalty, not a skin color.

### The lived experience of the Black (Fanon)

The fifth chapter of *Black Skin, White Masks* is titled "L'expérience vécue du Noir," the lived experience of the Black man. The first English translation (1967) rendered it "The Fact of Blackness," and critics such as David Macey argued this reversed Fanon's point: blackness is not a fact about a body but a way of being lived and made in a racist world. Richard Philcox's later translation restores "lived experience."

The chapter turns on a scene. In Lyon a white child points and says to its mother, "Tiens, un nègre" ("Look, a Negro!"). In that moment, Fanon says, his body stops being the unnoticed center from which he acts. Fanon here reworks two ideas. From [Sartre](jean-paul-sartre.md) he takes **the Look**: being seen by another fixes me as an object in their world. From [Merleau-Ponty](maurice-merleau-ponty.md) he takes the **body schema**, the tacit, lived sense of one's own body that lets one act in the world without attending to it. Fanon argues that racism adds a "historico-racial" and an "epidermal" (skin-level) schema woven from other people's stories, so that the Black body is experienced in the third person. Scholars read this as a correction of both: Sartre's Look is reciprocal in principle, but the white gaze in a racist world is not returned on equal terms.

### Hegel, Négritude and Sartre's "Black Orpheus"

Fanon also returns to [Hegel's](../04-19th-century/g-w-f-hegel.md) story of lordship and bondage, read through Alexandre Kojève's influential lectures, to ask whether a struggle for recognition can even begin when one side does not count the other as a self. He takes Négritude seriously as a way of changing how Black people feel about themselves, while doubting it as an account of what Black people are. And he answers Sartre's essay "Black Orpheus" (1948), which praised Négritude but called it a stage in a dialectic that would give way to the universal struggle of the working class. Fanon replied that this took his own experience out of his hands before he had lived it. The book ends not with a doctrine but with a demand to keep questioning.

### Decolonization and violence (Fanon)

*The Wretched of the Earth* begins with a chapter on violence and moves on to the pitfalls of national consciousness, national culture, and the mental disorders produced by colonial war. Its starting claim is that colonialism is violent from the first: it was imposed by force, is kept in place by force, and reaches into the minds of the colonized. Decolonization is therefore not a negotiation between equals but the overturning of that order, carried out by the people and especially, Fanon thought, by rural masses and the urban poor rather than by a colonized middle class ready to step into the colonizers' place. He warns that a new national elite can simply inherit the colonial state, and asks for "a new way of thinking" and a "new man" not modeled on Europe. What he says about violence itself is the most contested part of his work; the main readings are set out under Criticisms and debates.

## Key works

- Du Bois, "The Conservation of Races" (1897) — short address defining race historically; the starting point of the race debate around him.
- Du Bois, *The Souls of Black Folk* (1903) — fourteen essays on double consciousness, education, Washington, religion and the "Sorrow Songs"; the classic.
- Du Bois, *Black Reconstruction in America* (1935) — revisionist history of Reconstruction and the "psychological wage" of whiteness.
- Du Bois, *Dusk of Dawn* (1940) — autobiography, subtitled *An Essay Toward an Autobiography of a Race Concept*.
- Fanon, *Black Skin, White Masks* (1952) — language, desire, the Look, and the lived experience of racism.
- Fanon, *The Wretched of the Earth* (1961) — violence, national consciousness and culture, and colonial psychiatry; Sartre's preface.

## Contributions

Du Bois made the inner experience of racism a subject for philosophy and social science at once, and gave later thought its vocabulary: double consciousness, the veil, the color line. He offered one of the first sustained accounts of race as historical rather than natural, and with *Black Reconstruction* an account of whiteness as a wage of status. Fanon joined phenomenology to psychiatry to show how racism shapes the felt body, corrected Sartre and Merleau-Ponty from a position neither had occupied, and produced the most influential theory of decolonization of the twentieth century, including its warning about post-independence elites.

## Impact and legacy

Double consciousness is the most discussed concept in the scholarship on Du Bois, and readers have long compared it with the "white masks" of Fanon's title. Du Bois's work is a cornerstone of Africana philosophy and the critical philosophy of race, and *The Philadelphia Negro* is read as an early landmark of American sociology. Fanon became required reading for anti-colonial and Black liberation movements in Africa, the Americas and beyond, then for postcolonial theory (Homi Bhabha, Edward Said), critical phenomenology, and the philosophy of psychiatry. [Arendt's](hannah-arendt.md) *On Violence* (1970) was written partly against the way Fanon was being read through Sartre's preface, and that exchange still frames debates on political violence.

## Criticisms and debates

- **Is Du Bois's race biological after all?** Kwame Anthony Appiah ("The Uncompleted Argument," 1985; *In My Father's House*, 1992) argued that "Conservation" quietly relies on common ancestry to tell races apart, and that the logic of the argument leads to rejecting race altogether. Lucius Outlaw replied that Du Bois offers a cluster concept, not strict necessary conditions; Paul Taylor and Ronald Sundstrom defend a workable social-historical reading.
- **Where does double consciousness come from?** Dickson Bruce traced it to Emerson's romanticism and to nineteenth-century psychology of split personality, discussed by James in the *Principles of Psychology* (1890). Joel Williamson and Sandra Adell read it as a reworking of Hegel's *Phenomenology*, especially the "unhappy consciousness." Both lines may be true at once.
- **Elitism and the "Talented Tenth."** Du Bois long held that an educated Black elite should guide the masses. Whether he later gave this up is disputed: Joy James argues he repudiated it, Cornel West calls his revisions "piecemeal," and Adolph Reed argues the elite-centered view runs through his whole career.
- **Does Fanon glorify violence?** Arendt held that violence can sometimes be justified but never creates power, and that Sartre's preface went farther than Fanon himself. Elizabeth Frazer and Kimberly Hutchings set out an "Arendt contra Fanon" disagreement about whether violence can be a political means at all. Richard Bernstein reads *Wretched* as a critique of colonial violence that argues armed struggle was justified in that exceptional case, which brings him closer to Arendt than either side's slogans suggest. The IEP entry stresses that the violence starts with the colonizer and is thrown back; the SEP entry stresses violence as a shock that forms new collective identity. Which reading fits the text is unresolved.
- **Gender and language in Fanon.** Feminist critics point to his harsher treatment of the Martinican writer Mayotte Capécia than of a comparable male case; Caribbean writers since the 1970s have defended creole speech against his treatment of it as a fallen French.

## Misreadings to avoid

- Double consciousness is not simply having two identities. It is seeing yourself through other people's contempt.
- The color line is not only an American issue. Du Bois defined it globally from the start.
- Du Bois did not hold that races are fixed biological types; whether his definition fully escapes biology is the dispute.
- Fanon did not simply copy Sartre. He used Sartre's Look and corrected it, and he answered "Black Orpheus" directly.
- The violence chapter is not the whole of *The Wretched of the Earth*, and Sartre's preface is not Fanon's text. Arendt's sharpest words were aimed at the preface.

## What to read first

1. Du Bois, *The Souls of Black Folk*, ch. I "Of Our Spiritual Strivings," then the Forethought and ch. II.
2. Du Bois, "The Conservation of Races," with Appiah's "The Uncompleted Argument" alongside.
3. Fanon, *Black Skin, White Masks*, ch. 5 "The Lived Experience of the Black Man" (Philcox translation), after rereading Sartre on the Look.
4. Fanon, *The Wretched of the Earth*, the opening chapter on violence and the chapter on national consciousness, then Sartre's preface.
5. Arendt, *On Violence*, as the classic reply, with Bernstein's essay on violence as a counterweight.

## Study questions

1. Du Bois calls double consciousness both a harm and a kind of "second-sight." Can it be both? What would it mean to overcome it?
2. Does Du Bois's definition of race in "The Conservation of Races" rely on biology? Test Appiah's objection against the text.
3. How does Fanon's scene on the Lyon street change Sartre's account of being looked at?
4. Why does Fanon object to Sartre calling Négritude a stage in a dialectic?
5. Is Bernstein right that Fanon and Arendt are closer on violence than they seem? What would each say about the difference between justifying violence and glorifying it?
