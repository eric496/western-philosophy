## At a glance

- **Dates:** 1929–2026
- **Place:** Born in Düsseldorf and raised in Gummersbach, Germany; taught at Heidelberg and Frankfurt; directed a Max Planck Institute in Starnberg, Bavaria, where he lived from 1971 and died
- **School/tradition:** Critical theory (the "second generation" of the [Frankfurt School](frankfurt-school.md)), joined to American pragmatism, speech-act theory and social theory
- **Known for:** Rebuilding critical theory on communication instead of production: the public sphere, communicative action, discourse ethics, and a discourse theory of law and deliberative democracy

**Responds to:** [Kant](../03-early-modern/immanuel-kant.md), [Hegel](../04-19th-century/g-w-f-hegel.md), [Marx](../04-19th-century/karl-marx.md), [Frankfurt School](frankfurt-school.md), [Gadamer](hans-georg-gadamer.md), [Foucault](michel-foucault.md), [Derrida](jacques-derrida.md) · **Answered by:** [Gadamer](hans-georg-gadamer.md), [Rawls](john-rawls.md), [Derrida](jacques-derrida.md), later critical theorists (Axel Honneth, Nancy Fraser, Seyla Benhabib) — debate ongoing

## Biography

Jürgen Habermas was born in Düsseldorf on 18 June 1929 and grew up in Gummersbach, a Protestant town near Cologne. He was born with a cleft palate and had two operations as a child. He later said that the speech difficulty it left made him think hard about how much people depend on one another and on being understood. His father, an official of the Cologne chamber of commerce, joined the Nazi Party in 1933. As a boy Habermas was enrolled in the junior Hitler Youth, and in the last months of the war he served with an anti-aircraft unit on the western front. The end of the war was his turning point. The Nuremberg trials and the first reports of the death camps, he said, showed him what the regime had been. In a 1979 interview he called himself a product of "reeducation": he learned that the constitutional democracies of France, Britain and the United States were a historical achievement, not a foreign imposition. Much of his life's work can be read as an attempt to give that achievement a philosophical defense.

He studied philosophy and German literature at Göttingen, Zurich and Bonn, and took his doctorate at Bonn in 1954 with a dissertation on Schelling. In 1953, still a student, he published an article in the *Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung* attacking Heidegger for reissuing, unrevised, a 1935 lecture that praised "the inner truth and greatness" of the Nazi movement. After some years as a freelance journalist he became Theodor Adorno's assistant at the Institute for Social Research in Frankfurt (1956–59). Max Horkheimer thought him a dangerous Marxist and blocked his habilitation (the second thesis a German academic needs), so Habermas completed it at Marburg under the Marxist political scientist Wolfgang Abendroth. That thesis became *The Structural Transformation of the Public Sphere* (1962).

In 1962, before the habilitation was formally complete, Hans-Georg Gadamer and Karl Löwith brought him to Heidelberg as a professor. In 1964 he returned to Frankfurt to take Horkheimer's chair in philosophy and sociology. He backed the student movement at first, then broke with its radical wing in 1967–68, warning against what he called "left-wing fascism", a phrase he later admitted was too harsh. In 1971 he became co-director of a Max Planck Institute at Starnberg, near Munich, where he did the research for *The Theory of Communicative Action* (1981). He left Starnberg in the early 1980s (sources give 1981 or 1983) and taught at Frankfurt until he retired in 1994.

Habermas was also Germany's leading public intellectual for half a century. He wrote in newspapers on the Nazi past, German reunification, European integration, genetic engineering and the Iraq war. He received the Peace Prize of the German Book Trade (2001) and the Kyoto Prize (2004). He married Ute Wesselhoeft in 1955, and they had three children. He kept publishing into his nineties, including a long history of philosophy told through the relation between faith and knowledge (2019; in English as *Also a History of Philosophy*, three volumes, 2023–25). He died at Starnberg on 14 March 2026, aged 96.

## Major theories and concepts

### The public sphere

The **public sphere** is the space, belonging neither to the state nor to the market nor to the family, where private people meet to argue about matters of common concern and so form "public opinion". In *Structural Transformation* Habermas traces its rise in 18th-century Britain, France and Germany: coffee houses, salons, reading societies and political journals, where arguments were meant to count by their merits and not by the rank of the speaker.

He then tells a story of decline. In 20th-century mass democracy, parties, corporations and interest groups take over; debate gives way to bargaining between organized interests, and the press turns into advertising and "public relations". Habermas calls this **refeudalization**: as at a feudal court, the public is again an audience that applauds decisions already made. The 18th-century ideal was always partly an ideology, since it excluded the propertyless and women, but Habermas holds it is still a standard real democracies can be measured against. Critics, notably feminist historians, argue that the exclusions were part of how the bourgeois public sphere was built, not an accident of it.

### Knowledge and human interests

*Knowledge and Human Interests* (1968) argues against **positivism**, the view that the methods of natural science are the model for all real knowledge. Habermas claims that knowledge is always guided by deep interests rooted in the human species. The natural sciences serve a *technical* interest in predicting and controlling processes. The historical and interpretive sciences serve a *practical* interest in mutual understanding. Critical self-reflection, with psychoanalysis as its model, serves an *emancipatory* interest in freeing ourselves from forces we did not know were shaping us. Habermas later dropped much of this framework, judging it too tied to a "philosophy of consciousness" built around a single knowing subject.

### Communicative and strategic action

The **linguistic turn** of the 1970s led to *The Theory of Communicative Action* (1981). Habermas distinguishes two ways of coordinating what we do. In **strategic action** I treat others as means: I try to get my way by threats, rewards or manipulation. In **communicative action** we try to reach understanding, and we coordinate through agreement we each have reasons to accept.

Every serious statement, Habermas argues, raises **validity claims**: that what I say is *true*, that my speech act is *right* given shared norms, and that I am *sincere*. A hearer can answer each claim with "yes" or "no", and the speaker is committed to back it with reasons if challenged. The ideal is that nothing but the force of the better argument, free of coercion, should decide. **Communicative rationality** is the capacity to raise and redeem such claims. This is Habermas's answer to [Horkheimer and Adorno](frankfurt-school.md): reason is not only the instrumental calculation they diagnosed; it is already at work, in a richer form, in ordinary speech.

### System and lifeworld; the colonization of the lifeworld

Habermas says societies must be seen both as **lifeworlds** and as **systems**. The lifeworld is the background of shared culture, social bonds and personal identities that communicative action draws on and renews. Systems are the market economy and state administration, which coordinate action through the "steering media" of money and power, without anyone needing to agree. Systems are a modern achievement: no one could run a world economy by discussion. The pathology Habermas calls **colonization of the lifeworld** occurs when money and administrative power push into areas, such as family life, education and culture, that can only be reproduced through communication. Example: a school judged entirely by measurable outputs, or a welfare system that turns citizens into clients managed by rules, can undermine the very understanding and solidarity those institutions depend on. This updates Weber's worry about bureaucracy and Marx's analysis of reification without, Habermas says, claiming that reason itself has turned into domination.

### Discourse ethics: (U) and (D)

**Discourse ethics** (*Moral Consciousness and Communicative Action*, 1983) recasts [Kant](../03-early-modern/immanuel-kant.md)'s moral philosophy in dialogue. Kant asks what I can consistently will as a universal law. Habermas asks what everyone affected could agree to in a real argument. His translator Thomas McCarthy put the shift neatly, in a passage Habermas quotes: the emphasis moves from "what each can will without contradiction to be a general law, to what all can will in agreement to be a universal norm".

Two principles do the work. The **discourse principle (D)** says that only norms that could win the approval of all affected, as participants in a practical discourse, can claim to be valid. The **universalization principle (U)** is a rule of argument for moral questions: a norm is valid only if all affected could accept the consequences and side effects its general observance would have for everyone's interests. Habermas argued that anyone who argues at all already accepts the rules that lead to (U), so a skeptic who denies it falls into a **performative contradiction**, saying something the act of saying it contradicts. How (U) and (D) relate, and whether the derivation works, is contested; Habermas later treated (D) as the more basic principle and (U) as its moral application. Discourse ethics is about the **right**, not the **good**: it tests norms of justice, while questions of how I or my community should live are "ethical" and remain more local.

### Law and deliberative democracy: *Between Facts and Norms*

*Between Facts and Norms* (1992) applies discourse theory to law. Modern law has a double face: it is a fact (enforced by the state) and makes a claim to legitimacy (it should deserve obedience). Habermas's principle of democracy says that laws are legitimate only if all citizens could assent to them in a legally organized process of discursive lawmaking.

His central thesis is the **co-originality** of private and public autonomy. Liberals tend to put private rights first and treat democracy as a means of protecting them; republicans put popular sovereignty first. Habermas argues that neither comes first: citizens can only govern themselves through law if they already have basic rights, and those rights only get their concrete shape through democratic lawmaking. The ideal is that the addressees of the law also understand themselves as its authors. The resulting model of **deliberative democracy** pictures an informal public sphere, of civil society, media and social movements, picking up problems and generating "communicative power", which flows through elections and parliaments into law and administration. Legitimacy comes from the quality of public reasoning, not just from counting votes.

### Debates and the late work on religion

- **With Gadamer (from 1967).** Habermas praised [Gadamer](hans-georg-gadamer.md)'s hermeneutics but argued that tradition can be systematically distorted by power, so we need a critique of ideology that can step back from it; Gadamer replied that critique too stands inside tradition and language.
- **The Historikerstreit (1986).** In *Die Zeit* (11 July 1986) Habermas accused the historians Ernst Nolte, Andreas Hillgruber, Michael Stürmer and Klaus Hildebrand of apologetic history writing that relativized the Holocaust. The historians' dispute that followed was a fight over West German national identity; Habermas argued that the only patriotism compatible with the Federal Republic's turn to the West was a **constitutional patriotism**, loyalty to democratic principles rather than to the nation.
- **With Foucault and Derrida.** *The Philosophical Discourse of Modernity* (1985) defends modernity as an unfinished project (the title of his 1980 Adorno Prize lecture). It reads Nietzsche as the turning point into postmodern thought and argues that Heidegger, Derrida and [Foucault](michel-foucault.md) criticize reason in ways that leave no standpoint for their own criticism. [Derrida](jacques-derrida.md) complained that Habermas had not read him; the two later reconciled and in 2003 jointly published an appeal for a common European foreign policy.
- **With Rawls (1995).** In the *Journal of Philosophy* Habermas praised [Rawls](john-rawls.md)'s *Political Liberalism* but argued that the original position is too monological and that Rawls ranks liberal rights above democratic self-determination. Rawls replied that Habermas's theory, built on a philosophy of language and communication, is itself a "comprehensive doctrine", while his own is meant to be political only.
- **Religion.** From the 2001 Peace Prize speech "Faith and Knowledge" onward, Habermas described a **postsecular** society in which religion persists. He held that religious traditions carry moral insights secular reason has not exhausted, debated the future Pope Benedict XVI (Joseph Ratzinger) in Munich in 2004, and proposed a **translation proviso**: citizens may argue in religious terms in the informal public sphere, but laws and court rulings must be justified in reasons open to all. He kept the stance he called "methodological atheism" in philosophy itself.

## Key works

- *The Structural Transformation of the Public Sphere* (1962; tr. Burger and Lawrence 1989) — The rise and decline of the bourgeois public sphere.
- *Knowledge and Human Interests* (1968; tr. Shapiro 1971) — The three knowledge-constitutive interests and the critique of positivism.
- *The Theory of Communicative Action* (1981; tr. McCarthy, 2 vols., 1984, 1987) — Communicative rationality, system and lifeworld; his magnum opus.
- *Moral Consciousness and Communicative Action* (1983; tr. Lenhardt and Nicholsen 1990) — The founding statement of discourse ethics.
- *The Philosophical Discourse of Modernity* (1985; tr. Lawrence 1987) — Twelve lectures defending modernity against Nietzsche, Heidegger, Derrida and Foucault.
- *Between Facts and Norms* (1992; tr. Rehg 1996) — Discourse theory of law and deliberative democracy.
- *Between Naturalism and Religion* (2005; tr. Cronin 2008) — Essays on religion in the public sphere.

## Contributions

Habermas gave critical theory a normative foundation it had lacked: a theory of communicative rationality that lets social criticism say what it is criticizing *in the name of*. He made the **public sphere** a central category for history, media studies and political science, joined speech-act theory to social theory, devised **discourse ethics**, and built one of the most developed theories of **deliberative democracy** and of law's legitimacy.

## Impact and legacy

His work shaped sociology, legal theory, communication studies and international relations as well as philosophy. Deliberative democracy, partly inspired by him, became a major research program and influenced practical experiments such as citizens' assemblies. Students and successors, including Axel Honneth (recognition), Seyla Benhabib and Rainer Forst, carried critical theory forward on his foundations, often by revising them. His defense of a post-national, constitutional Europe made him a voice in EU debates, and his part in the Historikerstreit helped fix the Holocaust at the center of German public memory. His notion of the public sphere is the default framework for debates about platform media and democracy.

## Criticisms and debates

- **Too idealized?** Critics argue that consensus through argument is unrealistic and hides power and conflict. Agonistic democrats such as Chantal Mouffe see disagreement as permanent; Habermas replies that the ideal is a standard implicit in practice, not a description.
- **Exclusions.** Nancy Fraser and others argue that the bourgeois public sphere was built on excluding women and workers, and that "counterpublics" matter; Habermas accepted parts of this in later writing.
- **The Foucault question.** Foucauldians reply that communication is itself shot through with power, so a "power-free" discourse is a fiction; Habermas's critics on the other side say Foucault cannot justify his own critique. This dispute is open.
- **Discourse ethics.** Whether (U) can really be derived from the presuppositions of argument, and whether a procedure can settle substantive moral questions, are standing disputes.
- **Religion.** Religious critics such as Nicholas Wolterstorff argue that the translation proviso still burdens believers unfairly; secular critics question whether religious content can be "translated" at all.

## Misreadings to avoid

- **"Habermas thinks people actually reach consensus."** The ideal speech situation is a counterfactual standard built into argument, not a forecast.
- **"He is just another Frankfurt School pessimist."** He rejected the pessimism of *Dialectic of Enlightenment* and defended the Enlightenment as unfinished.
- **"Communicative action means being nice."** It means coordinating through reasons all can accept, which includes hard criticism.
- **"Discourse ethics tells you how to live."** It tests norms of justice; questions of the good life are left to individuals and communities.
- **"He was a lifelong member of the Frankfurt Institute."** He was there only briefly (1956–59) and said there was no coherent "school" when he arrived.

## What to read first

1. The 1964 encyclopedia article "The Public Sphere" (in English in *New German Critique*, 1974), a few pages long, then *Structural Transformation*, Parts I–II.
2. "Discourse Ethics: Notes on a Program of Philosophical Justification", in *Moral Consciousness and Communicative Action*.
3. *The Theory of Communicative Action*, vol. 1, ch. 3 (the "first intermediate reflection" on social action), then vol. 2, ch. 8 on colonization.
4. *Between Facts and Norms*, ch. 3 (co-originality) and ch. 7 (deliberative politics).
5. *The Philosophical Discourse of Modernity*, lectures I and XI.

## Study questions

1. Is Habermas's ideal of the 18th-century public sphere a useful standard, or does its history of exclusion disqualify it?
2. What is the difference between strategic and communicative action? Can an advertisement ever be communicative action?
3. Give an example of "colonization of the lifeworld" from your own experience. What would decolonizing it look like?
4. How does (U) change Kant's categorical imperative, and what is gained or lost by moving from "what I can will" to "what all can agree to"?
5. Why does Habermas say private and public autonomy are "co-original"? Which side do you think Rawls puts first?
6. Does the translation proviso treat religious citizens fairly?
