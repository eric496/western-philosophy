# Nicolas Malebranche

## At a glance

- **Dates:** 1638–1715
- **Place:** Paris, France; a priest of the Oratory for his whole adult life
- **School/tradition:** Cartesian rationalism joined to the theology of Augustine
- **Known for:** Occasionalism (God is the only true cause), the "vision in God" (we see bodies through ideas that exist in God), and the claim that God governs the world through a few simple general laws

**Responds to:** [Augustine](../02-medieval/augustine-of-hippo.md), [Aquinas](../02-medieval/thomas-aquinas.md), [Descartes](rene-descartes.md) · **Answered by:** [Leibniz](gottfried-wilhelm-leibniz.md), [Berkeley](george-berkeley.md), [Hume](david-hume.md), [Mary Astell](early-modern-women-philosophers.md), [Reid](thomas-reid.md)

## Biography

Nicolas Malebranche was born in Paris on 6 August 1638, one of many children of a royal secretary. A malformed spine and weak lungs kept him at home with tutors until he was sixteen. He then studied at the Collège de la Marche and took theology at the Sorbonne, which left him with a lasting dislike of the Aristotle-based school philosophy called **scholasticism**. In 1660 he joined the Oratory, a congregation of priests devoted to the study of Augustine, and he was ordained on 14 September 1664.

The same year, as his early biographer Father Yves André tells it, he picked up Descartes's *Treatise on Man* at a Paris bookstall and was so excited that his heart pounded and he had to stop reading to catch his breath. He spent the next decade studying Cartesian method, mathematics and physics. The result was *The Search after Truth* (1674–75), and in 1678 he added sixteen *Elucidations* answering objections (a seventeenth came with the last edition in 1712).

His *Treatise on Nature and Grace* (1680) started the great quarrel of his life. Antoine Arnauld (1612–1694), a leading Jansenist theologian and a Cartesian, had urged him not to publish it. Arnauld opened fire in 1683 with *On True and False Ideas*, an attack on the theory of ideas in the *Search*, and the dispute moved on to providence, grace and miracles. It turned bitter: *Nature and Grace* was placed on the Catholic Index of Prohibited Books in 1690, and the *Search* in 1709.

Meanwhile Malebranche kept writing: the *Christian and Metaphysical Meditations* (1683), the *Treatise on Ethics* (1684), and the *Dialogues on Metaphysics and on Religion* (1688), the clearest short statement of his system. He also did real science. He wrote on the laws of motion, corresponded with Leibniz, who pressed him to admit that Descartes's conservation law fails, and in 1699 became an honorary member of the Paris Académie des sciences, where he defended a theory of color as frequencies of light. He died in Paris on 13 October 1715. Pierre Bayle had hailed him as the leading philosopher of the age.

## Major theories and concepts

### The Search after Truth: a manual for avoiding error

The *Search* is organized by the sources of error: the senses, imagination, pure understanding, inclinations, and passions, one book each, with a sixth book on method. Following Descartes, Malebranche argues that the senses are not there to teach us what bodies are. They tell us what is good or bad for our body. Colors, tastes and smells are modifications of the mind, not features of things. Error comes from treating these "natural judgments" as knowledge. The cure is to attend to clear ideas, above all the idea of **extension** (length, breadth and depth), which is all that matter is.

He adds a twist that Descartes would not accept. We have a clear idea of body, but only a confused "inner sensation" of our own soul. So for Malebranche, body is better known than mind, the reverse of Descartes's second *Meditation*.

### The vision in God

Book III asks how we perceive bodies at all. Malebranche starts from what he takes to be agreed: we do not perceive external objects "by themselves." It is absurd, he says, that "the soul should leave the body to stroll about the heavens to see the objects present there" (Book III, Part 2; OC 1:413). So we perceive through **ideas**, things present to the mind that represent objects.

Where are these ideas? He lists the alternatives: bodies send copies of themselves to us; the soul makes ideas when the body is affected; God created the ideas with the soul; or the soul sees bodies in its own perfections. He argues that each fails. A finite soul, for example, cannot contain the infinite, yet we do grasp infinity. What is left is that we see ideas in God, who contains the archetypes of everything he made. A second argument comes from Augustine: the ideas we grasp in geometry are eternal and necessary, so they cannot be passing states of a creature's mind.

Ordinary perception, then, has two parts. God causes a sensation (a feeling of color, say) in my soul, and God shows me the idea, which is in him. Later Malebranche spoke of a single **intelligible extension** in God that represents all bodies, and after about 1695 of an **efficacious idea**, an idea in God that acts on our minds. One scholar sums up the change as a move from vision in God to vision *by* God.

### The debate with Arnauld on ideas

Arnauld thought Malebranche had rigged the game from the first step. Ideas, Arnauld argued, are not little objects standing between the mind and the world. An idea is simply my perception itself, considered as representing something. In his words, "ideas, taken in the sense of representative beings, distinct from perceptions, are not needed by our soul in order to see bodies" (*On True and False Ideas*, 1683). He claimed, plausibly, that this was Descartes's own view.

Malebranche replied that his definition of an idea left that question open, and that his elimination argument settled it. His deeper reason was that if ideas were only my mental states, they could not have the necessity and permanence that geometry needs, and a priori knowledge of the material world would collapse into subjectivism. Arnauld also charged that putting extension in God came close to [Spinoza](baruch-spinoza.md)'s view that God is extended. Malebranche denied it: the extension in God is infinite and indivisible, unlike the divisible extension of the material world. Historians still treat this exchange as a founding debate about whether perception needs a go-between at all, the question Reid later pressed against the "Way of Ideas."

### Occasionalism: God the only true cause

**Occasionalism** is the view that God is the only real cause, and that creatures are only the "occasions" on which God acts. When one billiard ball strikes another, the impact is the occasion; God moves the second ball. When I will to raise my arm, my volition is the occasion; God moves the arm.

The old textbook story says Malebranche invented this as a stopgap for the Cartesian puzzle of how mind and body interact. Recent scholarship points out that the doctrine was much older: Aquinas had already argued against it. In the Islamic world, al-Ghazali had argued in the eleventh century that we never observe fire *making* cotton burn, only the burning following contact. Earlier Cartesians such as Louis de la Forge and Géraud de Cordemoy had moved toward it. Malebranche's version is the most thorough.

His main argument is about **necessary connection**. "A true cause as I understand it is one such that the mind perceives a necessary connection between it and its effect" (*Search* VI.ii.3). We can deny without contradiction that one body moves another, or that a brain state produces a feeling, so there is no necessary connection there. The only necessary connection we can perceive is between the will of an all-powerful being and what it wills. So only God is a true cause. A second argument, in the *Dialogues on Metaphysics*, starts from Descartes's claim that conserving the world is continuous creation. If God re-creates each body at each moment, he must create it somewhere, and its place from moment to moment is up to him alone, so motion is his work.

Malebranche also had religious motives. To credit creatures with divine power is, he said, a kind of idolatry, and in Book VI of the *Search* he calls the belief in causes in nature distinct from God the most dangerous error of the ancient philosophers.

### Nature and Grace: general volitions

If God does everything, why does nature look lawlike, and why does it contain evils? Malebranche's answer is that God acts almost always by **general volitions**: he wills laws, such as the laws of the communication of motion, and the particular effects follow. Only in miracles does he act by **particular volitions**. Because God acts generally, natural science is possible: we can explain effects by the laws that express his general will.

The same idea drives his theodicy (a defense of God's goodness given evil). God could have prevented a malformed birth by a particular volition, and so made a more perfect world. But he would have had to give up the simplicity of his ways, which best expresses his wisdom. God wills the world with the most effects produced by the fewest laws, and the evils come with it. In the *Treatise on Nature and Grace* he extends this to grace: God distributes grace by general laws too, and sinners are responsible because they freely "consent" to lesser goods instead of loving God.

Arnauld objected that this denies God's care for particulars, which Scripture teaches, and that the account of freedom was worse than Pelagianism, the heresy Augustine fought.

## Key works

- *The Search after Truth* (1674–75; *Elucidations* 1678; last edition 1712) — The main work: errors of sense, imagination and passion, the vision in God (Book III), occasionalism (Book VI and Elucidation XV). The standard English version is the Lennon–Olscamp translation (1980; Cambridge, 1997), which includes the *Elucidations*.
- *Treatise on Nature and Grace* (1680) — God's general and particular volitions, theodicy, grace. The book that started the Arnauld quarrel.
- *Christian and Metaphysical Meditations* (1683) — The system presented as the voice of the Word instructing the soul.
- *Treatise on Ethics* (1684) — Virtue as the love of the order God reveals.
- *Dialogues on Metaphysics and on Religion* (1688) — A compact, readable summary of the vision in God and occasionalism (English: Jolley and Scott, 1997).

## In their own words

> When we perceive something sensible, two things are found in our perception: sensation and pure idea. The sensation is a modification of our soul, and it is God who causes it in us. As for the idea found in conjunction with the sensation, it is in God, and we see it because it pleases God to reveal it to us.

— *The Search after Truth*, Book III, Part 2, ch. 6, trans. Lennon and Olscamp, p. 234 (OC 1:445)

> A true cause as I understand it is one such that the mind perceives a necessary connection between it and its effect. Now the mind perceives a necessary connection between the will of an infinite being and its effect.

— *The Search after Truth*, Book VI, Part 2, ch. 3, trans. Lennon and Olscamp, p. 450 (OC 2:316)

> I shall not therefore be surprised if some men imagine that I run into the enthusiasm of Malebranche; though in truth I am very remote from it. He builds on the most abstract general ideas, which I entirely disclaim. He asserts an absolute external world, which I deny.

— George Berkeley, *Three Dialogues between Hylas and Philonous* (1713), Second Dialogue (Philonous speaking)

Notice what the second passage leaves out: it never says creatures lack a necessary connection to their effects. That step needs a further argument. What is it?

## Contributions

Malebranche turned occasionalism from a scattered Cartesian tendency into a full system with a precise test for causation (necessary connection). He gave the first sustained argument that the soul is less well known than body, posed the question of whether ideas are mental acts or objects in a famous public debate, and built a theodicy around the simplicity of general laws.

## Impact and legacy

Bayle praised him, and for a generation he was one of the most discussed philosophers in Europe. In England John Norris adopted his views, and Mary Astell argued against occasionalism in her letters with Norris (see [Early modern women philosophers](early-modern-women-philosophers.md)). [Leibniz](gottfried-wilhelm-leibniz.md) agreed that created substances do not strictly act on one another, but in the *New System* (1695) he rejected occasionalism as bringing in a god from the machine and a recourse to miracle, later calling it a perpetual miracle, and offered pre-established harmony instead.

[Berkeley](george-berkeley.md) read Malebranche closely, and his critics at once saw the likeness: both make God the direct source of our perceptions. Berkeley denied the debt in the passage quoted above, and the extent of the influence is a classic scholarly question (A. A. Luce's *Berkeley and Malebranche*, 1934, argued it was large).

[Hume](david-hume.md) took over Malebranche's negative argument: we never perceive a necessary connection between one body and another, or between a volition and a movement. He refused the positive conclusion. In the *Enquiry* (1748), Section VII, he says "Malebranche and other Cartesians" made God's sole efficacy "the foundation of all their philosophy," and he answers that a theory this remote from experience has taken us into "fairy land." He adds that we have no better idea of how God's will produces effects than of how bodies do. Hume's own account of causation as constant conjunction plus a habit of the mind can be read as Malebranche's occasionalism with God taken away.

## Criticisms and debates

- **The perpetual miracle.** Leibniz argued that anything God does with no contribution from created natures is a miracle, whether or not it follows a general law. Bayle replied for Malebranche that acting by general laws is not acting extraordinarily. Whether general laws are enough to make nature natural is still debated.
- **Fontenelle's dilemma.** Bernard de Fontenelle (1686) argued that mechanism and occasionalism clash: if collision necessitates a change of motion, collision meets Malebranche's own test for a true cause. Malebranche replied that the laws of collision come from God's will, not from the nature of bodies. Scholars disagree about whether the reply works.
- **What is a general volition?** Steven Nadler has argued that Malebranche means only that God's many volitions conform to general laws. Others (Black, Jolley, Schmaltz) reply that Arnauld read him correctly: God really has few volitions, and the laws themselves are "efficacious."
- **Freedom.** If God is the only cause, how can we be free and responsible for sin? Malebranche says our free act is a "consent" that adds no real being, almost a resting. Scholars debate whether this is consistent with occasionalism.
- **Ideas.** Was Arnauld right that Malebranche simply assumed ideas are objects distinct from perceptions? Or does the eliminative argument, plus the Augustinian argument from necessity, earn that assumption?

## Misreadings to avoid

- **"Occasionalism was invented to fix mind–body interaction."** It covers body–body causation too, it is older than Descartes, and Malebranche's motives were also theological.
- **"Malebranche is an idealist like Berkeley."** He believed in an external material world, though he held its existence is known with certainty only by faith in Scripture.
- **"Vision in God means we see God's essence."** He says we see what in God represents creatures, not God as he is in himself.
- **"If God does everything, science is pointless."** His general volitions are exactly what make lawlike science possible.

## What to read first

1. *Dialogues on Metaphysics and on Religion*, Dialogues 1–3 and 7 — vision in God and occasionalism in conversational form.
2. *The Search after Truth*, Book III, Part 2, chs. 1–7 (Lennon–Olscamp) — the theory of ideas and the vision in God.
3. *The Search after Truth*, Book VI, Part 2, ch. 3, with Elucidation XV — the arguments for occasionalism.
4. *Treatise on Nature and Grace*, First Discourse — general volitions and the problem of evil.
5. Hume, *Enquiry*, Section VII — to see what happens to the argument once God is removed.

## Study questions

1. Malebranche says a true cause must have a necessary connection to its effect. Is that a fair definition of "cause," or does it guarantee his conclusion?
2. Lay out the eliminative argument for the vision in God. Which alternative do you think he dismisses too quickly?
3. Who has the better theory of ideas, Malebranche or Arnauld? What does each lose?
4. Can a God who acts by general laws be called caring toward particular creatures?
5. Hume accepts Malebranche's premise and rejects his conclusion. What is the exact point where they part?
