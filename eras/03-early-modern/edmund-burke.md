## At a glance

- **Dates:** 1729–1797
- **Place:** Dublin and London
- **School/tradition:** Whig parliamentarian and political philosopher; later read as the founder of modern conservatism
- **Known for:** His *Reflections on the Revolution in France* (1790) and its arguments from prescription, prejudice, the "little platoon" of local attachment, and society as a partnership across generations — set against what he called "metaphysic rights"

**Responds to:** [Locke](john-locke.md), [Hume](david-hume.md), [Rousseau](jean-jacques-rousseau.md) · **Answered by:** [Wollstonecraft](mary-wollstonecraft.md), Paine (*Rights of Man*), [Kant](immanuel-kant.md) (on the sublime)

## Biography

Edmund Burke was born on 12 January 1729 in Dublin, in a British Empire that treated Ireland as a dependency. His father Richard was a Protestant attorney; his mother Mary Nagle came from a Catholic family of County Cork, and his sister was raised Catholic — the mixed background shadowed his public life. He attended a Quaker school at Ballitore under Abraham Shackleton, then Trinity College, Dublin, taking his BA in 1748. In 1750 he went to London to read for the Bar, but books pulled him off that track. He married Jane Mary Nugent, daughter of his Catholic physician, on 12 March 1757; their only surviving son Richard was born the following February.

Two early works made his name. *A Vindication of Natural Society* (1756), published anonymously in the voice of the deist peer Viscount Bolingbroke, is a straight-faced reductio: Bolingbroke's arguments against revealed religion, if good, discredit civil society as a whole. The next year came *A Philosophical Enquiry into the Origin of our Ideas of the Sublime and Beautiful* (1757; revised 1759). From 1758 he edited the new *Annual Register*.

Politics arrived in 1765, when he became private secretary to the Marquis of Rockingham and entered the Commons for the pocket borough of Wendover. He sat for twenty-nine years: Wendover until 1774, Bristol from 1774 to 1780, then Rockingham's borough of Malton until he retired in 1794. He never held cabinet office; his power was that of a party man and an orator.

Four causes consumed those years. He fought the king's "friends" and the use of patronage to control Parliament (*Thoughts on the Cause of the Present Discontents*, 1770). He urged conciliation with the American colonies rather than coercion (*American Taxation*, 1774; *Conciliation with America*, 1775). He pressed for Catholic relief and free trade with Ireland — positions that cost him his Bristol seat, which he gave up on 9 September 1780 by declining the poll rather than fight a contest he had lost in advance. In February 1780 he also introduced his plan of Economical Reform of the Civil List; a watered-down version became law only in 1782. And he waged, for a decade, an impeachment of Warren Hastings, first governor-general of Bengal; the trial opened in 1788 and dragged on until 1795, when Hastings was acquitted of every charge.

Then came the French Revolution. Burke, in his sixties, broke publicly with his old Whig allies — including Charles James Fox — and on 1 November 1790 published *Reflections on the Revolution in France*. It set off the greatest pamphlet war of the age. He followed with *An Appeal from the New to the Old Whigs* (1791) and the *Letters on a Regicide Peace* (1795–97), arguing that regicide France was an "armed doctrine" with which no peace was possible. He retired in summer 1794. His son Richard, elected to succeed him at Malton, died aged thirty-six on 2 August 1794; Burke never recovered. He died at Beaconsfield on 9 July 1797.

## Major theories and concepts

### The sublime and the beautiful

Burke's first large work, the *Philosophical Enquiry* (1757), is aesthetics as applied psychology. He wants a natural history of two feelings: the awe that overcomes you before a mountain or a cathedral, and the pleasure of a small, smooth, well-proportioned face. Earlier writers lumped these together as beauty; Burke argues they come from different sources. The beautiful is grounded in pleasure, especially the pleasures of society; its marks are smallness, smoothness, delicacy. The sublime is grounded in pain, danger and the instinct of self-preservation — but at a safe distance, so that it does not actually hurt. His Part I §VII definition: whatever excites the ideas of pain and danger, "or is conversant about terrible objects, or operates in a manner analogous to terror, is a source of the sublime." The sublime is a delighted terror — the mind, not quite overwhelmed, enlarged by its brush with what might.

Burke works the empiricist program [Locke](john-locke.md) made famous, which puts him in the same world as his contemporary [David Hume](david-hume.md). The mind he describes reasons after it feels — a premise he carries straight into politics. The *Enquiry* also became an important source, and a target of criticism, for [Kant's](immanuel-kant.md) account of the sublime in the *Critique of Judgment*.

### Prescription: the authority of long use

The engine of Burke's politics is a doctrine lawyers already knew. In English property law, *prescription* is the principle that long and peaceful possession creates a right: use land undisturbed for the required period and the title holds, even without an original grant. Burke extends this from land to institutions. A constitution, a corporation, an established church — if it has been exercised quietly over generations and ordinary life has arranged itself around it, that is powerful evidence of fitness, not set aside merely because a short argument from first principles says it should be. The claim blocks the move from "this looks irrational on paper" to "therefore sweep it away," and treats surviving institutions as carriers of practical knowledge too dispersed to write down. Its tension is obvious — a prescriptive right is often also an inherited privilege, and some long-standing arrangements (slavery, say) are wrong. Burke answers by distinguishing abuse from use; critics press whether the doctrine gives a usable rule for telling them apart.

### Prejudice as stored reasoning

In the *Reflections* Burke takes a word his readers used only as an insult and uses it, deliberately, as praise. "We are afraid," he writes, "to put men to live and trade each on his own private stock of reason, because we suspect that this stock in each man is small, and that the individuals would do better to avail themselves of the general bank and capital of nations and of ages." A *prejudice*, on this picture, is a judgment made before reflection, inherited from a community — and that is precisely its value. It arrives already connected to feeling, so it moves the holder; it is available in the emergency, when there is no time to argue first principles; it makes virtue a habit, "not a series of unconnected acts." Burke does not say every prejudice is wise. He says that one which has lasted long and travelled widely probably has latent wisdom in it, and the burden lies on the critic who would strip it away. The argument is a direct challenge to the Enlightenment ideal — associated with [Rousseau](jean-jacques-rousseau.md) and the *philosophes* — that nothing should be believed which each person has not first examined on their own authority. Burke is not against examination; he is against the fantasy that each generation starts over.

### The little platoon

One sentence from the *Reflections* has become among the most quoted in English political writing: "To be attached to the subdivision, to love the little platoon we belong to in society, is the first principle (the germ as it were) of public affections. It is the first link in the series by which we proceed towards a love to our country and to mankind." The claim is psychological before it is political. Universal benevolence cannot be built in a vacuum: the feelings that bind a person to family, parish, regiment and city are the only soil in which a wider loyalty can grow. Sever those local attachments and you do not produce citizens of the world — you produce isolated individuals with no attachments at all. Burke's quarrel with the Revolution's reorganization of France — the new departmental map, the dissolution of monasteries, the levelling of local corporations — is partly that it tore up precisely these little platoons.

### Society as a partnership across generations

The best-known passage of the *Reflections* follows. "Society is indeed a contract," Burke concedes, but not the sort found in business — not a "partnership agreement in a trade of pepper and coffee, calico, or tobacco." It is "a partnership in all science; a partnership in all art; a partnership in every virtue and in all perfection." And because the ends of such a partnership cannot be reached in one lifetime, it is "a partnership not only between those who are living, but between those who are living, those who are dead, and those who are to be born." The social contract exists, but it binds many more parties than a Hobbesian or [Lockean](john-locke.md) contract between the people now alive; political action acquires a trustee character; and the standard of a decent politics is not just what the living consent to, but what the dead would recognize and the unborn can live with.

### Against abstract rights

Burke is not against rights as such. He accepts that English subjects have real, enforceable rights — of security, fair trial, property, liberty under law — and spends much of his career defending them against the Crown and the East India Company. What he rejects is the French Declaration's method: deducing a complete set of political entitlements from a bare definition of "man" prior to any society, and rebuilding institutions by that deduction. Real rights, he argues, are concrete and limited: the right to education or subsistence is not satisfied by an abstract claim but by actual schools, laws and courts, which only an actual political community can provide. "Metaphysical" rights taken as the standard become a dangerous instrument — any arrangement that falls short of the formula can be condemned as illegitimate, which licenses revolution.

### The trustee representative

On 3 November 1774 Burke told the Bristol electors who had just chosen him that a Member of Parliament is not an ambassador sent to vote their instructions. "Your representative owes you, not his industry only, but his judgment; and he betrays, instead of serving you, if he sacrifices it to your opinion." Parliament, on this view, is a deliberative assembly whose job is to reason about the common good rather than to tally local demands. Modern political science calls this the *trustee* model (against the *delegate* model), and the distinction is now standard in textbooks.

## Key works

- *A Vindication of Natural Society* (1756) — Anonymous Bolingbroke-persona satire: a reductio of deist argument used to defend civil society.
- *A Philosophical Enquiry into the Origin of our Ideas of the Sublime and Beautiful* (1757; revised 1759) — His aesthetic treatise; a theory of the sublime grounded in pain and self-preservation, of beauty in pleasure.
- *Thoughts on the Cause of the Present Discontents* (1770) — The classic defense of organized political parties against royal patronage.
- *Speech on American Taxation* (1774) and *Speech on Conciliation with America* (1775) — Case against taxing the colonies on grounds of prudence and inherited English liberty, not natural right.
- *Speech to the Electors of Bristol* (3 November 1774) — The trustee statement: a representative owes his judgment, not just his industry.
- *Reflections on the Revolution in France* (1 November 1790) — The major work: prescription, prejudice, the little platoon, society as partnership, and the case against abstract rights.
- *An Appeal from the New to the Old Whigs* (1791) and *Letters on a Regicide Peace* (1795–97) — Later works defending and extending the *Reflections*; the latter argues that revolutionary France is an "armed doctrine," not a normal state.

## In their own words

> Society is indeed a contract. Subordinate contracts for objects of mere occasional interest may be dissolved at pleasure; but the state ought not to be considered as nothing better than a partnership agreement in a trade of pepper and coffee, calico or tobacco, or some other such low concern, to be taken up for a little temporary interest, and to be dissolved by the fancy of the parties. It is to be looked on with other reverence; because it is not a partnership in things subservient only to the gross animal existence of a temporary and perishable nature. It is a partnership in all science; a partnership in all art; a partnership in every virtue, and in all perfection. As the ends of such a partnership cannot be obtained in many generations, it becomes a partnership not only between those who are living, but between those who are living, those who are dead, and those who are to be born.

— Edmund Burke, *Reflections on the Revolution in France* (1790; public domain)

This is Burke in a single breath. He grants the Lockean premise that society is a contract, denies the thin version that lets the current parties walk away, and finally makes the move that cannot be undone: the partners are not just the living. When Burke says "those who are to be born," he lands the Revolution's hardest problem on its sorest point, since the Revolution insisted that the dead have no rights over the living.

## Contributions

Burke gave political thought the vocabulary of prescription applied beyond law, of prejudice as stored social reasoning, of the little platoon, and of society as an inter-generational partnership. He gave representative democracy its clearest statement of the trustee ideal, and liberalism and conservatism alike a sustained critique of abstract rights as a method of political design. He defended organized political parties, free trade with Ireland, Catholic relief, conciliation with America, and standards of justice in India, in each case against powerful opposition in his own party. His *Enquiry* is a founding document of modern aesthetics.

## Impact and legacy

The *Reflections* reached an audience far beyond philosophy; the pamphlet war of 1790–92 — [Wollstonecraft](mary-wollstonecraft.md), Paine, and others — shaped British political argument for a generation. In the nineteenth century, liberals including John Stuart Mill read Burke as the great English theorist of practical reform. From the late nineteenth century (scholarship traces the shift to between 1885 and 1914) Conservative writers began describing him as the founder of their tradition, and twentieth-century American conservatives such as Russell Kirk made that claim central. His speeches on India have been read as an early rights-based critique of empire; the *Enquiry* fed into Romantic poetry and later aesthetics; and the trustee model of representation remains a working category of political science.

## Criticisms and debates

**Did Burke found conservatism?** Reference works routinely call him "the first explicitly conservative political theorist" (Britannica). It is also contested. Historians including J. G. A. Pocock argue that the retrospective rebranding smooths over the Whig polemicist who defended party government, Catholic relief and Economical Reform, and the fierce critic of imperial misrule in India; the Burke-as-founder narrative is itself a late nineteenth-century invention. Treat the question as live: no single reading is the whole figure.

**Is he consistent across America, Ireland, India and France?** His enemies thought him a trimmer. His own answer, worked out in the *Appeal*, was that the principle is one and the application different. In America, Ireland and India, inherited rights and settled ways of life were being overridden by distant power; he defended them. In France, the same was being done by a revolution speaking in the name of universal principles; he defended them again. Whether this is coherence or special pleading has been argued ever since.

**Wollstonecraft and Paine answer back.** Within weeks of the *Reflections*, [Mary Wollstonecraft](mary-wollstonecraft.md) published *A Vindication of the Rights of Men* (1790) — the first full-length rejoinder. Her charge is that Burke's reverence for inherited order is a defense of inherited privilege: his "chivalry" is cover for property, and his horror at the Revolution's treatment of the French queen tells us more about his sensibility than about justice. Thomas Paine's *Rights of Man*, Part I (1791) and Part II (1792), set the opposition that structures British radical thought thereafter: where Burke appeals to prescription, Paine appeals to the equal natural rights of each generation. Read side by side the two books remain the clearest education in the two ways of thinking about reform.

## Misreadings to avoid

- **"Burke was always a Tory."** He sat as a Whig and fought the king's party on patronage, America, Ireland and India. The French Revolution split his old party; it did not change his seat.
- **"Burke rejected rights."** He defended concrete English rights throughout his career. What he rejected was deducing political entitlements from an abstract definition of man.
- **"Burke invented resistance to change."** He supported Catholic relief, free trade with Ireland, Economical Reform, impeachment of a sitting Governor-General, and conciliation with America. He opposed one kind of change — the kind that scraps the inheritance.
- **"Burke's trustee model gives MPs a free hand."** It gives representatives judgment, not immunity; Burke withdrew at Bristol in 1780 rather than face a poll he knew he would lose, and never denied the electors' right to turn him out.
- **"The *Enquiry* is a youthful curiosity."** It became a working source for Kant's third *Critique* and for Romantic poetics.

## What to read first

1. *Reflections on the Revolution in France* — the little-platoon, prescription, prejudice and partnership passages first; then the attacks on abstract rights.
2. *Speech to the Electors of Bristol* (3 November 1774) — short; the trustee statement in context.
3. *Speech on Conciliation with America* (1775) — the practical Burke, choosing policy over natural-right argument.
4. Mary Wollstonecraft, *A Vindication of the Rights of Men* (1790), with Thomas Paine, *Rights of Man* Part I (1791) — Burke's two best early critics; read alongside the *Reflections*.
5. *A Philosophical Enquiry into the Origin of our Ideas of the Sublime and Beautiful* (1757), Part I — the terror-sublime, in his own compact prose.
