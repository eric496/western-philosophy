## At a glance

- **Dates:** 1723–1790
- **Place:** Scotland; held chairs of logic and moral philosophy at Glasgow
- **School/tradition:** Moral philosopher of the Scottish Enlightenment
- **Known for:** *The Theory of Moral Sentiments* (1759), which grounds morality in sympathy disciplined by an "impartial spectator," and *An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations* (1776), the founding text of classical economics — which Smith himself understood as one part of a larger science of human nature and society

**Responds to:** [Hutcheson](moral-sense-school.md), [Hume](david-hume.md), [Montesquieu](montesquieu.md) · **Answered by:** [J. S. Mill](../04-19th-century/john-stuart-mill.md), [Marx](../04-19th-century/karl-marx.md)

## Biography

Smith was born in Kirkcaldy, a small Fife port town, baptized in June 1723 (the exact birth date is unknown), a few months after his father's death. His father had been comptroller of customs at Kirkcaldy, and Smith was raised by his mother, Margaret Douglas, to whom he stayed close all her life. After the local burgh school he went to the University of Glasgow in 1737. There he studied under [Francis Hutcheson](moral-sense-school.md), whose lectures in moral philosophy set his direction. Fifty years later he still called him "the never-to-be-forgotten Dr. Hutcheson" (letter to Principal Davidson, 1787, printed in Rae's *Life*, ch. 29).

He then spent six years at Balliol College, Oxford, as a Snell exhibitioner, which he found intellectually deadening and left without a degree. He later wrote in the *Wealth of Nations* that most Oxford professors had long since given up even the pretence of teaching. Returning to Scotland, he gave public lectures in Edinburgh from 1748, on rhetoric and literature among other subjects, and there began his friendship with [David Hume](david-hume.md), twelve years his senior, which became the most important of his life.

In 1751 he was appointed professor at Glasgow, first of logic, then from 1752 of moral philosophy, the chair Hutcheson had held. He later called his thirteen years at Glasgow the most useful and happiest period of his life. His course covered natural theology, ethics, jurisprudence and the economic policy of states, so economics grew up inside moral philosophy. The ethics part became the *Theory of Moral Sentiments*, warmly received on publication in 1759.

In 1764 he resigned to tutor the young Duke of Buccleuch on a European tour, a post carrying a generous lifetime pension that bought him independence. Bored in Toulouse, he wrote to Hume that he had begun a book to pass the time: the first sign of the *Wealth of Nations*. In Geneva he visited [Voltaire](bayle-and-voltaire.md) several times; in Paris he met Quesnay, Turgot and the other French "economists" (the physiocrats), whose view that only agriculture creates wealth he later criticized. The tour ended abruptly in 1766, and Smith spent most of the next decade at home in Kirkcaldy drafting the *Wealth of Nations*. Published in 1776, it made him famous across Europe.

The same year Hume died. Smith's public letter on his friend's death, published with Hume's short autobiography, described the famous unbeliever as "approaching as nearly to the idea of a perfectly wise and virtuous man as perhaps the nature of human frailty will permit" (letter to William Strahan, 1776, printed in Rae's *Life*, ch. 19). It brought Smith a storm of clerical abuse. From 1778 he served as a commissioner of customs in Edinburgh, and in 1787 Glasgow University elected him its Rector.

He spent his last years revising both books. The sixth edition of the *Theory of Moral Sentiments* (1790) contains major additions written in full knowledge of the *Wealth of Nations*, including a new Part VI on the character of virtue. He died in Edinburgh on 17 July 1790. On his deathbed he ordered his unfinished manuscripts burned, a loss scholars still regret; the surviving lecture notes, published posthumously, show that his Glasgow course had unified ethics, law, government, and economics in a single framework.

## Major theories and concepts

### Sympathy and fellow-feeling

The *Theory of Moral Sentiments* opens with a claim against pure egoism: however selfish man may be supposed, "there are evidently some principles in his nature, which interest him in the fortune of others." Smith's mechanism is **sympathy** — not pity, but the imaginative fellow-feeling by which we place ourselves in another's situation and feel, in weaker form, what we conceive they feel. Sympathy follows the *situation*, not just the visible emotion: we can blush for someone who behaves shamefully even when they feel no shame themselves.

Moral judgment arises from a match or mismatch. We approve of someone's grief, anger, or joy when, imagining ourselves in their place, we find our own sympathetic emotion coincides with theirs; Smith calls this fit **propriety**. Both sides adjust. The person concerned tones their feeling down to a pitch the spectator can follow, and the spectator works to imagine the situation more fully. The effort to meet in the middle produces two families of virtue, the "awful" virtues of self-command and the "amiable" virtues of sensitivity and humanity.

Because sympathy is projective, it is fallible — we sympathise more readily with joy than sorrow, with the near than the distant, and with the rich and powerful more than the poor, a deformation Smith flags as "the great and most universal cause of the corruption of our moral sentiments." The tension is built in: the same mechanism that makes morality possible also bends it toward rank and wealth.

### Smith between Hutcheson and Hume

Smith's theory is best read as a revision of his two great predecessors. **Hutcheson** had argued that we perceive virtue through a special **moral sense**, a faculty somewhat like sight or hearing, and that virtue consists in benevolence. Smith admired him, calling him the most acute and judicious defender of the benevolence system (TMS VII.ii.3), but he rejected both claims. A distinct moral sense, he argued, is unnecessary once sympathy explains approval, and benevolence alone cannot account for the virtues of prudence, justice and self-command.

**Hume** had also built ethics on sympathy, but for Hume sympathy is a kind of emotional contagion, and what we approve in the end is a trait's usefulness or agreeableness. Smith keeps sympathy and changes it into an act of imagination aimed at a situation. He also argues that utility comes second. We first approve of a judgment or a taste because it is "just" and fits its object; "the idea of the utility of all qualities of this kind, is plainly an after-thought, and not what first recommended them to our approbation" (TMS I.i.4.4). In Part IV he names Hume as the "ingenious and agreeable author" who reduced approval of virtue to a perception of utility, and answers him.

One objection, usually traced to a letter from Hume in 1759, was that if approval rests on sympathy, sympathy with someone's pain ought to make approval painful. Smith added a footnote to the second edition separating two things: the sympathetic feeling itself, which may be painful, and the pleasant feeling of noticing that it matches the other person's.

### The impartial spectator

To correct sympathy's biases, Smith introduces his central device: the **impartial spectator**, the imagined standpoint of a fully informed, disinterested observer within the self — "the man within the breast." Conscience is the habit of judging our own conduct as this spectator would. We learn the standpoint socially, by seeing ourselves through others' eyes, then internalize it so that we can judge ourselves even when actual spectators are fooled or absent.

The device lets him explain how morality can be both socially formed and genuinely self-critical, and why the desire at morality's root is not merely to be praised but to be *praiseworthy*. "Man naturally desires, not only to be loved, but to be lovely," he wrote in the 1790 edition (TMS III.2.1). His example is praise for something we did not do: whoever applauds us for it, Smith says, "applauds not us, but another person," so it gives us no real satisfaction (TMS III.2). General moral rules, for Smith, crystallize out of repeated spectator judgments, and we lean on them when self-love clouds a particular case. The internal tension is how impartial the spectator can be when it is built out of a particular society's reactions. Smith's 1790 additions stress the gap between the spectator within and the actual crowd, perhaps to answer exactly this worry.

### Division of labor and the extent of the market

The *Wealth of Nations* begins with the division of labor. In a small pin factory Smith had seen, ten workers, some of them performing two or three of about eighteen operations, made upwards of forty-eight thousand pins a day; working alone, each might hardly have made twenty. Specialization raises productivity through dexterity, saved time, and the invention of machinery. Exchange arises from a propensity to "truck, barter, and exchange," and since specialization only pays when there are buyers for the extra output, the division of labor is limited by the extent of the market (WN I.iii).

The tension is one Smith states himself. In Book V, he warns that a worker confined to a few simple operations risks becoming "as stupid and ignorant as it is possible for a human creature to become," an evil public education must counter. The engine of growth is also a threat to the mind of the worker who drives it.

### Self-interest, markets, and the invisible hand

Markets coordinate self-interested action: it is not from the benevolence of the butcher, brewer, or baker that we expect dinner, but from their regard to their own interest. Prices settle around a "natural price" through competition, and capital, left free, tends to flow where it is most productive. His policy conclusions follow: restrict monopoly and mercantile privilege, let prices and wages settle competitively, and confine the state to defense, justice, public works, and education. Crucially, his subject throughout is not greed but the whole institutional order — law, custom, and government — within which self-interest operates.

Three times across his works Smith uses the phrase **invisible hand** for such unintended social benefits of individual action, and only once in each place. In the early "History of Astronomy" it is a joke about superstition: no one thought "the invisible hand of Jupiter" was at work when fire burned or water refreshed, only in thunder and storms. In the *Theory of Moral Sentiments* (IV.1.10) the rich, pursuing their own vanity, are "led by an invisible hand" to share the necessaries of life with the poor almost as if the earth had been divided equally. In the *Wealth of Nations* (IV.ii.9) a merchant who prefers domestic to foreign industry for his own security is led to promote the public interest without intending it. The phrase became a slogan only long after Smith; in his own work it marks a pattern he took seriously, unintended order, without being the name of a doctrine.

### Justice, history, and the duties of the state

Smith separates **justice** from the other virtues. Beneficence is "the ornament which embellishes, not the foundation which supports the building"; justice, by contrast, is "the main pillar that upholds the whole edifice" (TMS II.ii.3.4). Justice is mainly negative: not injuring others in person, property or reputation. That is why it can be enforced by law while kindness cannot. Commerce depends on it.

His lectures add a historical frame, the **four stages** theory: societies move from hunting to pasturage, agriculture and commerce, and each mode of subsistence brings its own forms of property, law and government. Commercial society, on this account, brought something Smith valued above its wealth: it gradually ended the personal dependence of tenants and servants on great lords. The tension is that the same society produces the merchants' "conspiracy against the public" that Smith attacks, and the stunted worker of Book V.

## Key works

- *The Theory of Moral Sentiments* (1759; 6th edition 1790) — sympathy, the impartial spectator, virtue, and the corruption of morals by wealth and rank.
- *An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations* (1776) — division of labor, price formation, the critique of mercantilism, taxation, colonies, and the duties of the state.
- *Lectures on Jurisprudence* (student notes, published 1896/1978) — the Glasgow lectures linking property, government, and the four-stages theory of society.
- *Lectures on Rhetoric and Belles Lettres* (student notes of 1762–63, rediscovered in the twentieth century) — his teaching on style, communication and literature.
- *Essays on Philosophical Subjects* (1795, posthumous) — including his "History of Astronomy," a study of how scientific systems arise to soothe the imagination.

## In their own words

> How selfish soever man may be supposed, there are evidently some principles in his nature, which interest him in the fortune of others, and render their happiness necessary to him, though he derives nothing from it, except the pleasure of seeing it.

— Adam Smith, *The Theory of Moral Sentiments* I.i.1.1 (1759; public domain)

> It is reason, principle, conscience, the inhabitant of the breast, the man within, the great judge and arbiter of our conduct.

— Adam Smith, *The Theory of Moral Sentiments* III.3.4 (public domain)

> It is not from the benevolence of the butcher, the brewer, or the baker that we expect our dinner, but from their regard to their own interest. We address ourselves, not to their humanity, but to their self-love, and never talk to them of our own necessities, but of their advantages.

— Adam Smith, *Wealth of Nations* I.ii.2 (1776; public domain)

> By pursuing his own interest, he frequently promotes that of the society more effectually than when he really intends to promote it.

— Adam Smith, *Wealth of Nations* IV.ii.9 (1776; public domain)

> People of the same trade seldom meet together, even for merriment and diversion, but the conversation ends in a conspiracy against the public, or in some contrivance to raise prices.

— Adam Smith, *Wealth of Nations* I.x.c.27 (1776; public domain)

Read the first and third together. The butcher passage describes how we bargain with strangers, not what human nature is. The last is a reminder that the same self-interest which feeds us also tempts sellers to rig the market.

## Contributions

Smith created modern economics as a systematic discipline while simultaneously producing one of the eighteenth century's most sophisticated moral psychologies. The impartial spectator remains a live model of conscience and moral learning, and his analysis of unintended social order — how institutions channel private action into public outcomes — shaped sociology and political theory as much as economics. He also gave the "four stages" account of how economic life shapes law and government, a forerunner of later historical sociology.

## Impact and legacy

The *Wealth of Nations* became the canonical text of classical economics, shaping nineteenth-century liberal policy and every subsequent debate about markets and the state; Ricardo, Mill, and Marx all built on or against it. The *Theory of Moral Sentiments* was eclipsed for a century by its sibling's fame, then revived by twentieth-century scholarship as a major work in its own right, influencing virtue ethics, moral psychology, and Amartya Sen's work on justice and identity. The Glasgow Edition of his works and correspondence (from 1976), which added the rediscovered lecture notes, made it possible to read him as one system rather than two famous books.

## Criticisms and debates

- **The "Adam Smith problem."** The most famous debate is the so-called **"Adam Smith problem"** (*das Adam Smith Problem*), posed by nineteenth-century German scholars: the *Theory* seems to found morality on sympathy and benevolence, the *Wealth* on self-interest — contradiction? One early version said Smith changed his mind after meeting French materialists in Paris in 1764–66.
  - *The case for a real tension:* the *Wealth* speaks almost entirely of self-love, and the butcher passage seems to say benevolence plays no role in economic life.
  - *The reply:* the modern consensus largely dissolves the problem. Self-interest in Smith is one motive among several, and prudence is itself a virtue in the *Theory*. Sympathy is a mechanism of judgment rather than benevolence itself. The Glasgow lecture notes of 1762–63 already contain the economics, before the Paris trip. Both books were revised together across his life, and the *Wealth* presupposes the legal and moral framework the *Theory* describes.
  - *What is still argued:* some scholars think a softer version survives, for example in how the *Theory*'s admiration for self-command and its warnings about vanity sit with the *Wealth*'s praise of growth driven by the desire to better our condition.
- **The invisible hand.** Genuine debates remain about how far his invisible hand was a considered doctrine or a passing metaphor. Some read it as a providential design argument; others as irony, or as a handy name for unintended consequences.
- **Labor and value.** Economists still debate whether his labor theory of value survives his own qualifications, and Ricardo and Marx each developed it in a different direction.
- **How egalitarian?** Readers disagree about how egalitarian his economics really is, given his sharp remarks on landlords, merchants' conspiracies against the public, and the stunting effects of repetitive labor. Some read him as a friend of the poor; others stress his acceptance of rank.

## Misreadings to avoid

- Smith did not teach that selfishness is good or sufficient: markets in his account run inside justice — justice is, in his words, "the main pillar that upholds the whole edifice" of society — and he distrusted businessmen's political influence.
- The invisible hand is a minor phrase (used once in each major work, plus once in the "History of Astronomy"), not the title of a theory.
- The *Wealth of Nations* is not a brief for a minimal state: Smith assigns government defense, justice, infrastructure, and education, and supports some interventions, including public schooling and usury laws.
- Smith's "sympathy" is not pity or kindness; it is the imaginative act of taking up another's situation, and it works for joy and anger as well as sorrow.
- The impartial spectator is not an ideal observer outside society; it is a standpoint each person builds from real social experience and then turns on themselves.

## What to read first

1. *The Theory of Moral Sentiments*, Part I, Section I — sympathy and propriety.
2. *Theory of Moral Sentiments*, Part III, chapters 1–3 — the impartial spectator and conscience.
3. *Wealth of Nations*, Book I, chapters 1–3 — division of labor and the extent of the market.
4. *Wealth of Nations*, Book IV, chapter 2, and Book V, chapter 1, part 3 — the attack on mercantilism, the invisible hand in context, and education. Reading the two books together is the surest cure for the caricature.

## Study questions

1. How does Smith's sympathy differ from Hume's? Use the example of blushing for someone who feels no shame.
2. Why does Smith think we want to be praiseworthy and not only praised? What role does the impartial spectator play in that?
3. Smith says sympathy bends toward the rich and powerful. Does the impartial spectator correct this bias, or is it shaped by the same society that produces it?
4. Read the three "invisible hand" passages side by side. Do they express one idea, or three different ones?
5. State the "Adam Smith problem" in your own words. Which reply to it do you find strongest, and why?
6. Why does Smith treat justice differently from benevolence, and what follows for what a government may enforce?
