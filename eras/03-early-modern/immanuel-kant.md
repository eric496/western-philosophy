# Immanuel Kant

*Era: Early Modern Philosophy (c. 1600 – 1800) · [Era overview](README.md) · [Master index](../../index.md)*

## At a glance

- **Lifespan:** 1724–1804
- **Place:** Königsberg, East Prussia (now Kaliningrad) — born, educated, taught, and died there
- **Tradition:** Critical philosophy; a synthesis of rationalism and empiricism
- **Known for:** The mind structures experience through space, time, and the categories; morality as a law rational agents give themselves; reason learning its own limits

## Biography

Kant was born in Königsberg in 1724, the fourth of nine children of a harness-maker. The household was shaped by Pietism, a devout, inward-looking Lutheranism stressing conscience and moral discipline over ceremony. Kant later left Pietist practice behind, but its seriousness about self-examination marks his ethics.

He entered the University of Königsberg at sixteen, studying philosophy, mathematics, and natural science under teachers formed by Christian Wolff, the German heir of Leibniz, and by Newtonian physics. After several years as a private tutor — one of his few periods away from Königsberg — he became a *Privatdozent* in 1755, an unsalaried lecturer paid by his students. He lectured for fifteen years on logic, metaphysics, ethics, geography, anthropology, and physics before receiving a professorship in 1770, aged forty-six. Stories of walks so regular that neighbours set their watches by them caricature a genuinely disciplined later life; students remembered a lively, witty teacher. He never married.

The decisive change was his “critical turn,” roughly 1770–81. Kant later said reading Hume interrupted his “dogmatic slumber”: Hume’s scepticism about causation showed that the Wolffian metaphysics he had been taught assumed what needed proving. His inaugural dissertation of 1770 began separating sensibility from understanding, and after a decade of near-silence in print he published the *Critique of Pure Reason* in 1781. Misunderstood at first, it prompted the clearer *Prolegomena* (1783) and a substantially revised second edition in 1787.

The practical philosophy followed: *Groundwork* (1785), *Critique of Practical Reason* (1788), and *Critique of Judgment* (1790). Late in life he collided with the state. *Religion within the Bounds of Bare Reason* (1793), which treated revealed religion as a vehicle for moral faith, drew an order from the Prussian authorities under Frederick William II, in a period of censorship, not to write or lecture further on religion. Kant complied while that king lived — arguing his promise had been made to that king personally — and resumed after the king’s death in 1797. He retired in 1796 and died in Königsberg in 1804.

## Major theories and concepts

### Synthetic a priori knowledge

A judgment is *analytic* if the predicate is already contained in the subject (“all bachelors are unmarried”) and *synthetic* if it adds something. Knowledge is *a posteriori* if it rests on experience, *a priori* if it does not depend on any particular experience. Kant’s explosive claim is that there are *synthetic a priori* judgments: claims that extend knowledge yet hold necessarily. His examples include arithmetic and geometry, and “every event has a cause.” Hume had shown experience never gives necessity — we see one billiard ball follow another, never the *must*. So how are such judgments possible at all? That question drives the first *Critique*.

### The Copernican revolution

Earlier philosophy assumed cognition must conform to objects. Kant proposes the reverse: objects *of experience* conform to our cognition — as Copernicus explained the heavens better by moving the observer rather than the stars. A camera does not invent a scene, but every photo bears the marks of its lens and format. Likewise the mind does not create the world, but anything we can experience must arrive in a form our faculties can receive and organise. Hence we can know a priori the *structure* experience must have — because we contribute it — while its content comes from outside us.

### Forms of intuition and the categories

Kant distinguishes *sensibility*, which receives impressions, from *understanding*, which thinks about them. “Thoughts without content are empty; intuitions without concepts are blind.” Experience needs both.

Sensibility has two a priori *forms of intuition*: space, the form of outer sense, and time, the form of inner sense. They are not things perceived among other things, nor concepts learned from experience — we cannot represent anything outside us except in space, or our own states except as successive in time. Hence geometry and arithmetic seemed to Kant both necessary and informative: they articulate these forms.

The understanding contributes twelve *categories* — pure concepts such as unity, substance, causality, and necessity. They are rules for unifying impressions into experience of objects: to experience an *event*, rather than a mere sequence of impressions, is already to apply causality. His “Transcendental Deduction” argues the categories legitimately apply to anything that can be an object for a self-conscious mind — the “I think” that must be able to accompany all my representations.

### Transcendental idealism: phenomena and noumena

Because we know objects only as structured by our forms and categories, we know *phenomena* — things as they appear under the conditions of human cognition. *Noumena*, or things in themselves, are things considered apart from those conditions. The noumenon is primarily a *limit-concept*: it marks where knowledge stops, not a secret second world we can describe.

Kant pairs this with *empirical realism*. Within experience, tables, planets, and causes are perfectly real, public, and law-governed; space and time are empirically real though transcendentally ideal. Kant angrily rejected the label of Berkeley-style idealist — in the *Prolegomena* and the B-edition “Refutation of Idealism” he argues outer experience is immediate, and that consciousness of my own existence in time presupposes something permanent outside me. He does **not** hold that the world is a dream inside individual minds, and he rejected that label himself.

### The antinomies

When reason treats the world as a completed totality beyond possible experience, it generates *antinomies*: apparently rigorous proofs for contradictory conclusions. Is the world finite or infinite? Is everything causally determined, or is there freedom? Is there a necessary being, or not? For Kant the collisions are diagnostic: both sides assume the world-as-a-whole is a knowable object, which transcendental idealism denies. Distinguishing phenomena from things in themselves dissolves some antinomies and leaves logical room — though no proof — for freedom.

### The categorical imperative

Some requirements, Kant thinks, are not merely advisable but unconditional. A *hypothetical* imperative says: if you want X, do Y. A *categorical* imperative commands regardless of your wants.

Its universal-law formulation: “Act only in accordance with that maxim through which you can at the same time will that it become a universal law.” A *maxim* is your act’s subjective principle — “In circumstances C, I will do A to achieve P.” The test asks whether you can consistently will everyone to act on it. A lying promise fails: universalised, promises would cease to be believed, so the maxim destroys the practice it depends on.

Its humanity formulation: “Act in such a way that you treat humanity, whether in your own person or in the person of any other, always at the same time as an end and never merely as a means.” Using someone as a means is ordinary — you use a taxi driver’s services — but never *merely* as a means: deception and coercion bypass rational consent and treat a person as a tool only.

This is a *test of maxims*, not the Golden Rule and not a rule list. Kant distinguishes it from “do as you would be done by,” which is indexed to what you happen to want and, he thought, cannot ground duties to oneself or strict duties of justice. Particular duties must be derived, and Kant’s own strict applications — notoriously on lying — are his applications, debated even by Kantians.

### Autonomy

Why is a rational being bound by this law? *Autonomy*: the will gives the moral law to itself. This does not mean inventing values to suit yourself. It means being bound not by inclination, custom, authority, or consequences, but by a law issuing from your own reason — a law any rational agent would give itself. Its opposite, *heteronomy*, takes the law from outside reason. Autonomy grounds dignity: persons have no mere price because they are legislators of the moral law, not only its subjects.

### Postulates of practical reason

Theoretical reason could not prove freedom, God, or immortality — and critique shows why it never will. But practical reason *postulates* them as necessary conditions of morality. We must act under the idea of freedom, since the moral law addresses agents who can act from duty. Morality aims at the highest good — virtue with happiness proportioned to it — which nature and a single lifetime cannot guarantee, so reason postulates immortality and a God who can secure that harmony. These are rational *faith*, not knowledge: Kant’s remark that he had to “deny knowledge in order to make room for faith” names exactly this division.

### Perpetual peace and aesthetics, briefly

*Toward Perpetual Peace* (1795) sketches a federation of republican states, cosmopolitan right limited to hospitality, and public law as conditions for ending war — an ancestor of liberal internationalism. The *Critique of Judgment* (1790) analyses beauty as “purposiveness without a purpose” — pleasing universally yet without a concept — and teleological judgment as our necessary but regulative way of thinking about organisms.

## Key works

- *Critique of Pure Reason* (1781, “A”; substantially revised 1787, “B”) — The masterwork on the structure and limits of theoretical knowledge; cited by A/B page.
- *Prolegomena to Any Future Metaphysics* (1783) — Shorter, clearer restatement after the *Critique* was misunderstood; the best entry to his epistemology.
- *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals* (1785) — The categorical imperative and autonomy in three compact sections.
- *Critique of Practical Reason* (1788) — Systematic ethics: the moral law, freedom, and the postulates.
- *Critique of Judgment* (1790) — Aesthetics and teleology; completes the three Critiques.
- *Religion within the Bounds of Bare Reason* (1793) — Moral faith versus revealed religion; triggered Prussian censorship.
- *Toward Perpetual Peace* (1795) — Conditions for lasting international peace, in a partly ironic treaty format.

## Contributions

Kant’s first contribution is the critical method: before asking what we know, ask what makes such knowledge possible and where its jurisdiction ends. Second, he made the mind active in knowledge — neither a passive copy of the world nor spinning truth from reason alone, experience is given content in contributed form. Third, he gave modern ethics its most influential non-consequentialist foundation: morality grounded in autonomy and dignity, not divine command, happiness, or sentiment. Fourth, he showed how questions about God, freedom, and the soul could be neither answered by theory nor dismissed, but relocated to the practical standpoint.

## Impact and legacy

Almost every later movement defines itself through or against Kant. Fichte, Schelling, and Hegel tried to remove the unknowable thing in itself and complete his system; Schopenhauer kept it and rebuilt around the will. Neo-Kantian schools dominated German universities in the late nineteenth century, and analytic philosophy inherited his questions about the a priori and the limits of sense. In ethics he underlies modern deontology, human-rights talk of dignity, and Rawls’s political liberalism; his peace proposals fed liberal international theory. Even naturalised modern claims that experience is actively structured are recognisably Kantian in shape.

## Criticisms and debates

Early critics objected that the thing in itself is incoherent: Kant says it affects us, yet causality is valid only within experience — so how can it affect anything? Others ask whether the twelve categories are genuinely derived or borrowed from traditional logic. Hegel charged Kantian morality with empty formalism: critics since argue the universal-law test either rules out too little (cleverly redescribed maxims can pass) or quietly smuggles in consequences. Scholars also debate how to reconcile his universalist ethics with period-bound writings on race and sex containing hierarchical claims in tension with his own principles — whether these are detachable prejudices or symptoms of deeper problems. Interpretation of transcendental idealism itself remains divided between “two-world” readings (separate realms) and “two-aspect” readings (the same things considered two ways).

## Misreadings to avoid

- Kant does not say we can know nothing; science gives genuine knowledge — of phenomena.
- He is not a Berkeley-style idealist, and rejected that label. Minds structure a real, law-governed empirical world; they do not dream it.
- The noumenon is not a hidden parallel world to speculate about. It is a limit-concept.
- The categorical imperative is not the Golden Rule and not a rulebook. It is a test applied to your maxim.
- Autonomy is not doing whatever you choose. It is obedience to a law your own reason gives universally.

## What to read first

1. *Groundwork*, Sections I–II — the ethics in its clearest form.
2. *Prolegomena*, Preamble and Part I — the epistemology without the first *Critique*’s density.
3. *Critique of Pure Reason*, Transcendental Aesthetic and Analytic, in a guided edition — as a capstone.
4. *Toward Perpetual Peace* — short, accessible Kant the political thinker.
