# Thomas Reid

## At a glance

- **Dates:** 1710–1796
- **Place:** Born in Strachan, Kincardineshire; taught at Aberdeen and, from 1764, at Glasgow, Scotland
- **School/tradition:** Scottish Enlightenment; founder of the "common sense" school; direct realism
- **Known for:** Arguing that the "Way of Ideas" from Descartes to Hume rests on an unproved assumption, and answering it with direct realism, first principles of common sense, and agent causation

**Responds to:** [Descartes](rene-descartes.md), [Locke](john-locke.md), [Berkeley](george-berkeley.md), [Hume](david-hume.md) · **Answered by:** [Kant](immanuel-kant.md), [Peirce](../04-19th-century/charles-sanders-peirce.md), [G. E. Moore](../05-20th-century/g-e-moore.md)

## Biography

Thomas Reid was born on 26 April 1710 (Old Style calendar) at Strachan, in Kincardineshire, north-east Scotland. His father, Lewis Reid, was minister of the parish for fifty years, and his mother belonged to the Gregory family, which produced several professors of mathematics. At Marischal College, Aberdeen, which he entered in 1722, he studied philosophy under George Turnbull, graduated in 1726, and then trained for the ministry. He worked as the college librarian from 1733 to 1736. There he formed a close friendship with John Stewart, later professor of mathematics at Marischal.

In 1737 he became minister of New Machar, near Aberdeen. The parish was angry about church patronage, and tradition says an uncle guarded the pulpit stairs with a sword. Reid won the parish over, and in 1740 he married his cousin Elizabeth Reid. His first publication, an "Essay on Quantity" (1748), argued against applying mathematics to virtue and merit.

Hume's *Treatise* (1739) is what turned him to philosophy. Reid later described his own project as a response to it: he found Hume's reasoning valid given its starting points, so the starting points had to be examined. In the early 1750s he became a professor of philosophy at King's College, Aberdeen. In 1758 he and his cousin John Gregory founded the Aberdeen Philosophical Society (the "Wise Club"), whose members included James Beattie and George Campbell. Reid's papers there became his *Inquiry into the Human Mind on the Principles of Common Sense* (1764).

Before publication, Reid sent the *Inquiry* to Hume through their friend Hugh Blair. Hume replied courteously, and Reid acknowledged that Hume's writings had prompted his own. The same year Reid succeeded Adam Smith as professor of moral philosophy at Glasgow. There he lectured on natural theology, ethics and politics, and added a course on rhetoric on his own initiative.

He retired from lecturing in 1780 to write up his lectures as the *Essays on the Intellectual Powers of Man* (1785) and *Essays on the Active Powers of Man* (1788). His wife died in 1792, and he died of paralysis on 7 October 1796. Dugald Stewart's life of Reid, published in 1803, is the original biographical authority.

## Major theories and concepts

### The critique of the "Way of Ideas"

Reid's name for the target of his criticism is the **Way of Ideas** (or "ideal theory"). It is the view, shared by Descartes, Locke, Berkeley and Hume, that the immediate objects of the mind are always mental items called ideas. When I seem to see an apple, what I immediately perceive is an idea of the apple; the apple itself is at best inferred.

Reid argues that this view has no good argument behind it. Philosophers simply take it for granted. In his words, ordinary speech about "having an idea of" something involves a mind, an act of thinking, and an object thought about. Philosophers add "a fourth, to wit, the idea, which is the immediate object" (*Essays on the Intellectual Powers*, Essay I, ch. 1, as printed in the 1859 Walker edition). That fourth item rests on a philosophical opinion, not on observation.

He then presses two further points. An idea standing between mind and world explains nothing, since how a mind connects to an idea is no clearer than how it connects to an object. And the view leads to skepticism: if we perceive only ideas, we cannot infer their causes in the world, because, by Hume's own account of causation, such inferences require repeated experience of both cause and effect, and we never experience the cause. Berkeley avoids the problem by treating objects as collections of ideas, which Reid regards as a violation of common sense, though he admired how thoroughly Berkeley pursued the idea.

Reid also appealed to Newton's first rule of reasoning, which admits no causes beyond those true and sufficient to explain the appearances. Ideas, he argued, are a hypothesis with no evidence behind them.

### Direct realism: sensation and perception

Reid's own account distinguishes **sensation** from **perception**. A sensation is a feeling tied to a sense organ, such as the pain of being hit by a thrown ball. A perception of an object involves, as he puts it, three things: some conception of the object, a strong and irresistible belief in its present existence, and the fact that this belief is immediate, not the result of reasoning (*Intellectual Powers*, Walker ed., pp. 57–59).

How do sensations relate to perceptions? Reid says sensations function as **natural signs**. When I grasp an ivory ball, I feel a certain sensation of touch. The sensation is in my mind and resembles nothing material, yet by the laws of my constitution it is at once followed by the conception and belief that I hold a hard, smooth, round body. Reid says this belief is "grounded neither upon reasoning nor upon experience" (Walker ed., p. 194). He calls the process **suggestion**. Sensation suggests the quality of the object, as a word suggests its meaning, without resembling it.

He supports this with a thought experiment. Imagine an adult who has lost all memory of touch and extension and must relearn the world from sensations alone, such as a pinprick or a blunt object pressed against the skin. Reid argues that these sensations would not yield the idea of extension or shape. Since we do have these ideas, they are not copied from sensations, as Hume's copy principle claims, and the link must be an original feature of our constitution.

### Original and acquired perception

Reid divides perceptions into **original** and **acquired**. Original perceptions need no learning. Touch originally gives us three-dimensional shape, hardness and distance, while sight originally gives only two-dimensional visible figure, magnitude and angular position. The rest we learn. Smell, taste and hearing originally give only sensations, and we learn by experience that the fragrance comes from a rose.

Acquired perception happens so fast we mistake it for original. Looking at an ivory ball, I seem simply to see that it is round, but only because experience has linked the look of shading with the feel of a sphere. A sphere painted on a flat surface can therefore fool us. Reid says there is no fallacy in the original perception, only in what custom has added.

### The geometry of visibles

To answer Hume's argument from perceptual relativity, Reid worked out the mathematics of how things look. Hume had said that the table we see shrinks as we step back, while the real table does not change, so what we see must be an image in the mind. Reid replied that Hume confuses **apparent magnitude** with **real magnitude**. The visible figure of the table is a relation between the table and the eye. Reid showed that it varies in a regular way with the table's shape and size and with its distance and orientation, and that the variation can be calculated by geometry, as shapes projected onto the surface of a sphere. That regularity, he argued, is evidence that visible figure is objective, not a private image.

Scholars ask whether Reid in effect described a non-Euclidean geometry, and whether his visible figures are a kind of intermediary that brings back part of what he rejected.

### First principles of common sense

When reason can go no further, Reid says, we rely on **first principles**, beliefs given by our constitution that need no proof. His list for contingent truths includes that the things we distinctly perceive really exist and are as we perceive them, that things we distinctly remember really happened, and that we have a continuing personal identity. Such principles have typical marks: denying them is not merely false but absurd; they are believed as soon as understood; and they are shared across ages and nations and show up in the structure of all languages.

Reid does not claim that majority opinion proves anything. He treats the universality of certain beliefs as an observation about human cognition. His central argument against the skeptic is a parity argument. The skeptic asks why I trust perception, and Reid asks why I should trust reason more. Both faculties come from the same maker, and a maker who gave us one faulty faculty might have given us another. To overthrow perception the skeptic must show that reason has firmer credentials, and cannot, because the principles of logic are themselves things we cannot help believing. Common sense, on this view, constrains philosophy and does not prove its conclusions.

Reid's theory of empirical knowledge is usually classed as externalist: a belief can be justified without the believer being able to cite further reasons for it.

### Causation, active power and agent causation

Reid accepts part of Hume's negative case. We have no sensory experience of one event necessitating another. He rejects the lesson Hume draws, that our idea of causation is therefore mistaken. Our concept of cause, like our concept of an external object, does not resemble the sensations that lead us to it, and that is no reason to doubt it. He also objects to Hume's definition of cause as constant conjunction: it would make anything first of its kind causeless, and it would make night the cause of day, since nothing has followed each other more regularly.

Reid distinguishes **physical causes**, which are laws of nature, from **efficient causes**. Laws, he says, are rules by which effects are produced, and rules produce nothing themselves; behind them must stand something with power. An efficient cause is a being who has the power to produce an effect and exerts it. For Reid, only minds have such active power, since our only clear conception of power comes from our own willing. Free action, which Reid calls **moral liberty**, is action of which the agent is the efficient cause. This is Reid's **agent-causal** theory of free will: the cause of a free action is the agent, not an earlier event.

Against the worry that motives determine action, he argues that the necessitarians' own slogan — "when there are contrary motives, the strongest must prevail" (*Essays on the Active Powers*, Essay IV, ch. 4) — is trivial if "strongest" just means the one that wins, and false if it means the strongest urge or the strongest rational consideration, since we sometimes act against both.

### Memory and personal identity

Reid gave memory a direct theory too: what we remember is the past event itself, not a present idea of it. Distinct memory, like perception, comes with belief that cannot be justified without circularity, since any proof of memory's reliability would rely on memory.

He turned this against Locke's account of personal identity, which makes sameness of person depend on consciousness of past actions. Reid offered three objections: Locke runs consciousness and memory together; his theory violates transitivity; and it is circular, an objection Reid credits to [Bishop Butler](moral-sense-school.md). His famous illustration is the **brave officer** (quoted below): a boy is flogged, becomes a young officer who remembers the flogging, and then a general who remembers the young officer's exploit but has forgotten the flogging. By Locke's doctrine, Reid argues, the general is the same person as the young officer, who is the same person as the boy. Yet the general lacks consciousness of the flogging, so by the same doctrine he is not the boy. The general "is, and at the same time is not" the same person as the one flogged (*Intellectual Powers*, Essay III, ch. 6). Identity is transitive, so a theory that breaks transitivity cannot be about real identity.

Reid's own view is that a person is an indivisible, continuing thing that thinks, acts and suffers, and he says little more about its nature in his published works.

## Key works

- *An Essay on Quantity* (1748) — His first publication, on whether mathematics applies to ethics.
- *An Inquiry into the Human Mind on the Principles of Common Sense* (1764) — His most original work: an analysis of the senses and the case against the Way of Ideas.
- *Essays on the Intellectual Powers of Man* (1785) — Perception, memory, first principles, personal identity.
- *Essays on the Active Powers of Man* (1788) — Power, will, motives, liberty, morality.

## In their own words

> Suppose a brave officer to have been flogged when a boy at school for robbing an orchard, to have taken a standard from the enemy in his first campaign, and to have been made a general in advanced life; suppose, also, which must be admitted to be possible, that, when he took the standard, he was conscious of his having been flogged at school, and that, when made a general, he was conscious of his taking the standard, but had absolutely lost the consciousness of his flogging.

— Thomas Reid, *Essays on the Intellectual Powers of Man*, Essay III, ch. 6 (1785; public domain; text as printed in the 1859 Walker edition, pp. 248–249)

Each link in the chain is plausible alone, and the contradiction appears only when they are joined. Which link should Locke give up?

## Contributions

Reid built a systematic case for **direct realism**, diagnosed the Way of Ideas as one shared mistake, worked out the geometry of visible appearances, and defended a fallible **foundationalism** resting on first principles. He developed an **agent-causal** theory of free will and the **brave officer** objection.

## Impact and legacy

Reid was the leading figure of the Scottish "common sense" school, which included James Beattie and Dugald Stewart, who attended Reid's lectures in 1772 and accepted most of his views. Sir William Hamilton, who edited Reid's *Works* (1846), tried to combine Reid with Kant. Through Royer-Collard the Scottish philosophy reached France, where Victor Cousin, who later turned to Schelling and Hegel, absorbed much of Reid, and Théodore Jouffroy translated his works into French (1828–36). An American editor writing in 1850 said that the psychology generally taught in England and the United States for the previous fifty years had been that of the Scottish school, of which Reid was the acknowledged head.

[Charles Sanders Peirce](../04-19th-century/charles-sanders-peirce.md) called his own view **Critical Common-sensism**, a blend of Kant and Reid, while judging Reid's version insufficiently fallibilist: Peirce held that even our most secure beliefs must stay open to criticism. In "A Defence of Common Sense" (1925), [G. E. Moore](../05-20th-century/g-e-moore.md) began by listing truisms that he claimed to know for certain. Reid's link between ordinary language and common sense influenced Moore and J. L. Austin ([Austin & Strawson](../05-20th-century/austin-and-strawson.md)).

Contemporary epistemology has revisited Reid as an early externalist. Nicholas Wolterstorff's *Thomas Reid and the Story of Epistemology* (Cambridge University Press, 2001) connects Reid to present-day debates, and Reid's account of non-inferential belief in God, modeled on perception, anticipates the parity argument in Alvin Plantinga's *God and Other Minds*. His agent-causal account of freedom has also been used to respond to the problem of moral evil.

## Criticisms and debates

- **Kant's complaint.** In the preface to the *Prolegomena* ([Kant](immanuel-kant.md)), Kant named Reid, Oswald, Beattie and Priestley as critics of Hume who, instead of examining reason, took the easy route of appealing to common sense, which he treated as an appeal to the multitude. The 1911 *Britannica* replies that Reid's real work was to attack Hume's premises, and notes that he and Kant agree on some points about space and judgment.
- **Too many principles.** Joseph Priestley (1774) counted thirteen principles of common sense in the *Inquiry* and called them arbitrary and instinctive. Reid gives typical marks of a first principle, not a strict test, so the list is open to this charge.
- **What does "direct" mean?** If we perceive visible figures and sensations as signs, has Reid brought back an intermediary? Interpreters disagree.
- **Brave officer.** Reid was not the first critic of Locke's account. The editors' notes in the 1859 Walker edition mention earlier objections by John Sergeant and by Leibniz. Reid also observes that Locke himself saw some strange consequences of his doctrine, for example that twenty different beings might be one person.
- **Agent causation.** Many philosophers hold that every apparent case of an agent causing something is really a case of events causing events. Reid's view requires a basic causal relation between an agent and her action, and whether that relation is intelligible is a standing dispute.

## Misreadings to avoid

- **"Reid thinks whatever most people believe is true."** He treats universal belief as evidence about how human cognition works, and the first principles have to satisfy further tests.
- **"Common sense means unreflective opinion."** Reid meant beliefs common to rational beings as such, and he defended them with experiments and geometry.
- **"Direct realism means we never make mistakes."** Reid's acquired perception accounts for illusions like the painted sphere.
- **"Reid never understood Hume."** He accepted that Hume's reasoning follows from its starting points, and his reply was to deny those points.

## What to read first

1. *Inquiry into the Human Mind*, the chapters on touch and on seeing, including the closing sections on skepticism — sensation and perception, the geometry of visibles, and the reply to the skeptic.
2. *Essays on the Intellectual Powers*, Essay I, Chapter 1 — the account of "idea" — and Essay II on perception.
3. *Intellectual Powers*, Essay VI, Chapters 4–5 — the marks and list of first principles.
4. *Intellectual Powers*, Essay III, Chapter 6 — the brave officer.
5. *Essays on the Active Powers*, Essay I, Chapter 5, and Essay IV — power, motives, and liberty.
