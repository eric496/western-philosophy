# Jean-Jacques Rousseau

*Era: Early Modern Philosophy (c. 1600 – 1800) · [Era overview](README.md) · [Master index](../../index.md)*

## At a glance

1712–1778 · Geneva and France · Social-contract theorist, philosopher of education and human nature · Known for “Man is born free, and everywhere he is in chains,” natural human goodness, and the general will in *The Social Contract*.

## Biography

Jean-Jacques Rousseau was born on 28 June 1712 in Geneva, then an independent Calvinist republic. His mother died days after his birth; his watchmaker father fled Geneva in 1722, leaving the boy to unhappy apprenticeships. At sixteen Rousseau left Geneva and began years of wandering in Savoy and Italy, taken up by the Baronne de Warens, converting to Catholicism (later reversed), and educating himself as tutor, secretary, and music copyist. With Thérèse Levasseur, whom he eventually married, he had five children whom he consigned to a foundling hospital — a decision he defended and regretted in shifting ways, and which enemies never let him forget.

Fame came suddenly. In 1749, walking to visit the imprisoned Diderot, he read the Academy of Dijon’s prize question: had the arts and sciences improved morals? His answer — no — won, and the *Discourse on the Sciences and Arts* (1750) made him famous. The *Discourse on the Origin of Inequality* (1755) established him as civilisation’s scandalous internal critic. He moved among the *philosophes* before temperament and doctrine produced permanent quarrels.

In 1762 both *Émile* and *The Social Contract* appeared. *Émile*’s “Profession of Faith of the Savoyard Vicar” got the book condemned and ordered burned in Paris; an arrest warrant forced Rousseau to flee, and Geneva burned both books. Exile followed: Neuchâtel, the Île Saint-Pierre, and England in 1766 at David Hume’s invitation. That refuge collapsed into a famous quarrel — Rousseau, convinced of conspiracy, accused Hume of complicity; Hume published their correspondence in self-defence. Rousseau returned to France under a false name, finished the *Confessions*, and died at Ermenonville on 2 July 1778. The *Confessions* appeared posthumously (1782/1789). In 1794 the Revolution moved his remains to the Panthéon.

## Major theories and concepts

### Natural human goodness

Against original sin and Hobbes’s fearful natural man, Rousseau holds that humans are naturally good — not virtuous, since virtue requires reason and society, but uncorrupted: our original impulses do not aim at harm. Corruption is society’s artefact. Moral philosophy’s task thus changes: not disciplining a fallen nature, but understanding how good natures become vicious. Tension: the claim rests on conjectural history, inviting the charge that “natural goodness” is unfalsifiable.

### Self-preservation and pity

Natural man is moved by two principles prior to reason: concern for his own preservation, and pity — a natural repugnance at seeing a sentient being suffer, from which the social virtues later grow. Pity tempers self-preservation without calculation. Hobbes, Rousseau argues, missed pity entirely, and so built war into a nature containing the seed of peace.

### *Amour de soi* versus *amour-propre*

Rousseau’s most durable distinction separates *amour de soi* — self-love directed at genuine needs and one’s own good, absolute and in itself good — from *amour-propre*, relative self-love born only in society, which measures the self through others’ eyes and demands preference and rank. *Amour-propre* drives vanity, envy, and domination: I no longer ask “am I fed?” but “am I esteemed above him?” *Émile* is largely the art of delaying and channelling it; *The Social Contract* must give it a civic object. Rousseau is not simple about it: *amour-propre* also sources our sociality and potentially virtue. Whether it can be educated rather than merely feared remains debated.

### Private property and the origin of inequality

The *Discourse on Inequality*, Part II, opens famously: “The first man who, having enclosed a piece of ground, bethought himself of saying *This is mine*, and found people simple enough to believe him, was the true founder of civil society.” Property is no natural right, as in Locke, but a convention that hardened — through agriculture, metallurgy, comparison, and dependence — into inequality of wealth, then power, then domination legitimised by laws favouring the rich.

Two clarifications matter. The state of nature is a diagnostic device: Rousseau says it describes a state that “perhaps never existed,” separating what is original in us from what society added, as a physicist idealises away friction. And he did **not** propose abolishing society or a literal return to nature: language and society cannot be unlearned. The real question is what legitimate order is possible now — *The Social Contract*’s question.

### The general will

The general will is the citizen body’s will directed at the common good, expressed in general laws — laws coming from all, applying to all, naming no particular person. It is defined by its **object**, not by counting: it is *not* majority whim, *not* a vote total, and *not* a leader embodying the people. Rousseau distinguishes it from the “will of all,” the mere sum of private wills, which aggregates selfishness.

A majority can therefore be *wrong* about the general will. Voting, for Rousseau, asks not “what do I want?” but “what is the common good?” — and a majority can be outvoted in error, the losing side taking defeat as evidence it misjudged the common interest. The idea presupposes conditions he lists soberly: rough equality, no entrenched factions, informed citizens, a small polity. Where these fail, the general will falls silent or is counterfeited — his theory carries its own warning label.

### “Forced to be free” and legitimate government

Whoever refuses the general will, Rousseau says, shall be “forced to be free.” In his system freedom is not licence but obedience to a law one has prescribed for oneself. As co-author of the law, the person compelled to obey is held to his own legislation in his public capacity, against private appetite — self-legislation enforced, not submission to an alien power. Whether that distinction bears the weight placed on it is Rousseau interpretation’s central controversy; it depends on the law genuinely being general and genuinely one’s own.

Government, finally, is strictly subordinate. Sovereignty — legislative authority expressing the general will — belongs inalienably to the assembled people and cannot be represented: deputies are agents, never substitutes. (The English, he says, are free only during elections.) Government is a mere executive commission applying the laws, revocable at the people’s pleasure. Magistrates who legislate usurp sovereignty and dissolve the compact.

## Key works

- *Discourse on the Origin of Inequality* (1755) — Conjectural history from natural goodness through property to domination.
- *Émile, or On Education* (1762) — Novel-treatise on raising a child according to nature; condemned and burned in Paris and Geneva, forcing Rousseau to flee arrest.
- *The Social Contract* (1762) — Opens “Man is born free, and everywhere he is in chains”; the systematic account of legitimacy, sovereignty, and the general will.
- *Confessions* (posthumous, 1782/1789) — The frank autobiography; a primary source for his life and a founding work of modern self-writing.

## Contributions

Rousseau made inequality a problem requiring genealogy rather than justification, gave moral psychology the pair *amour de soi* / *amour-propre*, and gave democracy its most demanding idea: legitimacy as a people legislating for itself under general laws. *Émile* reshaped education by insisting the child is not a miniature adult. The *Confessions* effectively created modern autobiography.

## Impact and legacy

Kant revered him: Kant confessed he had once despised common people who know nothing, until “Rousseau set me right” — grounding dignity in what all persons share. The French Revolution claimed him as prophet, though how directly his texts caused revolutionary practice is debated. Democratic and deliberative theory draw on the general will; Romanticism drew on his nature and selfhood; modern education descends from *Émile*. He supplies arguments to liberal democracy’s defenders and critics alike.

## Criticisms and debates

The charged debate is the “totalitarian Rousseau”: the twentieth-century reading, associated with Jacob Talmon (1952) and Isaiah Berlin on positive liberty, that the general will and “forced to be free” license coercion in the people’s name. Defenders reply that this ignores Rousseau’s safeguards — generality of law, small polities, horror of anyone embodying the people — and read him as a participatory democrat. It is a debate, not a settled verdict. Other live criticisms: his treatment of women (Sophie in *Émile* Book V, criticised since Wollstonecraft), and the practicability of his small-republic conditions.

## Misreadings to avoid

- **“Rousseau wanted a return to nature.”** No — the state of nature is diagnostic; the problem is legitimacy within society as it is.
- **“The general will is whatever the majority votes.”** It is defined by its object — the common good in general laws — and majorities can err about it.
- **“A leader can embody the general will.”** Sovereignty cannot be represented or alienated to a person.
- **“The totalitarian verdict is settled.”** It is an influential, contested twentieth-century interpretation.

## What to read first

1. *The Social Contract*, Book I and Book II, chapters 1–7 — legitimacy and the general will.
2. *Discourse on Inequality*, opening of Part II — the enclosure scene, “This is mine,” in context.
3. *Émile*, Books I–III (selections) — natural education and the delaying of *amour-propre*.
4. *Confessions*, Book I — the voice behind the system.
