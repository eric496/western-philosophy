# Renaissance Platonism and Humanism: Ficino, Pico, Erasmus and Bruno

## At a glance

- **Dates:** Marsilio Ficino (1433–1499) · Giovanni Pico della Mirandola (1463–1494) · Desiderius Erasmus (1466 or 1469 – 1536; the birth year is disputed) · Giordano Bruno (1548–1600)
- **Place:** Florence and the Medici villa at Careggi; Mirandola, Florence, Paris and Rome; Rotterdam, Paris, England, Leuven, Basel and Freiburg; Naples, then Geneva, Paris, London, Wittenberg, Frankfurt, Venice and Rome
- **School/tradition:** Renaissance Platonism (Ficino, Pico, Bruno); Christian humanism (Erasmus)
- **Known for:** putting Plato and Plotinus into Latin and defending the immortality of the soul (Ficino); the 900 theses and a speech on human self-shaping (Pico); *The Praise of Folly*, the first published Greek New Testament and the free-will debate with Luther (Erasmus); an infinite universe of countless worlds, and death at the stake (Bruno)

**Responds to:** [Plato](../01-ancient/plato.md), [Plotinus](../01-ancient/plotinus.md), [Aristotle](../01-ancient/aristotle.md), [Lucretius](../01-ancient/cicero-and-lucretius.md), [Pyrrho and the skeptics](../01-ancient/pyrrho-of-elis-and-the-skeptics.md), [Aquinas](../02-medieval/thomas-aquinas.md), [Nicholas of Cusa](../02-medieval/nicholas-of-cusa.md) · **Answered by:** [Machiavelli](niccolo-machiavelli.md), [Montaigne](michel-de-montaigne.md), [Bacon](francis-bacon.md), [Spinoza](baruch-spinoza.md), [Schelling](../04-19th-century/friedrich-schelling.md)

**Why they are grouped here.** These four never formed one school, and they would not have agreed with each other. They belong together because between them they show what "Renaissance philosophy" was before Descartes: a return to ancient texts, read in the original Greek, as a way to renew Christian life and thought. Ficino supplied the texts and a Christian Platonism to read them by. Pico, his younger friend, pushed the idea that all wisdom agrees to its limit. Erasmus turned the same scholarly tools on the Bible and on the Church, and then had to choose between Rome and Luther. Bruno, a generation later, took Platonist and Lucretian ideas out of the Church's control altogether. All four lived before this era's conventional start in 1600, as did [Machiavelli](niccolo-machiavelli.md) and [Montaigne](michel-de-montaigne.md); they are the soil the era grew from.

## Biography

**Marsilio Ficino (1433–1499)** was born at Figline, near Florence, the son of a physician whose patients included Cosimo de' Medici, Florence's leading citizen and one of the richest men in Europe. He received a traditional scholastic and medical education and learned Greek in the 1450s. In 1462 Cosimo gave him a Greek manuscript of Plato to translate, and in 1463 a small property at Careggi, near the Medici villa. Cosimo first asked him to put aside Plato for the *Corpus Hermeticum*, a set of Greek religious texts then believed to be the work of an ancient Egyptian sage, Hermes Trismegistus; they are in fact from the early Christian centuries. Ficino read his Plato translations to the dying Cosimo in 1464. He was ordained a priest in 1473, became a canon of Florence cathedral, and saw himself as a healer of souls and bodies alike. The complete Plato appeared in print in October 1484, the first translation of Plato's whole surviving corpus into a Western language, and a Latin Plotinus followed in 1492. He died in 1499.

**Giovanni Pico della Mirandola (1463–1494)** was born on 24 February 1463 into the family of the counts of Mirandola, near Modena. He studied church law at Bologna, then philosophy at Ferrara and Padua, where his teacher was Elia del Medigo, a Jewish Aristotelian. By 1484 he was in Ficino's circle in Florence. Late in 1486 he printed 900 short theses drawn from pagan, Christian, Muslim and Jewish authorities and invited scholars from all over Europe to debate them in Rome, at his expense. Pope Innocent VIII stopped the debate, a commission condemned thirteen theses, and when Pico published a hasty *Apology* the pope condemned the whole book. Pico fled to France, was briefly jailed at the pope's request, and returned to Florence under Lorenzo de' Medici's protection. In 1493 Pope Alexander VI pardoned him. He fell under the influence of the preacher Savonarola and died suddenly on 17 November 1494, aged thirty-one. Rumors of poison were common in his day, but a 2018 forensic study of his remains found potentially lethal levels of arsenic.

**Desiderius Erasmus (1466 or 1469 – 1536)** was born at Rotterdam on 28 October, the illegitimate son of a priest; the year is disputed, and the *Stanford Encyclopedia* now gives 1469. Orphaned young, he was steered into a monastery of Augustinian canons at Steyn and ordained in 1492, a step he later said he had been pushed into. Study at Paris left him with a lifelong dislike of scholastic theology. A visit to England in 1499 made Thomas More and John Colet his friends and drew him to Greek and to the Bible. He became the most famous scholar in Europe through his books: the *Adages* (1500), the *Handbook of the Christian Soldier* (1503), *The Praise of Folly* (1511) and, in 1516, the first Greek New Testament to reach the market. He settled at Leuven in 1517 and later at Basel. When the Reformation broke, critics said "Erasmus laid the egg, and Luther hatched it." He resisted pressure to attack Luther until 1524. When Basel turned Protestant in 1529 he moved to Catholic Freiburg; he died in 1536.

**Giordano Bruno (1548–1600)** was born Filippo Bruno at Nola, near Naples, in early 1548, the son of a soldier. He entered the Dominican convent of San Domenico in Naples at seventeen, taking the name Giordano, and was ordained in 1572. Investigated for heretical views, probably on the Trinity, he fled the order in 1576. For fifteen years he wandered: Geneva, Toulouse, Paris, London (1583–85), Wittenberg, Prague, Helmstedt and Frankfurt. He is the only known sixteenth-century philosopher to have been excommunicated by Catholics, Calvinists and Lutherans alike. He also had a gift for offending hosts: *The Ash Wednesday Supper* (London, 1584) mocked Oxford's dons. In 1591 a Venetian nobleman, Giovanni Mocenigo, invited him to teach the art of memory. On 22 May 1592 Mocenigo denounced him to the Venetian Inquisition. Rome had him extradited in January 1593, and the trial lasted seven years. He was burned alive in the Campo de' Fiori in Rome on 17 February 1600.

## Major theories and concepts

### Ficino: the ancient theology and the immortal soul

Ficino believed that one divine wisdom had been passed down through a chain of **ancient theologians** (*prisci theologi*): Hermes, Orpheus, Pythagoras and finally Plato. On this view Plato was not a pagan rival to Christianity but a forerunner, and Platonism could be used to win back educated doubters. His great treatise, the *Platonic Theology*, written 1469–74 and printed in 1482, bears the subtitle *On the Immortality of Souls*. Its first chapter's title states the stakes. Every human soul has a natural desire to return to its divine origin, and no earthly good satisfies it. If the soul died with the body, that desire would be pointless, and the creature that has it would be the unhappiest of all. God, Ficino adds, would be a "wicked tyrant" to plant such a longing with no chance of fulfillment.

The argument is from **natural appetite**: a desire built into a nature must be capable of being satisfied. Its weakness is plain. Why must every natural desire be fulfillable? Ficino also had to fight a Platonic doctrine Christians could not accept, the transmigration of souls into new bodies. He reread Plato's myths as hints of the resurrection or blamed the doctrine on Pythagoras.

**The Academy and Platonic love.** Older histories pictured a formal "Platonic Academy of Florence," founded by Cosimo and led by Ficino, meeting like Plato's school in Athens. The evidence is thinner. There is a 1462 letter in which Ficino thanks Cosimo for "the Academy you arranged" at Careggi, and a document recording the 1463 gift of property. But no source shows a school with members, rules or regular meetings. Since James Hankins's work, most scholars hold that Ficino's "academy" meant a circle of friends and pupils, private teaching, or even Plato's dialogues themselves, not an institution. Others think the older picture contains some truth. The dispute is about evidence, and it is still open.

Ficino also coined the phrase **Platonic love**. In his commentary on Plato's *Symposium* (*On Love*, written in the late 1460s) love is the desire for beauty, and beauty in a person is the splendor of God shining through a body. True love uses that beauty as a hook that draws the soul upward toward God; false love stops at the body. The modern sense of "love without sex" is a later narrowing.

### Pico: the 900 theses and the harmony of all wisdom

Pico's project was **concord** (*concordia*), sometimes called **syncretism**: the belief that apparently opposed schools agree at a deeper level. He began with Plato and Aristotle, then widened the claim to Arab philosophers, the Hermetic texts, and the **Kabbalah**, a Jewish tradition of mystical interpretation of scripture, which he was among the first Christians to study. He held that Kabbalah, properly read, confirmed Christian doctrine. The 900 theses were the showcase. Many were technical, some outrageous theological novelties, among them the claim that magic and Kabbalah prove Christ's divinity. The tension is obvious: if every tradition agrees, why defend one Church's doctrine against the rest?

**The Oration and the self-shaping human being.** To open the debate Pico wrote a speech he never delivered or published. His nephew printed it in 1496 as just "A Very Elegant Oration." The words "on the dignity of man" first described it inside a 1504 edition, and only in 1557 did a Basel edition use "On the Dignity of Man" as its title. Its opening is famous. God, having filled every rung of the ladder of creation, has no fixed place left for Adam. So he gives the human being no fixed nature. Adam may sink to the level of the beasts or rise to the angels, shaping himself by his own choice, like a sculptor of himself.

How to read this is a live debate. Nineteenth- and twentieth-century historians, from Jacob Burckhardt to Ernst Cassirer, Paul Oskar Kristeller and Eugenio Garin, read the opening as a manifesto of human freedom and dignity, and so of Renaissance humanism. Brian Copenhaver argues that this reading projects Kant onto Pico. The rest of the speech says what we should freely choose: to leave our human nature behind, become like the angels by moral and philosophical purification, magic and Kabbalah, and finally lose the self in union with God. On that reading, Pico's freedom is a starting point for climbing out of humanity, not a celebration of it.

### Erasmus: Christian humanism and the philosophy of Christ

**Humanism** in this period meant the study of the *humanities*: grammar, rhetoric, poetry, history and moral philosophy, read in the classical originals. Erasmus used these skills for religion. His *philosophia Christi*, the "philosophy of Christ," was a simple, inward piety drawn straight from the Gospels, set against what he saw as the empty disputes of scholastic theologians and the outward rituals of popular religion. The Greek New Testament of 1516 put this into practice. He printed the Greek text with a new Latin translation and notes, corrected the medieval Latin Bible where the Greek disagreed, and defended textual criticism as a philologist's job, not a theologian's. Critics were alarmed. He left out a verse on the Trinity (1 John 5:7) for which he had found no Greek evidence, and he changed "do penance" to "repent" at Matthew 3:2, which looked Lutheran to them.

*The Praise of Folly* (1511), written as a joke for Thomas More (whose name echoes *moria*, Greek for "folly"), has Folly herself give a speech in her own praise. It begins as light satire and becomes savage about theologians, monks and princes. Then, at the end, it turns: Christianity itself is a kind of holy folly, as Paul said, "We are fools for Christ's sake." The reader is never sure where Folly speaks for Erasmus, and that uncertainty is part of the point.

### Erasmus against Luther on free will

In *A Discussion of Free Will* (1524) Erasmus defined free will as a power by which a person can turn toward or away from what leads to salvation. His method was skeptical in an old sense. He gathered biblical texts on both sides, showed that they did not settle the question, said he would gladly suspend judgment, and accepted the Church's teaching that free will exists. Luther's reply, *The Bondage of the Will* (1525), attacked both the method and the answer. A Christian, he said, must make firm assertions, not weigh probabilities; human will, enslaved by sin, contributes nothing to salvation, which is God's work alone. Luther also praised Erasmus for going straight to the heart of the dispute. Erasmus answered with *Hyperaspistes* (1526). The exchange set the terms for the long Protestant–Catholic quarrel about grace and freedom.

### Bruno: an infinite universe of countless worlds

Bruno was an early and bold defender of Copernicus, who had put the sun, not the earth, at the center of the planets (1543). Many readers treated Copernicus's system as a calculating device; Bruno insisted it was physically true, and he went further than Copernicus. If the earth moves, the apparent turning of the sphere of fixed stars is an illusion, and nothing requires that the stars sit on a sphere at all. The universe, he argued, is an infinite, uniform space filled with countless suns, each with its own planets, which he thought could be inhabited like ours. He drew on [Lucretius](../01-ancient/cicero-and-lucretius.md) and on [Nicholas of Cusa](../02-medieval/nicholas-of-cusa.md), whom he claimed as a forerunner. His reasons were philosophical and theological, not observational: an infinite God, he held, must express himself in an infinite universe, which he called "a second god" and a living mirror of God. Bruno did not identify God with the universe outright. God remained, for him, a simple unity beyond all distinctions.

### Bruno's trial: the records and the legend

The full trial record is lost. What survives includes a summary of the proceedings (the *Sommario*), published by Angelo Mercati in 1942, and the documents collected by Luigi Firpo. They show charges that were mostly theological: doubts about the Trinity, the divinity of Christ and transubstantiation; the transmigration of souls; the eternity of the world; and, alongside these, the motion of the earth against Scripture and the plurality of worlds. In January 1599 the Jesuit theologian Robert Bellarmine helped draw up eight heretical propositions for Bruno to abjure. That list is lost, and only two of the eight are known; neither is about astronomy. Bruno offered qualified retractions, then on 21 December 1599 refused to recant at all. An eyewitness, the Catholic convert Caspar Schoppe, wrote on the day of the execution that the sentence listed denials of transubstantiation and the virgin birth, an attack on the papacy, and "horrible absurdities" from his Latin books, among them infinite worlds, the transmigration of souls and magic.

Nineteenth-century Italian liberals made Bruno a **martyr for science** and free thought, and in 1889 raised a statue to him on the spot where he died, facing the Vatican. That story is half right. Bruno died for refusing to recant, and his cosmology was among the views read out against him. But he was not a scientist in the modern sense: he argued from metaphysics, theology and Hermetic and magical ideas, not from observation, and most of what condemned him concerned Christ, the Trinity and the soul. Historians still dispute how much weight his many worlds carried. Alberto Martínez (2016) argues that it was a significant charge in its own right; others treat it as a minor item in a theological case. Galileo's later trial (1633) was a separate case about Copernicanism, and the two should not be merged.

## Key works

- Ficino, Latin Plato (drafted by 1468–69; printed 1484) and Plotinus (printed 1492) — the standard Latin versions for three centuries; *Commentary on Plato's Symposium* (*On Love*, late 1460s) — the source of "Platonic love."
- Ficino, *Platonic Theology: On the Immortality of Souls* (written 1469–74; printed 1482) — his systematic case for immortality; *Three Books on Life* (1489) — medicine, astrology and natural magic for scholars.
- Pico, *900 Conclusions* (1486) and the *Oration* written to introduce them (published posthumously, 1496); *Heptaplus* (1489) — a Kabbalist reading of Genesis; *On Being and the One* (after 1490) — Plato and Aristotle reconciled.
- Erasmus, *The Praise of Folly* (1511) — satire that ends in a praise of Christian folly; Greek New Testament (1516; revised 1519, 1522, 1527, 1531).
- Erasmus, *A Discussion of Free Will* (1524) and *Hyperaspistes* (1526); read with Luther's *The Bondage of the Will* (1525).
- Bruno, *The Ash Wednesday Supper*, *On the Cause, the Principle and the One* and *On the Infinite Universe and Worlds* (all London, 1584) — Copernicanism, metaphysics and cosmology in Italian dialogues; *The Heroic Frenzies* (1585) — the soul's love-driven ascent.

## Contributions

Ficino made Plato and Plotinus readable in the West again and gave Europe a Christian Platonism of the soul and of love. Pico introduced Kabbalah to Christian thought and gave the period its most quoted image of human self-making. Erasmus founded the critical editing of the New Testament and gave Christian humanism its program. Bruno was among the first to argue for an infinite universe of many suns and worlds.

## Impact and legacy

Ficino's Latin Plato and Plotinus stayed standard for over three centuries, and his theory of love ran through sixteenth-century writers from Castiglione and Bembo to Ronsard. Pico's attack on predictive astrology was widely read, and his Christian Kabbalah had a long esoteric afterlife. Erasmus's New Testament underlay later Protestant translations into the vernacular. His Christian skepticism, which accepted the Church's judgment where Scripture is unclear, has been traced forward to [Montaigne](michel-de-montaigne.md) and the skeptical fideists of the next century. [Bacon](francis-bacon.md), in *The Advancement of Learning* (1605), blamed the humanist revival for making scholars chase fine phrases instead of substance, and retold Erasmus's own joke against the cult of Cicero. Kepler engaged with Bruno's cosmology while rejecting an infinite universe. Enlightenment and nineteenth-century readers called Bruno a forerunner of [Spinoza](baruch-spinoza.md), though a direct debt is unproven. [Schelling](../04-19th-century/friedrich-schelling.md) named his 1802 dialogue *Bruno* after him.

## Criticisms and debates

- **Was there a Renaissance philosophy at all?** Kristeller argued that humanism was a set of literary studies, not a philosophy; others reply that Pico and Erasmus made real philosophical claims in rhetorical form.
- **Pico's meaning.** Manifesto of human dignity (Garin, Kristeller) or program for becoming angels (Copenhaver)?
- **Erasmus's skepticism.** Luther called it waffling that left souls without certainty. Defenders see a principled modesty about questions Scripture leaves open; critics see caution born of fear for his safety. The *Stanford Encyclopedia* suggests both played a part.
- **Bruno's case.** Theological heretic, scientific martyr, or Hermetic magician (Frances Yates's influential 1964 reading, which the *Stanford Encyclopedia* thinks obscures his stated aim of a "new philosophy")? Each label captures part of the record.

## Misreadings to avoid

- **"Renaissance humanists were secular."** All four were Christians of some kind, three were priests, and Ficino and Erasmus wrote to renew the faith.
- **"Pico wrote a treatise called *On the Dignity of Man*."** He wrote an untitled speech; the title came decades later.
- **"Platonic love means love without sex."** For Ficino it meant love that ascends through beauty toward God.
- **"Erasmus sided with Luther."** He shared Luther's dislike of abuses, but rejected schism and denied Luther on free will.
- **"Bruno was burned for Copernicanism."** Cosmology was one charge among many, mostly theological; and he argued for infinity from metaphysics, not from observation.

## In their own words

> "Were the soul not immortal, no creature would be more miserable than man."

— Ficino, *Platonic Theology*, Bk 1, ch. 1 (chapter title); tr. Michael J. B. Allen, I Tatti Renaissance Library, vol. 1, pp. 14–15, as quoted in the *Stanford Encyclopedia*, "Marsilio Ficino," §3.3

> "we shall be ourselves no longer, but shall be Him, the very one who made us"

— Pico, *Oration*, tr. Brian Copenhaver (I Tatti Renaissance Library 93, 2022), pp. 88–102, as quoted in the *Stanford Encyclopedia*, "Giovanni Pico della Mirandola," §2.4

> "all Christian religion seems to have a kind of alliance with folly and in no respect to have any accord with wisdom"

— Erasmus, *The Praise of Folly* (1511), closing section on Christian folly; tr. John Wilson (1668)

> "a power in the human will, by which, a man may apply himself to those things which lead unto eternal salvation, or turn away from the same"

— Erasmus's definition of free will (*De libero arbitrio*, 1524), as quoted by Luther, *The Bondage of the Will* (1525), Sect. XLI; tr. Henry Cole (1823)

> "he neither needed nor wished to recant, that he did not have anything to recant"

— record of Bruno's reply to the inquisitors, 21 December 1599 (Firpo, *Il processo di Giordano Bruno*, 2000, p. 463, doc. 64), in the *Stanford Encyclopedia*'s translation, "Giordano Bruno," §1

> "Greater perhaps is your fear in pronouncing my sentence than mine in hearing it."

— Bruno to his judges, as reported in Caspar Schoppe's letter of 17 February 1600; tr. J. Lewis McIntyre, *Giordano Bruno* (1903), Part I, ch. XVIII

## Study questions

1. Ficino argues that a natural desire for God shows the soul is immortal. What hidden premise does the argument need, and is it plausible?
2. Read the opening of Pico's *Oration* and then its later sections. Does the speech celebrate human nature or invite us to escape it?
3. Erasmus says he would suspend judgment on free will if he could; Luther says faith requires assertions. Who has the better view of religious certainty?
4. *The Praise of Folly* is spoken by Folly. How can a reader tell when she is being mocked and when she speaks for the author?
5. Was Bruno a martyr for science? Use the charges recorded at his trial to argue for a more precise description.

## What to read first

1. Erasmus, *The Praise of Folly* — short, funny, and in a free public-domain translation.
2. Pico, *Oration* — read all of it, not just the opening, then the *Stanford Encyclopedia* entry on Pico.
3. Ficino, *Platonic Theology*, Book 1, or selections from *On Love*.
4. Erasmus, *A Discussion of Free Will*, with the opening sections of Luther's reply; then Bruno, *The Ash Wednesday Supper*, and the *Stanford Encyclopedia* account of his trial.
