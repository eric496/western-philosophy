## At a glance

- **Dates:** Johann Georg Hamann (1730–1788) · Johann Gottfried Herder (1744–1803)
- **Place:** Königsberg in East Prussia for both at the start; Herder then Riga, Strasbourg, Bückeburg and, from 1776, Weimar
- **School/tradition:** German critics of the Enlightenment from inside it; forerunners of *Sturm und Drang* ("Storm and Stress") and Romanticism; founders of modern philosophy of language and of historicism
- **Known for:** Hamann's claim that reason cannot be separated from language, the senses and tradition, and his "metacritique" of Kant; Herder's theses that thought depends on language and that meaning is word-usage, his theory of interpretation, and his pluralism about cultures

**Responds to:** [Kant](immanuel-kant.md), [Hume](david-hume.md), [Rousseau](jean-jacques-rousseau.md), [Leibniz](gottfried-wilhelm-leibniz.md), [Lessing](gotthold-ephraim-lessing.md), [Spinoza](baruch-spinoza.md) · **Answered by:** [Hegel](../04-19th-century/g-w-f-hegel.md), [Schleiermacher](../04-19th-century/friedrich-schleiermacher.md), [Kierkegaard](../04-19th-century/soren-kierkegaard.md), [Gadamer](../05-20th-century/hans-georg-gadamer.md)

**Why they are grouped here.** Hamann and Herder were friends from Königsberg, both close to Kant, and both argued that the Enlightenment picture of a pure, universal reason left out what makes human thinking possible: language, feeling, history and culture. They are often read together under Isaiah Berlin's label "the Counter-Enlightenment." That label is itself disputed (see below), and the two men differ sharply: Hamann was a Christian ironist who distrusted systems and arguments alike, while Herder wanted theories that were consistent and backed by argument. They belong in one entry because they are two sides of one argument with their age.

## Biography

**Johann Georg Hamann (1730–1788)** was born in Königsberg, the son of a midwife and a barber-surgeon. He studied at the university there, drifting from philosophy and theology to law while mostly reading literature, philology and rhetoric, and left without a degree. After work as a household tutor, he joined the firm of his friend Christoph Berens, who sent him on a commercial mission to London. It failed. Out of money and friends, he read the Bible through in a garret and underwent a religious conversion (1758) that redirected his whole life.

Back in Riga he asked to marry Berens's sister and was refused, partly because Berens, an enthusiast for the Enlightenment, found Hamann's new piety repellent. Berens and Kant then tried to win him back to the Enlightenment cause. His answer was his first public essay, *Socratic Memorabilia* (1759), dedicated to the two of them, which turns Socrates, the Enlightenment's hero of reason, into a figure of faith who knows that he knows nothing. Hamann never held an academic or church post, partly because a strong speech impediment ruled out lecturing and preaching. Through Kant's help he became a minor official in the Prussian tax administration of Frederick the Great, a king he despised. He lived for years with a woman he never married and had four children with her. He wrote under the pen name "the Magus of the North," in short, allusive, deliberately obscure pieces. He died in Münster in 1788, at the invitation of an admirer, Princess Gallitzin.

**Johann Gottfried Herder (1744–1803)** was born in Mohrungen in East Prussia to a schoolteacher's family of modest means. In 1762 he enrolled at Königsberg, where Kant, then in his pre-critical period, gave him special privileges because of his unusual ability, and where his lifelong friendship with Hamann began. From 1764 he taught school and preached in Riga, publishing the *Fragments on Recent German Literature* (1767–68) and the *Critical Forests* (1769). In 1769 he left for France and then Strasbourg, where in 1770 he met the young Goethe and influenced him deeply.

His *Treatise on the Origin of Language* won the Berlin Academy's prize in 1771 and appeared in 1772. As court preacher at Bückeburg (1771–76) he wrote *This Too a Philosophy of History for the Formation of Humanity* (1774). In 1776, partly through Goethe, he became General Superintendent of the Lutheran clergy in Weimar and held the post for the rest of his life. Weimar produced his longest works: the *Ideas for the Philosophy of History of Humanity* (1784–91), *God: Some Conversations* (1787), and the *Letters for the Advancement of Humanity* (1793–97). Late in life he attacked his old teacher in the *Metacritique* (1799) and *Calligone* (1800). He died in Weimar in 1803.

## Major theories and concepts

### Hamann: reason is language

Hamann's central claim is that there is no such thing as "reason" in the singular, only reasoning, which people do, fallibly, in particular languages and traditions. Philosophers, he thought, take an activity, turn the verb into a noun and then treat the noun as a thing. He called this move *prosopopoeia* (personification), after the stage practice of turning a quality like chastity into a character who walks and talks. Language is where the trick happens and where it can be undone, because every concept we reason with comes to us in words we inherited. His most quoted line puts it as a confession rather than a theorem: "Reason is language." He also insisted that language begins not in definitions but in image and passion, as in *Aesthetica in nuce* (1762): poetry comes first, abstraction later.

The tension is that Hamann gives no system and few arguments, by design. He thought any grand system forced reality into a false consistency. Whether that makes him profound or merely evasive is part of the dispute about him.

### Hamann's metacritique of Kant

Kant's *Critique of Pure Reason* (1781) tried to "purify" reason by separating what reason contributes from what experience supplies. Hamann's short *Metacritique of the Purism of Reason* (written 1784, published only after his death) answers that this purification cannot be done. Reason cannot be purified of tradition, of experience, or finally of language, which carries both. So Kant's divisions between sensibility and understanding, a priori and a posteriori, are drawn inside a medium that already joins them. Hamann turned Hume's skepticism against Kant here; he had himself introduced Kant to Hume, while using Hume to defend faith rather than unbelief.

### Herder: thought depends on language

Herder defended three theses, as Michael Forster reconstructs them. First, thought is essentially dependent on and bounded by language: one can think only if one has a language, and only what one could express in it. Second, meaning is word-usage, not an idea in the head or a Platonic form. Third, concepts are rooted in sensation, with abstract ones reached by metaphorical extension. The *Treatise on the Origin of Language* argues that language is neither a direct gift from God nor animal cries refined; it arises from a human capacity for reflection (*Besonnenheit*), the ability to single out a feature of experience and mark it.

Forster argues that Herder held the first two theses by the mid-1760s and that Hamann adopted them later, under Herder's influence. That reverses Berlin's story, in which Hamann hands them to Herder.

### Herder on interpretation and history

If thought is bound to language, and languages differ, then people in other periods and cultures may think in ways deeply unlike ours. Hume had written that mankind are much the same in all times and places. Herder denied it, and built a theory of interpretation around the gap. The interpreter must work out word-usages, a work's genre and its whole historical setting. His metaphor for this is *Einfühlung*, "feeling one's way in." Friedrich Meinecke read it as projecting oneself into the past; Forster reads it as hard historical and philological work, the opposite of projection.

*This Too a Philosophy of History* (1774) applies the point to history as a whole. It mocks the Enlightenment habit of ranking every age by how closely it resembles eighteenth-century Paris. Each culture has its own standards of happiness and value, so the comparisons are, at bottom, problematic. Critics call this relativism, and Forster grants the label is reasonable; the tension is that Herder also believed in a growing "humanity" that history develops.

### Expressivism and pluralism

Charles Taylor named the position *expressivism*: thought and meaning are not complete inside us before we express them; they take shape in outward expression. Herder held a narrow version, where language is the key medium even for the thought in painting or music. Hamann, on Forster's reading, held a broader one. From this follows pluralism about cultures: each people expresses itself in its own language, poetry and customs, and each deserves respect. Herder collected folk songs from many nations for that reason.

### The Volksgeist debates

Herder spoke of a people's "spirit," "genius" or "national character." The single word *Volksgeist* is usually traced to later writers, Hegel among them, but the idea has been laid at Herder's door. R. R. Ergang (1931) and Karl Popper (1945) made him a father of German nationalism. Forster calls that deeply misleading: Herder applied his respect for nations to all peoples equally, rejected any "favorite people," defined nations by language and culture rather than race (he rejected the concept of race), opposed conquest and colonialism, and called himself a cosmopolitan. How far later nationalists could still borrow from him is the live question.

## Key works

- Hamann, *Socratic Memorabilia* (1759) — Socrates as a witness to faith and ignorance, aimed at Kant and Berens.
- Hamann, *Aesthetica in nuce* (1762, in *Crusades of the Philologist*) — poetry and image as the first language of humanity.
- Hamann, *Metacritique of the Purism of Reason* (1784, published posthumously) — the critique of Kant from language.
- Herder, *Treatise on the Origin of Language* (1772) — the prize essay; language from human reflection.
- Herder, *This Too a Philosophy of History for the Formation of Humanity* (1774) — the attack on Enlightenment progress-history.
- Herder, *Ideas for the Philosophy of History of Humanity* (1784–91) — the long natural and cultural history; Kant reviewed it critically in 1785.
- Herder, *Letters for the Advancement of Humanity* (1793–97) — politics and cosmopolitanism after the French Revolution.
- Isaiah Berlin, "The Counter-Enlightenment" (1973), *Vico and Herder* (1976), *The Magus of the North* (1993) — the framing that made them famous in English.

## Contributions

Hamann made language, not consciousness, the place where philosophy must start, long before the twentieth-century "linguistic turn." Herder turned that insight into theses that, in Forster's judgment, founded the philosophy of language as we know it, and into a method of interpretation that Schleiermacher, Dilthey and Gadamer would build on. Herder also gave history and anthropology the principle that other cultures must be understood from inside before they are judged.

## Impact and legacy

Herder shaped Goethe and the *Sturm und Drang*, the Romantics and the study of folk literature, and fed directly into Hegel's philosophy of history and Schleiermacher's hermeneutics. Hamann was admired by Goethe, influenced Jacobi and Hegel, and mattered greatly to Kierkegaard. In English, Berlin's essays brought both back into view as founders of value pluralism and as critics of a rationalism he thought could turn technocratic.

## Criticisms and debates

The biggest dispute is the label. Berlin popularized "the Counter-Enlightenment," though he did not coin it, and read Hamann as a near-irrationalist and wholesale opponent of the Enlightenment. Most Hamann scholars now resist this, describing him as a "metacritic" who argued with his contemporaries on specific points and shared much with them; the idea of a single Enlightenment for him to oppose is itself contested. Herder fits the label even less, as an empiricist, a cosmopolitan and a defender of argued theory. A second dispute is who influenced whom on language (Berlin: Hamann to Herder; Forster: the reverse). A third is whether Herder's pluralism collapses into relativism, and whether it can coexist with his belief in progress toward "humanity." Kant's own objection, in his 1785 review of the *Ideas*, was that Herder replaced argument with analogy and imagination.

## Misreadings to avoid

- Hamann did not reject reason; he denied that reason exists apart from language, sense and tradition.
- "Counter-Enlightenment" is not a school either man joined. It is a later and contested label.
- Herder was not a German nationalist in the modern sense. His respect for peoples was meant for every people equally.
- *Einfühlung* in Herder is not mystical empathy. On the standard modern reading it is careful historical research.
- Herder did not identify thought with language; he held that thought depends on and is bounded by it.

## In their own words

- Hamann: "Poetry is the mother-tongue of the human race" (*Aesthetica in nuce*, 1762; Nadler ed., N II, 197).
- Hamann, in a letter of 1784: "Reason is language—Logos; I gnaw on this marrowbone and will gnaw myself to death over it." (Ziesemer–Henkel ed., ZH 5, 177:16–21)
- Hamann: "Hence it happens that one takes words for concepts, and concepts for the things themselves" (ZH 5, 264:34–265:1).
- Herder: "at bottom all comparison proves to be problematic … Each nation has its center of happiness in itself, like every sphere its center of gravity!" (*This Too a Philosophy of History*, 1774; Forster trans., *Philosophical Writings*, pp. 296–297)
- Herder, quoting Fénelon with approval: "I love my family more than myself; more than my family my fatherland; more than my fatherland humankind" (*Letters for the Advancement of Humanity*; Forster trans., *Philosophical Writings*, p. 389)

*Hamann's lines are in the English given in Gwen Griffith-Dickson's Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry; Herder's are Michael Forster's translations as quoted in his SEP entry.*

## Study questions

1. What does Hamann mean by calling reason a "personification," and is he right that philosophers turn activities into things?
2. Can Kant's division between what reason supplies and what experience supplies survive Hamann's point that both arrive through language?
3. Herder says one can think only what one could express in language. Find a counterexample, and ask how Herder might answer it.
4. Is "each nation has its center of happiness in itself" relativism? Can Herder also say that some cultures treat people better than others?
5. Does the label "Counter-Enlightenment" help or hinder in understanding these two? What would a better label be?

## What to read first

1. Herder, *This Too a Philosophy of History* (in Forster, ed., *Herder: Philosophical Writings*, Cambridge, 2002) — short, polemical and the clearest way in.
2. Herder, *Treatise on the Origin of Language* (same volume) — the language theses in action.
3. Hamann, *Socratic Memorabilia* and *Metacritique* (in Kenneth Haynes, ed., *Hamann: Writings on Philosophy and Language*, Cambridge, 2007) — read slowly, with the notes.
4. Isaiah Berlin, "The Counter-Enlightenment" — then the SEP entries on Hamann and Herder, which argue with him.
