## At a glance
- **Dates:** 1759–1797
- **Place:** England (London, with periods in Ireland, Portugal, revolutionary France and Scandinavia)
- **School/tradition:** Writer and philosopher of the Enlightenment and the French Revolutionary era; the radical, rational-dissenting circle around the publisher Joseph Johnson
- **Known for:** *A Vindication of the Rights of Woman* (1792), the founding text of modern feminism, which argues that women's apparent inferiority is the product of denied education, not nature, and demands equal schooling so women can become rational, independent citizens
- **Also known for:** *A Vindication of the Rights of Men* (1790), the first reply to Burke's *Reflections*, and the *Letters* from Scandinavia (1796)

**Responds to:** [Rousseau](jean-jacques-rousseau.md), [Burke](edmund-burke.md), [Elisabeth, Cavendish, Conway & Astell](early-modern-women-philosophers.md) · **Answered by:** the suffrage and women's-education movements, [John Stuart Mill](../04-19th-century/john-stuart-mill.md), [Simone de Beauvoir](../05-20th-century/simone-de-beauvoir.md), and the twentieth-century feminists who recovered her ([Feminist philosophy after Beauvoir](../05-20th-century/feminist-philosophy-after-beauvoir.md))

## Biography
Wollstonecraft was born on 27 April 1759 in Spitalfields, London, the second of seven children, into a family sliding down the social scale: her grandfather had been a prosperous master weaver, but her father squandered his inheritance on a string of failed attempts at gentleman farming, and drank. Violence at home marked her childhood — Godwin's *Memoirs* records that she lay whole nights on the landing outside her mother's door to intercept his rages. Only her elder brother received a formal education. She was largely self-educated, reading the Bible, Milton, Shakespeare and the moralists, and she worked in the few employments open to respectable women: companion (at Bath, from 1778), schoolteacher, governess.

In 1784 she founded a school at Newington Green with her sisters and her friend Fanny Blood. Newington Green was a centre of rational Dissent, and there she came to know the minister Richard Price, whose sermons she attended and whose theology of reason and conscience shaped her own. Fanny Blood married and went to Lisbon; Wollstonecraft sailed out to nurse her through childbirth, and Fanny's death there in 1785 haunted her writing. The school failed, and a miserable governess post with the Kingsborough family in Ireland (1786–87) followed — but she had begun to write, encouraged by the radical publisher Joseph Johnson, who published her *Thoughts on the Education of Daughters* (1787) and employed her as a reviewer and translator for his *Analytical Review*, founded in 1788. Her novel *Mary: A Fiction* and the children's book *Original Stories from Real Life* appeared in 1788. Through Johnson's circle she met Thomas Paine, the painter Henry Fuseli, William Godwin, and other reformers.

The French Revolution made her a public combatant. Price's 1789 sermon welcoming the Revolution provoked Edmund Burke's *Reflections on the Revolution in France* (1790), and Burke's attack on Price provoked her *Vindication of the Rights of Men* (1790) — published anonymously at the end of November, then under her name in the second edition in December, among the first and fiercest replies to Burke. In September 1791 Talleyrand presented to the French National Assembly a report on public instruction proposing that girls receive only domestic schooling; that autumn she wrote the *Rights of Woman*, published in 1792 and dedicated to him in open challenge. Godwin later claimed it was "begun, carried on, and finished in the state in which it now appears, in a period of no more than six weeks" (*Memoirs*, ch. VI).

In December 1792 she traveled to revolutionary Paris alone, witnessed the Terror, and wrote a history of the Revolution's first years. There she began a relationship with the American adventurer and merchant Gilbert Imlay; when British subjects came under suspicion she passed as his wife, though they never married. Their daughter, Fanny, was born at Le Havre in May 1794. Imlay drifted away and was unfaithful, and in May 1795, back in London, she attempted suicide. Recovering, she traveled in Scandinavia on business for Imlay that summer, and the published *Letters Written during a Short Residence in Sweden, Norway, and Denmark* (1796) — part travel book, part meditation — became her most popular work in her lifetime. On her return, finding Imlay living with another woman, she threw herself from Putney Bridge into the Thames in October 1795, and was brought back to life by the people who found her.

In 1796 she renewed her acquaintance with Godwin, the philosopher of *Political Justice*, who had disliked her at their first meeting in 1791. They became lovers, and when she was pregnant they married at St Pancras in March 1797, though both had written against marriage, and kept separate working lodgings. On 30 August 1797 she gave birth to a second daughter — Mary, the future Mary Shelley, author of *Frankenstein*. The placenta was retained, infection set in, and she died of puerperal fever on 10 September, aged thirty-eight. Godwin published her unfinished novel *The Wrongs of Woman, or Maria* with other *Posthumous Works* in 1798, together with his well-meant *Memoirs*, whose candor about Imlay, Fanny's birth and the suicide attempts scandalized the age and wrecked her reputation for a century.

## Major theories and concepts

### Reason, education, and manufactured inferiority
The *Vindication*'s engine is a single argument applied relentlessly. Women's minds appear frivolous and weak, Wollstonecraft concedes, but the cause is educational and social, not natural: girls are trained from infancy in cunning, softness, and what she calls a "pampered desire of pleasing," denied serious study and bodily exercise, and rewarded for beauty rather than understanding — then their resulting character is cited as proof they merit nothing better. If women share with men the God-given faculty of reason — and her theology insists they do, since virtue requires rational self-government — then justice and consistency demand the education that develops it. She puts the wager plainly: let women's faculties unfold, and if they truly are weaker, the case against equality can be remade; until then, arguing from produced incapacity to natural incapacity begs the question.

The underlying psychology is Lockean: character is formed by early association of ideas and habit, not stamped in at birth (chapter 6 of the *Vindication* is devoted to this). A concrete example is her account of "sensibility." A girl confined indoors with dolls, novels and talk of dress grows nervous, timid and easily swayed by feeling; that is then called the female temperament. The tension is that Wollstonecraft also accepts some natural difference — she grants men greater bodily strength — so the argument must show which differences are real, and she can only say that the decisive experiment has not yet been run.

### Reason and virtue have no sex
The philosophical core is a claim about virtue itself. If virtue is the perfecting of reason, and reason is one faculty in every human soul, then there cannot be one virtue for men (courage, wisdom, justice) and another for women (gentleness, docility, charm). Wollstonecraft therefore throws down her gauntlet and denies "the existence of sexual virtues," even modesty, which she redefines as a sober purity of mind proper to both sexes. A virtue that differs by sex, she argues, is no longer grounded in truth; it becomes "relative," measured by what is useful or pleasing to men, and men appoint themselves its judges. The example she presses hardest is the double standard of chastity: a man's lapses are forgiven while a woman's single fault ruins her, which shows that what is called female virtue is really reputation, kept for the sake of men's property and pride. The internal tension is that her own standard is demanding — she asks women to suppress the wish to please and to master the passions — and some readers find her picture of the rational, self-governed woman austere.

### Against Rousseau's Sophie
Her sharpest theoretical target is Rousseau's *Émile* (1762), whose Book V designs Sophie's education entirely around pleasing and serving Émile: she is to be weak, modest, attentive to opinion, and trained from childhood to yield. Wollstonecraft, who admired Rousseau's attack on artificial society, answers in chapter 5 that he betrays his own principles when he reaches women. An education aimed at attractiveness makes women artificial, dependent, and ultimately worse wives and mothers — the very roles conservatives claimed to protect — because charm fades within a few years of marriage and leaves nothing behind it. Already in chapter 3 she had dismissed his claim that girls love dolls and dress "from their birth," pointing out that they are given nothing else to do. The tension is that she must use Rousseau's own ideal of natural, unspoiled development against him, which commits her to a strong claim about what is natural in women that she elsewhere says cannot yet be known.

### National co-education
Her alternative is co-educational national schooling, set out in chapter 12 as a direct counter-proposal to Talleyrand: day schools established by government, boys and girls taught together, the youngest classes free and open to all ranks, with the same subjects and with physical exercise, so that women become companions to their husbands rather than "alluring mistresses," and mothers capable of educating their own children. Day schools rather than boarding schools keep children within family affection; mixing the sexes early, she argues, makes them friends rather than strangers and cures the false delicacy that separation breeds. Equality of education benefits the whole society: it produces better marriages, better citizens, and women who, if widowed, can support themselves rather than sinking into dependence — she even suggests women might study medicine, business and politics. The tension is that the plan divides children at age nine by expected occupation, so her universal schooling still sorts by class.

### Rights, virtue, and independence
From the *Rights of Men* onward, Wollstonecraft grounds her politics in natural rights against Burke's reverence for inherited custom. Burke had defended the British constitution as an inheritance and mocked the abstract "rights of men"; she replies that rights come from reason and God, not from precedent, and that what Burke calls the wisdom of ages often protects property and rank — primogeniture sacrifices younger children to the eldest son, game laws and press-warrants oppress the poor, and the slave trade, she points out, is also an ancient custom. She also turns his aesthetics against him: Burke's *Enquiry* had coded the sublime as masculine and the beautiful as feminine and weak, and she accuses him of reasoning with sentiment instead of argument. In the *Rights of Woman* she extends the logic: a society is judged by the reason and virtue it cultivates in all its members; inherited wealth, standing armies and courtly rank corrupt; and virtue is one standard for both sexes. Women need civil and political standing, including a voice in representation (she suggests in chapter 9 that women ought to have representatives), because duties cannot be demanded of beings denied the independence that makes duty possible. Her key term is independence: not isolation, but the material and intellectual capacity to govern oneself, without which "virtue" is obedience rebranded.

## Key works
- *Thoughts on the Education of Daughters* (1787) — her first book; practical and already critical of the accomplishments-curriculum for girls.
- *Mary: A Fiction* and *Original Stories from Real Life* (1788) — a novel of a woman's inner life, and Lockean moral tales for children.
- *A Vindication of the Rights of Men* (1790) — the reply to Burke defending the Revolution, Price, and natural rights against prescription.
- *A Vindication of the Rights of Woman* (1792) — the major work: education, Rousseau, sexual double standards, national co-education, and women's civil standing.
- *An Historical and Moral View of the Origin and Progress of the French Revolution* (1794) — her history of the Revolution's early phase, written in Paris.
- *Letters Written during a Short Residence in Sweden, Norway, and Denmark* (1796) — travel letters blending landscape, commerce, and reflections on society and motherhood.
- *The Wrongs of Woman, or Maria* (unfinished; published posthumously 1798) — a novel of a wife confined in an asylum by her husband, dramatizing the legal powerlessness of married women.

## In their own words

> The birthright of man, to give you, Sir, a short definition of this disputed right, is such a degree of liberty, civil and religious, as is compatible with the liberty of every other individual with whom he is united in a social compact, and the continued existence of that compact.

— Mary Wollstonecraft, *A Vindication of the Rights of Men* (2nd ed., 1790), early in the letter; public domain

Against Burke's inherited liberties, a rule derived from reason: everyone's freedom, limited only by equal freedom for all.

> Who made man the exclusive judge, if woman partake with him the gift of reason?

— *A Vindication of the Rights of Woman* (1792), dedication to Talleyrand; public domain

The whole book in one question: if reason is shared, men cannot be judges in their own cause.

> Taught from their infancy, that beauty is woman's sceptre, the mind shapes itself to the body, and, roaming round its gilt cage, only seeks to adorn its prison.

— *Rights of Woman*, ch. 3

> I here throw down my gauntlet, and deny the existence of sexual virtues, not excepting modesty.

— *Rights of Woman*, ch. 3

> I do not wish them to have power over men; but over themselves.

— *Rights of Woman*, ch. 4, replying to Rousseau's warning that women educated like men will have less power over men

> To render this practicable, day schools for particular ages should be established by government, in which boys and girls might be educated together.

— *Rights of Woman*, ch. 12, "On National Education"

## Contributions
Wollstonecraft founded modern feminist philosophy: she first systematically applied Enlightenment universalism — reason, rights, education — to sex, exposing the contradiction of proclaiming universal rights while excluding half of humanity. She gave the argument its lasting shape: inferiority produced by unequal treatment cannot be cited as evidence of natural inferiority. She also contributed to revolutionary-era debates on Burke, property, and education, proposed a free, mixed national school system decades before any state built one, and pioneered a confessional, first-person mode of philosophical travel writing.

## Impact and legacy
In her lifetime the *Rights of Woman* was widely reviewed and was translated into French and German in the 1790s; it was also widely read in America. After Godwin's *Memoirs* made her name a byword, nineteenth-century reformers quietly used her arguments while avoiding her name: William Thompson and Anna Wheeler, the Owenite socialists, and in the end John Stuart Mill's *Subjection of Women* (1869) drew on the space she opened. Abroad she influenced Flora Tristan in France, and the American women's-rights movement read her openly. The suffrage and women's-education movements recovered her by name, and twentieth-century feminism canonized the *Vindication* as a founding document; Beauvoir's *The Second Sex* places her in its genealogy of women who resisted. Since the late twentieth century scholars have read her less as an isolated forerunner and more as an Enlightenment moral and political philosopher, with renewed attention to the *Rights of Men*, the Revolution history and the *Letters*. Her daughter Mary Shelley's career, and the Wollstonecraft-Godwin circle's role in radical publishing, extend her influence through Romantic literature as well as political thought.

## Criticisms and debates
Critics from her day to ours have pressed several points. Conservative contemporaries mocked her, and after the *Memoirs* many read her arguments through her life rather than on their merits. She often argues instrumentally — educate women to make better wives and mothers — and scholars debate how far her equality is intrinsic rather than justified by social utility, and how far her address to middle-class women limits the argument's reach; her contempt for "ladies" of fashion and for servants sits awkwardly with her universalism. Her remarks on modesty and sexuality are stricter than modern readers expect, and her case rests partly on theological premises (an immortal soul accountable to God) that secular feminists have had to rebuild on other foundations. A further debate asks whether she wants women to become like men — she says she wishes them "more masculine" in the sense of rational and strong — or whether she is redefining virtue for both. Others note the *Vindication*'s haste: it is repetitive and unfinished in plan, with a promised second volume never written. A live interpretive question is how far the *Letters* and the late novel revise her early rationalism toward a fuller place for passion and imagination. Defenders reply that the core argument — produced inferiority is no evidence of natural inferiority — stands independent of these framings, and that the instrumental appeals were the price of being heard in 1792.

## Misreadings to avoid
- She did not argue that women are identical to men in every respect; she argued that differences in character are unproven while education is unequal — the experiment has not been run.
- She is not only a feminist writer: the *Rights of Men*, the Revolution history, and the *Letters* make her a major voice in the wider radical Enlightenment.
- The *Vindication* is not a polite request for accomplishments; it demands institutional change — national co-education — and challenges men's power directly.
- She is not hostile to marriage or motherhood; she wants them grounded in friendship and reason rather than in charm and dependence.
- "Masculine" in her mouth is not a call for women to imitate men's manners; it means the rational strength that the age called masculine and that she claims for both sexes.

## What to read first
The *Vindication of the Rights of Woman*, chapters 1–3, gives the argument's core; the dedication to Talleyrand is a model statement of intent. Then sample the *Letters from Sweden* for her voice at its most personal, and pair the *Rights of Men* with Burke for the debate in full.

1. *Rights of Woman*, dedication and introduction — the aim, and the claim to address women "in the grand light of human creatures."
2. *Rights of Woman*, chapters 2–3 — manufactured character and the denial of sexual virtues.
3. *Rights of Woman*, chapter 5, section on Rousseau, read beside *Émile* Book V.
4. *Rights of Woman*, chapter 12 — the national-education plan.
5. *Rights of Men*, the first third, beside Burke's *Reflections*.
6. *Letters from Sweden*, letters 1–8.

## Study questions
1. Reconstruct the "manufactured inferiority" argument in your own words. Why does Wollstonecraft think that pointing to women's actual weaknesses begs the question, and what evidence would settle the matter?
2. Wollstonecraft denies there are "sexual virtues," even modesty. What is her argument from the unity of reason, and how does she redefine modesty so that it applies to both sexes?
3. Compare Rousseau's education of Sophie in *Émile* Book V with Wollstonecraft's reply. Is she right that Rousseau betrays his own principles when he turns to women?
4. Many of her arguments say that educated women will make better wives, mothers and citizens. Does this instrumental case weaken her claim that women are owed education as rational beings, or strengthen it?
5. In the *Rights of Men*, how does she answer Burke's defense of inherited liberties, and how does her definition of "the birthright of man" differ from his idea of an inheritance?
6. Her national-education plan sends children of all ranks to the same free school until nine, then separates them. What does this show about the limits of her egalitarianism?
