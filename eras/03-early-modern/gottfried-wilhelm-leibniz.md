## At a glance

- **Dates:** 1646–1716
- **Place:** Leipzig and Hanover, Germany; travel across Europe
- **School/tradition:** Rationalism
- **Known for:** Monads and pre-established harmony, sufficient reason, the “best of all possible worlds,” and — independently of Newton — the calculus

**Responds to:** [Locke](john-locke.md) · **Answered by:** [Voltaire](bayle-and-voltaire.md), [Kant](immanuel-kant.md), [Émilie du Châtelet](emilie-du-chatelet.md)

## Biography

Leibniz was born in Leipzig in 1646, near the end of the Thirty Years’ War. His father, a professor of moral philosophy, died when he was six, leaving a library in which the boy largely educated himself. He entered Leipzig University at fourteen and received his law doctorate at Altdorf in 1667, after Leipzig refused him, officially for youth.

He declined an academic post and entered service — first in Mainz, then for most of his life at Hanover, as diplomat, librarian, counsellor, and engineer. In Paris (1672–76) he studied under Huygens and made his calculus breakthroughs. In 1676 he visited Spinoza and read his manuscripts closely; that influence is still debated. At Hanover he ran the ducal library, pursued an unfinished history of the ruling house, and spent years on a failed Harz mining project. He corresponded with over a thousand people — a one-man research network.

A universal polymath, he worked seriously in logic, mathematics, physics, geology, law, linguistics, and theology, much of it left in manuscript. He died in Hanover in 1716, out of favor: his employer had become George I of England and left for London, where Leibniz — embroiled in the calculus dispute — was not welcome. His reputation was rebuilt as his papers were edited in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries.

## Major theories and concepts

### Monads and pre-established harmony

Extended matter cannot be ultimate: anything extended is divisible, so its unity is borrowed. What is truly one must be simple, and the only simple things we know from inside are soul-like. Hence *monads*: simple, indivisible, mind-like substances, each with *perception* (representing the world) and *appetition* (an internal drive to the next perception).

Monads are “windowless”: nothing enters or leaves, so they do not causally interact. Each unfolds its history internally, yet *mirrors the entire universe* from its own point of view, with varying clarity — from a stone-like monad’s bare perception to a rational mind.

Why, then, does my willing to raise my arm coincide with my arm rising? *Pre-established harmony*: God created each monad so its states correspond perfectly with every other’s, like clocks built to keep time together without adjustment. Mind and body follow their own laws in perfect synchrony; apparent interaction is what harmony looks like from inside.

### Contradiction and sufficient reason

Leibniz organizes reasoning around two principles. The *principle of contradiction* governs necessary truths: a proposition is necessary if its denial implies a contradiction, as in mathematics and logic. The *principle of sufficient reason* governs contingent truths and existence: no fact is real and no statement true “unless there be a sufficient reason why it is so and not otherwise” (*Monadology* §32, Duncan translation).

That second principle drives his metaphysics. Contingent things cannot supply their own ultimate reason, so the reason for the whole series of contingent things must lie in a necessary being — God. Whether the principle is self-evident, provable, or a rationalist article of faith is still debated.

### Identity of indiscernibles

If two things shared *every* property, there would be no sufficient reason to place one here and the other there — they would be the same thing. Hence no two distinct substances are exactly alike: every monad’s point of view is unique, and, Leibniz claimed, you will never find two perfectly identical leaves. Against Newton he wielded this in the Leibniz–Clarke correspondence: if space were a uniform container, God could have placed the universe three feet left with no difference, violating sufficient reason. Space is therefore *relational* — an order of coexistences, not a thing.

### Theodicy and the best of all possible worlds

Why would a perfect God permit evil? The *Theodicy* (1710) — the work that gave the genre its name — argues God chose the best among infinitely many possible worlds. “Best” is technical: the best *compossible package*. A world is a complete, mutually compatible set of things and events; some evils may be inseparable from a whole that is overall the best combination of variety, order, and perfection. Leibniz distinguishes metaphysical evil (finitude itself), physical evil (suffering), and moral evil (sin), and holds that God *permits* rather than wills evil, as a condition of the best whole.

So the phrase does **not** mean every event is good, or that suffering should be passively approved. [Voltaire’s](bayle-and-voltaire.md) *Candide* (1759), after the Lisbon earthquake, satirises a popularized, Panglossian version — brilliant satire of a vulgarization, not a refutation. Leibniz’s real difficulties lie in how finite minds could verify a global optimum, and whether “best” is coherent if worlds could improve indefinitely.

### Innate ideas as dispositions: the veined marble

Leibniz agreed with Locke that no ideas are present fully formed — and disagreed with almost everything else. In the *New Essays*, his reply to Locke, he argues necessary truths cannot come from experience, which shows what *is*, never what *must* be. The mind contains innate *dispositions and structures*: truths are in us virtually, as veins in a block of marble predispose it to yield one statue rather than another. His amendment to the empiricist slogan: nothing is in the intellect that was not first in the senses — *except the intellect itself*. Experience awakens knowledge; it does not explain its necessity.

### Possible worlds

A possible world is a complete way things could have been — a maximal set of compossible individuals and events. For Leibniz these exist as *ideas in God’s mind*, from which he chose one to actualize. They are not parallel universes alongside ours. Modern possible-worlds talk descends from Leibniz, usually with the theology stripped out.

### Calculus and the Newton dispute

Leibniz published the first account of the differential calculus in 1684. Newton had developed his version earlier but published later; the ensuing priority dispute embittered Leibniz’s last years. Modern scholarship treats the inventions as independent — Newton first in discovery, Leibniz first in publication — and Leibniz’s notation (∫, *d*) is still used. The Leibniz–Clarke correspondence (1715–16), with Newton’s spokesman Samuel Clarke, remains a classic on absolute versus relational space and time.

## Signature arguments

**The argument from sufficient reason to God (*Monadology* §§32–39)**

1. Principle of sufficient reason: no fact is real and no statement true "unless there be a sufficient reason why it is so and not otherwise."
2. The things in the world are contingent: each could have been otherwise, so none supplies its own ultimate reason.
3. Tracing reasons back through earlier contingent things, however far, even to infinity, never gives the reason for the whole series, since every member stands in the same need.
4. So the sufficient reason for the whole series must lie outside it, in something that carries the reason for its existence within itself.
Therefore, there exists a necessary being, the ultimate reason of things, which we call God.

**Objection.** David Hume, through the character Cleanthes in the *Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion* (Part IX), denied that the whole series needs a reason beyond its members. A collection is only the uniting of its parts: explain each part by an earlier one and nothing is left over to explain. Later critics add that the principle itself is not self-evident, and that the universe as a whole may simply have no explanation.

**Reply.** Leibniz answers with his own example, in *On the Ultimate Origination of Things* (1697): if a geometry book were copied from an earlier copy forever, each copy would be explained by its predecessor, yet we would still have no reason why there is such a book at all, or why it says what it says. Explaining each member is not explaining why the whole series exists rather than some other series or none. Whether this answers Hume, or simply restates the demand Hume rejects, depends on whether one accepts the principle in its strong form, and that is still debated.

**Against absolute space (Leibniz–Clarke correspondence, Third Paper §5; Fourth Paper)**

1. God never acts without a sufficient reason; a mere will with no motive would be unworthy of perfect wisdom.
2. Suppose space were an absolute, uniform container, as Newton held. Then God could have placed the whole universe three feet to the left, or turned it around, with every relation among things exactly as it is now.
3. Nothing could give God a reason to prefer one of these placements to another.
4. Identity of indiscernibles: two situations with no discernible difference are not two situations but one, described in two ways.
Therefore, the shifted world is no different world at all, and space is not a thing but relational: an order of coexisting things.

**Objection.** Samuel Clarke, writing for Newton, replied (Second Reply §1) that where the options are exactly alike, the sufficient reason is often nothing other than the mere will of God. He pressed the point (Fourth Reply): if God could never choose between perfectly alike placements, he could not have placed any two equal, perfectly alike particles of matter at all, so Leibniz's argument proves too much.

**Reply.** Leibniz answered that a mere will without any motive is a fiction, contrary to God's perfection, so Clarke's appeal to bare will gives up the principle of sufficient reason itself. And he accepted the consequence Clarke thought absurd: a wise God would produce no perfectly alike bodies, so there are none in nature, which is just what the identity of indiscernibles says. The exchange ended with Leibniz's death in 1716 and settled nothing: whether a perfect will may choose without a reason, and whether absolute or relational space is right, are both still argued.

## Key works

- *Discourse on Metaphysics* (1686, unpublished in his lifetime) — A compact statement of the system; the alarmed replies of Arnauld, to whom he sent a summary, produced a famous correspondence.
- *Theodicy* (1710) — On God’s goodness, freedom, and the origin of evil. The only full-length book published in his lifetime.
- *New Essays on Human Understanding* (written 1704, published 1765) — A chapter-by-chapter dialogue reply to Locke, withheld after Locke’s death in 1704. Locke never saw it.
- *Monadology* (written 1714, posthumous) — About ninety short paragraphs; the purest summary of the monad system. No single Leibniz book contains his whole system.
- *Principles of Nature and Grace* (written 1714; first published 1718) — A companion summary, often read with the *Monadology*.

## Contributions

Leibniz made sufficient reason a central metaphysical tool, put individuality and point of view at reality’s foundation, gave mathematics the calculus in modern notation, and pioneered formal logic, binary arithmetic, and calculating machines. His dream of a universal characteristic — a formal language in which disputes could be settled by calculation, “let us calculate” — anticipates modern logic and computing.

## Impact and legacy

In his own century his influence ran mainly through Wolff’s textbooks — the system Kant inherited and reacted against — while *Candide* fixed a misleading popular image. The deeper recovery came as his logical papers were published: Russell’s study of 1900 presented him as a founder of modern logic. Possible-worlds semantics, analytic metaphysics, and philosophy-of-mind debates about representation are all recognisably Leibnizian, often rediscovered before they were recognized as his.

## Criticisms and debates

The monadology strikes many as extravagant: infinitely many windowless minds, coordinated without interaction, can look like a device for avoiding problems rather than solving them. Sufficient reason is contested — many deny every fact must have an explanation, or that the universe as a whole could have one. The theodicy faces the evidential problem of evil: even granting compossibility, critics argue the scale of suffering makes “best package” implausible. Scholars also debate whether the *Monadology*’s system was present from the 1680s or a late crystallization.

## Misreadings to avoid

- “Best of all possible worlds” means best compossible whole — not that every event is good.
- *Candide* satirises a popularized version, not Leibniz’s argument.
- Possible worlds are ideas in God’s mind, not parallel universes.
- Monads are not physical atoms, and “windowless” does not mean ignorant: each represents the whole universe internally.
- Leibniz was not a cloistered professor; he wrote amid diplomacy, librarianship, and court service.

## What to read first

1. *Monadology* — the whole system in one sitting, with notes.
2. *Discourse on Metaphysics*, §§1–18 — the same ideas, more argument.
3. *Theodicy*, selections on evil — the “best world” claim in actual form.
4. Leibniz–Clarke correspondence, first letters.

## Study questions

1. Reconstruct Leibniz's argument from the principle of sufficient reason: contingent things cannot supply their own ultimate reason, so the reason for the whole series must lie in a necessary being. Where exactly can a critic refuse to follow — the principle itself, or its application to the whole series?
2. Derive the identity of indiscernibles from sufficient reason, then apply it to Newton's universe shifted three feet in absolute space, as in the Leibniz–Clarke correspondence. Why, for Leibniz, would the shift be no difference at all?
3. Press the strongest objection to the best of all possible worlds — the scale of suffering, or the thought that any world could be improved — then build Leibniz's reply using compossibility and God's permitting rather than willing evil. Does the reply answer, or relocate, the difficulty?
4. Compare pre-established harmony with Descartes's interaction and Spinoza's parallelism on one case, your willing raising your arm: what coincides, what causes what, in each system?
