## At a glance
- **Dates:** 1561–1626
- **Place:** England
- **School/tradition:** The new experimental science — its great herald; lawyer, courtier, and statesman who became Lord Chancellor
- **Known for:** Diagnosing the "idols of the mind" that distort inquiry, and proposing a reformed method of induction meant to replace scholastic logic with systematic observation — "knowledge itself is power"

**Responds to:** [Aristotle](../01-ancient/aristotle.md), [Montaigne](michel-de-montaigne.md) · **Answered by:** [Vico](giambattista-vico.md), [J. S. Mill](../04-19th-century/john-stuart-mill.md)

## Biography
Bacon was born in London to a high-ranking family: his father, Sir Nicholas Bacon, was Lord Keeper of the Great Seal under Elizabeth I, and his mother, Anne Cooke Bacon, was a learned translator of religious works. He entered Trinity College, Cambridge, at twelve, and came away unimpressed — he later said the Aristotelian philosophy taught there produced only barren disputation. After legal training at Gray's Inn and a diplomatic posting in France, he entered Parliament and began a long, careful climb through the courts of Elizabeth and then James I.

The details fill in that outline. He was born on 22 January 1561 at York House in the Strand, the younger of Nicholas and Anne's two sons. In April 1573 he went up to Trinity with his elder brother Anthony, tutored by John Whitgift, the future Archbishop of Canterbury, and left in December 1575. Admitted to Gray's Inn in 1576, he spent two and a half years in France with the ambassador Sir Amias Paulet. His father's death in 1579 brought him home and left him, as the youngest son, virtually without support, so the law and royal favour mattered to him for the rest of his life. He was first elected to the Commons in 1581, sat for Melcombe Regis from 1584, and was in every Parliament until 1621.

Under Elizabeth his career stalled. A 1593 speech against the government's tax bill cost him the queen's goodwill, and his patron, the Earl of Essex, failed to win him the posts he wanted. When Essex rebelled in 1601, Bacon helped prosecute him and wrote the official account, a choice that has shadowed his reputation ever since. Meanwhile he wrote: the first *Essays* (1597) appeared with the Latin *Meditationes Sacrae*, and by about 1602–3 he was sketching the "great instauration" of learning.

His rise was spectacular and his fall matched it. Knighted in 1603, he became Attorney General (1613), Lord Keeper (1617), and Lord Chancellor and Baron Verulam (1618), then Viscount St Alban (1621). Along the way he became Solicitor General (1607) and in 1606 married Alice Barnham, then about fourteen. In 1621, Parliament charged him with taking bribes from suitors in cases before him. Bacon admitted accepting gifts — a common, if corrupt, practice — while denying they had swayed his judgments; he was fined, briefly imprisoned in the Tower, and barred from office. The sentence of 3 May set the fine at £40,000; he spent only a few days in the Tower, and the king soon eased the fine. Historians still debate how corrupt he was by the standards of his day, and SEP notes he was caught in a wider campaign against the royal favourite Buckingham, but the disgrace ended his political career.

Retirement gave him five years to write. His philosophical project, the *Instauratio Magna* ("Great Instauration" or Great Renewal), was planned in six parts and never completed; it aimed at nothing less than a total rebuilding of human knowledge on new foundations. In those years he published the *History of the Reign of King Henry VII* (1622), natural histories of the winds and of life and death, the Latin *De Augmentis Scientiarum* (1623), and the final *Essays* (1625).

He died in 1626, on 9 April at Highgate. According to John Aubrey's later account — the famous story, anecdotal in its details — he was traveling in snow near Highgate when he bought a chicken and stuffed it with snow to test whether cold could preserve flesh; he caught a chill, developed pneumonia, and died. Aubrey says he had the story from [Hobbes](thomas-hobbes.md), who used to walk with Bacon and write down his thoughts as they came. Later ages half-seriously called him a martyr to experiment. His essays, histories, and the utopian fragment *New Atlantis* were published and republished long after his death, and his reputation as the philosopher of the Scientific Revolution grew steadily through the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. His chaplain William Rawley issued *Sylva Sylvarum* with *New Atlantis* in 1627 and later collections of his papers.

## Major theories and concepts

### The Great Instauration
"Instauration" means renewal or rebuilding. Bacon's preface says there was only one course left: "to try the whole thing anew upon a better plan, and to commence a total reconstruction of sciences, arts, and all human knowledge, raised upon the proper foundations." The published "Plan of the Work" (1620) lists six parts: a survey of the divisions of the sciences; the *New Organon*, or method; a natural and experimental history; the "Ladder of the Intellect" (worked examples of the method); "Forerunners", provisional results; and finally the new philosophy itself.

**Reasoning:** existing learning is not just incomplete but badly founded, so patching it will not work; the new building must start from a clean survey and a new tool. **Example:** *The Advancement of Learning* is in effect a draft of part one, an inventory of what is known and what is missing. **Tension:** only part two exists in substantial form, and that only in two books, so the system Bacon is famous for is mostly a plan. He called the sixth part "a thing both above my strength and beyond my hopes."

### The idols of the mind
Before method can be reformed, Bacon argues, the mind itself must be cleared of systematic sources of error — the "idols" (*idola*), false notions that "beset" human understanding. He lists four. **Idols of the Tribe** are built into human nature generally: our senses deceive, we see patterns and purposes that are not there, and we prefer confirming instances to counterexamples. **Idols of the Cave** are individual: each person's upbringing, reading, and temperament creates a private cave that refracts the light of nature. **Idols of the Marketplace** come from language — words that name things that do not exist, or badly defined terms that generate empty disputes. **Idols of the Theatre** are received philosophical systems, which Bacon compares to stage plays: elegant invented worlds mistaken for the real one. The point is diagnostic before it is methodological: error is not random but structured, and inquiry must be designed to counteract it.

**Example:** Bacon's best illustration of the Tribe is the temple of votive pictures of sailors who prayed and survived shipwreck (*Novum Organum* I.46). The obvious question is where the pictures of those who prayed and drowned are, and the mind does not ask it. Today we would call this confirmation bias. **Tension:** the doctrine has to be applied to itself. If every mind is distorted by Tribe and Cave, Bacon's own method was designed by such a mind, and he can only answer that a method with external checks corrects minds better than unaided talent does.

### The new induction: tables and exclusion
Bacon's positive method, set out in the *Novum Organum* (1620) — the title announces a "new instrument" to replace Aristotle's *Organon* — rejects the scholastic syllogism, which he says can compel assent but cannot grip nature. Instead, the investigator compiles natural histories: organized tables of instances where a phenomenon (his example is heat) is present, absent, or present in degrees. By comparing these tables and progressively *excluding* candidate explanations, the inquirer narrows in on the "form" of the phenomenon — roughly, its underlying nature or law. Only after this slow ascent from particulars may one cautiously generalize. Bacon is candid that this is laborious; he compares his method to a compass that lets even an unsteady hand draw a true circle, and he insists science should be collective, cumulative work rather than the feat of lone geniuses.

The three tables, worked out in Book II, are the **Table of Essence and Presence** (the sun's rays, fiery meteors, wool and fur: things that are hot); the **Table of Deviation, or of Absence in Proximity** (the moon's rays: things that resemble the first list but lack heat); and the **Table of Degrees** (cases where heat rises and falls). Then comes **exclusion**. Because the moon's rays give light without heat, light cannot be the form of heat. For Bacon a single counterexample is decisive: "any one contradictory instance overthrows a conjecture as to the Form" (II.18). Only after exclusion does he allow a "First Vintage", a provisional positive answer: heat is "a motion, expansive, restrained, and acting in its strife upon the smaller particles of bodies" (II.20).

**Reasoning:** confirming instances pile up without proving anything, but a single good negative instance settles a question. **Tension:** exclusion only works if the list of candidate natures is complete, and nothing in the tables guarantees that. It also has to stop somewhere, which is why Bacon allows the First Vintage: truth "will sooner come out from error than from confusion" (II.20).

### The crucial instance
Among twenty-seven "prerogative instances" (cases with special evidential weight), the fourteenth is the *instantia crucis*. A *crux* is the signpost set up where a road forks, so Spedding translates it "Instance of the Fingerpost". When two causes always appear together, a fingerpost instance separates them and "the question is decided" (II.36). **Example:** is weight a pull from the earth, or a property of the body itself? Compare a weight-driven clock with a spring-driven one at the top of a steeple and at the bottom of a deep mine; if the weight clock slows at height and speeds underground, the earth's attraction is the cause. **Tension:** Bacon's idea passed into science as the "crucial experiment", but later philosophers of science doubt that any single test is decisive, since a failed prediction can always be blamed on an auxiliary assumption.

### Knowledge and power
Bacon's most famous claim is that human knowledge and human power "meet in one": nature, he writes in the *Novum Organum*, cannot be commanded except by being obeyed — "Nature to be commanded must be obeyed." Understanding causes is what makes reliable action — medicine, engineering, agriculture — possible, and the true test of a theory is its fruitfulness in works. This utilitarian aim scandalized some contemporaries and still shapes debate about Bacon: he recast natural philosophy from contemplation of truths into a project for "the relief of man's estate," and imagined it organized institutionally — the research house of Salomon's House in *New Atlantis* is often read as a blueprint for the Royal Society, founded a generation after his death.

**The exact wording.** The slogan "knowledge is power" is not in the *Novum Organum*. Aphorism I.3 reads in Latin *Scientia et potentia humana in idem coincidunt* ("human knowledge and human power meet in one"). The words "knowledge itself is power" translate *nam et ipsa scientia potestas est* in the essay "Of Heresies" in the *Meditationes Sacrae* (1597), and there the subject is God: Bacon is attacking heretics who make God's knowledge wider than his power. The famous phrase is a theological aside; the scientific doctrine is the I.3 version. **Tension:** "command" by "obedience" sounds humble, but the goal is still mastery, the "enlarging of the bounds of human empire" that *New Atlantis* names.

### The moderns and the ancients
Bacon turns the reverence for antiquity upside down. "The old age of the world is to be accounted the true antiquity" (I.84): the ancients lived in the world's youth, so we, who have more experience, are the real elders. Truth, he adds, is "the daughter of time, not of authority." **Example:** printing, gunpowder and the compass, none known to the ancients, had changed the world (I.129). **Tension:** the argument assumes knowledge accumulates, which is exactly what his reform is meant to secure, so it is a hope as much as an observation.

## Key works
- *Essays* (1597; expanded 1612 and 1625) — short, shrewd pieces on ambition, studies, friendship, and revenge; his most-read work in his own lifetime.
- *Meditationes Sacrae* (1597) — short Latin religious meditations printed with the first *Essays*; source of "knowledge itself is power".
- *The Advancement of Learning* (1605) — a survey and defense of learning, arguing for the dignity and expansion of knowledge; Book II maps the sciences and diagnoses their gaps.
- *Novum Organum* (1620) — the methodological core of his project: the idols, the rejection of syllogism, and the new induction. Written in aphorisms, deliberately provisional. Published in the *Instauratio Magna* volume with the Plan of the Work.
- *History of the Reign of King Henry VII* (1622) — written in disgrace; long admired as a model of political history in English.
- *De Augmentis Scientiarum* (1623) — the enlarged Latin version of the *Advancement*, in nine books.
- *New Atlantis* (published posthumously, 1627) — an unfinished utopian tale whose Salomon's House depicts a state-funded research institute.

## In their own words

> Human knowledge and human power meet in one; for where the cause is not known the effect cannot be produced. Nature to be commanded must be obeyed; and that which in contemplation is as the cause is in operation as the rule.

— Francis Bacon, *Novum Organum* I.3 (1620; tr. Spedding, Ellis and Heath, *Works* vol. IV, 1858, p. 47; public domain)

> There are four classes of Idols which beset men's minds. To these for distinction's sake I have assigned names,—calling the first class Idols of the Tribe; the second, Idols of the Cave; the third, Idols of the Market-place; the fourth, Idols of the Theatre.

— *Novum Organum* I.39 (tr. Spedding et al.; public domain)

> The men of experiment are like the ant; they only collect and use: the reasoners resemble spiders, who make cobwebs out of their own substance. But the bee takes a middle course; it gathers its material from the flowers of the garden and of the field, but transforms and digests it by a power of its own.

— *Novum Organum* I.95 (tr. Spedding et al.; public domain)

> If a man will begin with certainties, he shall end in doubts; but if he will be content to begin with doubts, he shall end in certainties.

— *The Advancement of Learning*, Book I, V.8 (1605; Morley edition, 1893; public domain)

> The end of our foundation is the knowledge of causes, and secret motions of things; and the enlarging of the bounds of human empire, to the effecting of all things possible.

— *New Atlantis* (1627; public domain)

## Contributions
Bacon gave the emerging science a philosophy and a rhetoric: a taxonomy of cognitive error, a program of organized fact-gathering and exclusion-based induction, and a justification for science as a public, collaborative enterprise aimed at human welfare. Even where practicing scientists did not follow his method literally, his vision helped legitimate experimental natural philosophy against the universities' Aristotelian curriculum. Two smaller ideas outlived the system: the weight of the negative instance, and the crucial experiment that decides between rival causes.

## Impact and legacy
The founders of the Royal Society (1660) claimed Bacon as their inspiration; Thomas Sprat's official *History* (1667) treats him as the movement's prophet. The French *philosophes* revered him — d'Alembert praised him at length in the *Preliminary Discourse*, and the *Encyclopédie* organized its tree of knowledge on Baconian lines. Voltaire called him "the father of experimental philosophy" (1734), and called the *Novum Organum* the scaffold by which the new philosophy was raised, no longer needed once the building stood. His influence runs onward through Locke and the empiricists to John Stuart Mill's logic of induction. Kant honoured him at the front of the *Critique of Pure Reason*. In the twentieth century he became a target as well as a hero: Adorno and Horkheimer of the [Frankfurt School](../05-20th-century/frankfurt-school.md), Lewis Mumford, and the eco-feminist Carolyn Merchant read the knowledge-as-power program as a root of the West's domineering attitude toward nature.

## Criticisms and debates
- **Did his method produce the science?** Scientists and historians have long noted that Bacon's own method, followed strictly, would rarely have produced the discoveries of Galileo, Kepler, or Newton, who relied on mathematics and bold hypothesis — tools Bacon undervalued. William Harvey reportedly said Bacon wrote philosophy "like a Lord Chancellor." Whether Bacon was a genuine methodologist or mainly a brilliant publicist for science remains debated.
- **The problem of induction.** His inductive program also faces the problem later sharpened by [Hume](david-hume.md): no amount of tabulated instances logically guarantees the next one. Defenders reply that Bacon never claimed mechanical certainty, and that his stress on exclusion, negative instances, and institutional checking anticipates later accounts of scientific method; [Popper](../05-20th-century/karl-popper.md)'s falsificationism also leans on the negative instance, while rejecting induction itself.
- **Mastery of nature.** Is the "empire" of *New Atlantis* benign relief of suffering or a program of domination? Readers split, and the answer often depends on how literally one takes his metaphors of command.
- **The man and the work.** His part in the Essex trial and the 1621 bribery conviction feed a long argument over whether his ethics undercut his calls to serve humanity.

## Misreadings to avoid
- Bacon did not invent "the scientific method" single-handed; he codified and championed tendencies also present in craftsmen, navigators, and earlier natural philosophers.
- The idols are not a list of other people's biases; the Tribe and Cave idols apply to every inquirer, including Bacon's readers.
- "Knowledge is power" is not a cynical slogan about domination; in context it means knowledge of causes enables beneficial works — though critics dispute how innocent that program was.
- Bacon's induction is not simple enumeration of agreeing cases; he called that "childish" and built his method on exclusion.
- He was not hostile to theory; he wanted axioms climbed to by steps, with experience and reason allied like the bee.

## What to read first
Start with the *Essays* for his voice, then *Novum Organum* Book I (the idols and the critique of existing philosophy) before attempting Book II's method in detail. *New Atlantis* is a quick, enjoyable way into his institutional vision.

1. *Essays*: "Of Truth", "Of Studies" and "Of Superstition".
2. The Preface and Plan of the *Instauratio Magna* (1620), a few pages each.
3. *Novum Organum* I.1–3, I.38–68 (the idols) and I.95.
4. *Novum Organum* II.10–20 (heat, the three tables, exclusion), then II.36.
5. *New Atlantis*, especially the description of Salomon's House.

## Study questions
1. Classify a modern error under the four idols, such as reading horoscopes or a debate stalled on an undefined word. Which idol fits best, and could one error belong to two?
2. Reconstruct Bacon's exclusion of light from the form of heat. What has to be true of the candidate list for the conclusion to follow?
3. Why does Bacon say the negative instance is "the more forcible of the two" (I.46)? Connect this to the votive-pictures story and to Hume's problem of induction.
4. Design an instance of the fingerpost for a question of your own, as Bacon did with the two clocks. What auxiliary assumptions could a critic use to resist your verdict?
5. Compare "nam et ipsa scientia potestas est" (1597) with *Novum Organum* I.3. How does the meaning of "knowledge is power" change between them?
6. Is Salomon's House a blueprint for modern science or a warning? Use the "enlarging of the bounds of human empire" in your answer.
