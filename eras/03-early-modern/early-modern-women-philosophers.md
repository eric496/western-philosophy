## At a glance

- **Dates:** Elisabeth of Bohemia 1618–1680 · Margaret Cavendish 1623–1673 · Anne Conway 1631–1679 · Mary Astell 1666–1731
- **Place:** The Hague and Herford; Colchester and continental exile; London and Warwickshire; Newcastle-upon-Tyne and Chelsea
- **School/tradition:** Four positions worked out in and against Cartesianism: critical dualism, vitalist materialism, spirit monism, and Christian Platonist feminism
- **Known for:** The interaction objection dualism never answered (Elisabeth); self-moving, perceiving matter and the first sustained critique of scientific instruments (Cavendish); one created substance of spirit, admired by Leibniz (Conway); Cartesian method as an argument for women's education, and marriage analyzed as absolute monarchy (Astell)

**Responds to:** [Descartes](rene-descartes.md), [Hobbes](thomas-hobbes.md), [Locke](john-locke.md) · **Answered by:** [Leibniz](gottfried-wilhelm-leibniz.md), [Wollstonecraft](mary-wollstonecraft.md)

## Biography

These four are grouped for practical reasons, not doctrinal ones. They worked within a lifetime of each other, on the same problems, under the same disability, and each left a small body of work: Elisabeth's surviving philosophy is one side of a correspondence; Conway's is a single short treatise, published anonymously eleven years after her death; Cavendish published a great deal into a vacuum, with no colleagues who would argue back; Astell wrote more than any of them and was answered mainly by one clergyman and one Lockean.

That disability is also why older histories left them out. Conway was taught by letter because, as a woman, she was barred from Cambridge; Elisabeth refused to let her side of the Descartes exchange be printed, so for two centuries readers saw his replies and guessed at the questions; Cavendish was received as mad, pretentious, a curiosity, and a genius at once, and the eccentricity rather than the arguments became her reputation. The canon-making histories then measured standing by publication, institutional position, and influence on later professors — a filter that excludes all four by construction.

**Elisabeth, Princess Palatine of Bohemia** (born 26 December 1618) was eldest daughter of Frederick V, Elector Palatine, and Elizabeth Stuart, daughter of James I of England. Frederick lost the Bohemian throne in 1620 and the family lived in exile at The Hague, where Elisabeth was tutored in Greek, Latin, French, English, and German and reportedly nicknamed "La Greque" by her siblings. She died on 8 February 1680 as abbess of Herford, a war-damaged community she rebuilt while sheltering Labadists and Quakers.

**Margaret Lucas Cavendish** was born in 1623 in Colchester, Essex, with no formal schooling in mathematics, history, philosophy, or the classical languages. A maid of honor to Queen Henrietta Maria, she followed the queen into French exile in 1644 and married William Cavendish in 1645, remaining abroad until 1660. William hosted the "Cavendish Circle," whose guests included Hobbes, Descartes, Mersenne, Gassendi, and Kenelm Digby. In 1667 she was granted the rare invitation to attend a Royal Society meeting, where much of the audience treated her as the exhibit. She died in December 1673.

**Anne Conway** (née Finch) was born in London in 1631 and raised in the house now known as Kensington Palace. Her half-brother John was a pupil of the Cambridge Platonist Henry More, who agreed to teach her philosophy by letter since she could not attend the university; the course was Cartesian, and the friendship lasted her life. She married Edward, third Viscount Conway, in 1651. Worsening illness brought her the physician-philosopher Francis Mercury van Helmont, who lived in her household for her last decade; through him she met the Jewish kabbalah, which contributed to a decisive break with her Cartesian training, and Quakerism, to which she converted shortly before her death in 1679.

**Mary Astell** was born in Newcastle-upon-Tyne on 12 November 1666 and taught by her uncle Ralph Astell, a Cambridge-educated clergyman-poet. Her father died when she was eleven; in the late 1680s she moved to London, apparently without family support. From 1693 she corresponded with the clergyman-philosopher John Norris, and published steadily until 1709, after which she ran a charity school for poor girls in Chelsea. She died there on 9 May 1731.

## Major theories and concepts

### Elisabeth: the interaction objection, and thought's dependence on the body

In her first letter, dated 6 May 1643, Elisabeth asked Descartes how the soul, being only a thinking substance, can determine the body's "spirits" — the fluids then believed to move nerve and muscle — so as to produce voluntary action. One thing determines another's motion by impulsion, which requires contact, or by the mover's surface and shape; a thinking substance has none of these, so either the soul is not what Descartes says it is or the interaction he needs cannot occur. He answered that the mind-body union is a primitive notion, known by living it rather than by analysis, and later, in *The Passions of the Soul* (1649), put the interaction in the pineal gland. Neither answers her: a primitive notion names the mystery, and a gland is extended matter.

The 1645 letters on regulating the passions show her own commitments. Elisabeth holds that we have real autonomy of thought — we can shift attention from one object to another, so thinking is not ordered by the causal order of matter — while insisting that the capacity for thought depends on the body's overall condition: illness, exhaustion, grief, and the "weaknesses of my sex" (letter of 24 May 1645), an example she pressed and Descartes brushed aside. She will not reduce thinking to bodily states, yet she doubts a thinking thing is strictly a substance, since a substance ought to be independent. What she would put in dualism's place is disputed: Lisa Shapiro reads her as advocating no clear alternative, Frederique Janssen-Lauret as a naturalistic dualist.

### Cavendish: matter that moves and knows itself

Cavendish's central claim is that everything in nature, minds included, is material, and nothing immaterial belongs to nature at all. Her materialism, though it anticipates arguments later associated with [Hobbes](thomas-hobbes.md) and [Hume](david-hume.md), is the opposite of a mechanism: for Hobbes and Descartes matter is inert and must be pushed, while for Cavendish matter moves itself, and bodies at every level of division are perceptive and intelligent. Her argument is an argument from order. Ralph Cudworth held that nature's order must come from chance, from direct divine action, or from a subordinate "Plastick Nature," since dead bodies cannot order themselves; Cavendish denies the premise. If matter is self-moving and knowing, order needs no external arranger — a fly builds its own body competently because the matter composing it perceives and acts.

### Cavendish: against the microscope

In 1665 Robert Hooke published *Micrographia*, the first book to show the public, in large fold-out plates, what ordinary objects look like under a lens; the following year Cavendish published at her own expense two books aimed at the basis of that new science, *Observations upon Experimental Philosophy* and the fiction appended to it, *The Blazing World*. Her objection concerns evidence. Instruments cannot reveal the interior motions of any creature; at best they show exterior shapes, and they distort even those, since curved or flawed glass presents an object deformed or multiplied, and light, shadow, and refraction alter it further — and where the presentation is false, the information taken from it is false too. Magnified appearances are artifacts, not self-interpreting data. In *The Blazing World* she makes the case by satire: bear-men peer through telescopes and quarrel endlessly about what they saw.

### Conway: one created substance, of spirit

Conway derives everything from God's attributes: God is good, wise, just, and above all living, so a creation of dead, inert stuff would contradict its source, and she therefore denies material body as such. There is one created substance, spirit, all of it living, moving, and perceiving; what we call body is spirit in a condensed, sluggish state, and what we call spirit is the same substance refined, so stone and soul differ in degree, not in kind. Against More and Descartes, the dualism of thinking and extended substance goes, and with it the interaction problem — Elisabeth's problem disappears if there is only one substance; against Hobbes and Spinoza, collapsing God into creation is a material pantheism confusing creature with creator, so Conway keeps the distinction with three "species": God; Christ, the "Middle Being" between God and creatures; and the Creatures, none of which can turn into another. Creatures, unlike God, are mutable and multiple, each composed of indefinitely many spirit particles — monads, though not in Leibniz's sense.

### Conway: pain as medicine, and no eternal hell

The metaphysics doubles as a theodicy. Evil is a falling away from God's perfection, and that falling away is a thickening: the creature becomes more corporeal, more inert, less like the living God, and that painful condition is the punishment of the sin that produced it, so sin punishes itself. But punishment is not retributive: pain is purgative, breaking up the sluggish state and starting the creature back toward refinement, in which becoming more spirit-like and becoming better are one process. From this she drew a conclusion dangerous to print — eternal hell would be infinite punishment for finite wrongdoing, which contradicts the nature of a just God, so hell cannot be eternal and all creatures are finally restored.

### Astell: Cartesian method as an argument for educating women

*A Serious Proposal to the Ladies* (1694) is remembered for one demand, an academic retreat amounting to a college for women, but its core is a method. Following Descartes's *Discourse on the Method* and Arnauld and Nicole's *Logic*, Astell teaches rules for thinking and defines knowledge strictly: clear perception followed by firm assent to conclusions rightly drawn from premises of which we have clear and distinct ideas. Knowledge so defined rests on reason, not on sensory experience, so libraries and Latin are not its precondition; women's intellectual shortfalls are circumstantial rather than natural, and training is the remedy. Her argument has a theological floor: each person has a "particle of Divinity" within, so a woman who neglects her mind neglects something divine. This makes education an obligation, and marks the limit of her feminism: the goal is each woman's own perfection, not political equality.

### Astell: marriage, and the Tory who would not extend the argument

*Some Reflections upon Marriage* (1700), occasioned by the scandalous marriage of Hortense Mancini, duchess of Mazarin, analyzes the institution in the vocabulary of politics. A woman marrying is one "who Elects a Monarch for Life, who gives him an Authority she cannot recall however he misapply it, who puts her Fortune and Person entirely in his Power." Contract theorists had built a literature on why irrevocable submission to a sovereign is intolerable, and said nothing about the identical structure in the household. Yet Astell was a High-Church Tory who held the doctrine of passive obedience and denied any right of active resistance, even to a tyrant; her quarrel with [Locke](john-locke.md) is that his natural law of self-preservation licenses rebellion, where she construes self-preservation as preserving the immortal soul. Her famous question — if all men are born free, how is it that all women are born slaves? — is therefore read by some scholars as ironic: a challenge to Whigs who tolerate absolutism in the household.

### Astell against occasionalism and against thinking matter

Two exchanges show Astell working as a metaphysician. The first, with John Norris, became *Letters Concerning the Love of God* (1695); Norris, following Malebranche, held that God is the only true cause, hence the only source of our good and the only proper object of love. Astell objects that occasionalism makes God's creation of material objects vain, since they do nothing, and that our reason for loving God should not rest on a contested thesis about causation. The second exchange was with Damaris Cudworth Masham, a Lockean who attacked the Astell–Norris ideals as harmful to morality; Astell replied inside *The Christian Religion* (1705), which also argues against [Locke](john-locke.md)'s suggestion that matter might think: we can conceive mind as thinking without extension and body as extended without thought, and things of which we can form complete independent ideas are really distinct.

## Key works

- **Elisabeth** — *Correspondence with René Descartes* (1643–1650) — her extant philosophy; the 1643 letters on interaction and the 1645 letters on the passions are the core. Descartes dedicated the *Principles of Philosophy* (1644) to her and wrote *The Passions of the Soul* (1649) at her request.
- **Cavendish** — *Poems and Fancies* (1653), *Philosophical and Physical Opinions* (1656), and *Philosophical Letters* (1664) — the natural philosophy taking shape, the last a letter-by-letter engagement with named contemporaries.
- **Cavendish** — *Observations upon Experimental Philosophy* (1666) with *The Description of a New World, Called the Blazing World* (1666), read together as she published them; and *Grounds of Natural Philosophy* (1668), her system's last and most orderly version.
- **Conway** — *Principia philosophiae antiquissimae et recentissimae* (Amsterdam, 1690), translated as *The Principles of the Most Ancient and Modern Philosophy* (London, 1692) — the whole surviving system, in about a hundred pages.
- **Astell** — *A Serious Proposal to the Ladies* (1694) and *Part II* (1697), the college proposal and the method that justifies it; *Letters Concerning the Love of God* (1695, with John Norris), her case against occasionalism.
- **Astell** — *Some Reflections upon Marriage* (1700), marriage as politics; and *The Christian Religion, as Profess'd by a Daughter of the Church of England* (1705), with the critique of thinking matter and the reply to Masham.

## Contributions

Elisabeth formulated the objection that defines the mind-body problem, in the one form Descartes could not absorb. Cavendish built a materialism of self-moving, perceiving matter that explains natural order without an external arranger, and argued first and at length that an instrument's output must be interpreted before it counts as evidence. Conway built a monism of spirit that dissolves the interaction problem by denying the dualism, and drew from it a theodicy rejecting eternal punishment. Astell turned Cartesian epistemology into an argument that women's intellectual inequality is circumstantial, proposed an institution to repair it, and analyzed marriage with the apparatus of political theory — a move that outlived her own politics.

## Impact and legacy

Elisabeth's influence shows in what Descartes's successors had to do: the systems of Spinoza, Malebranche, and Leibniz are partly attempts to answer her. Conway's reception is the best documented: Leibniz owned her treatise, probably a gift from Van Helmont, wrote in 1697 that his own views came nearer to hers than to other philosophers', and cited her in the *New Essays* as one of the better advocates of vitalism; some scholars trace his monads to her, though hers are both physical and spiritual and his purely spiritual. Astell was read by Locke, Leibniz, and Berkeley, and shaped a line of eighteenth-century defenders of women — Mary Chudleigh, Mary Wortley Montagu, Sarah Chapone — running toward [Wollstonecraft](mary-wollstonecraft.md) and, by the late nineteenth century, to suffragist writers. All four then largely vanished from the histories, returning on a schedule set by editions rather than arguments: Ruth Perry's biography of Astell in 1986, Conway's treatise newly translated in 1996.

## Criticisms and debates

- **Was Elisabeth a philosopher or an excellent interlocutor?** Reconstructing a position from one side of a correspondence is interpretive; the stronger reading, now common, credits her with commitments about causation, mind, and the dependence of thought on body.
- **Is Cavendish's perceiving matter explanatory?** It accounts for order by building order into the parts, which critics say relabels the problem; defenders reply that mechanism had its own unexplained residue in the transfer of motion.
- **How good was the case against instruments?** The distortion argument is sound about seventeenth-century lenses, but her own method offers no error-check, and the program she attacked delivered knowledge she had no way to anticipate.
- **Conway's monism and her species boundaries.** If body and spirit differ only in degree, it is not obvious the three species can be as impassable as she requires, nor that Middle Nature escapes the pantheism she charges to Spinoza; her theodicy also makes suffering instrumental.
- **Astell's feminism and her Tory politics.** Scholars disagree whether *Reflections* argues for women or ironically against Whigs, and whether a thinker who denied all rights of resistance can be a liberationist.

## Misreadings to avoid

- **"Cavendish was an eccentric, not a philosopher."** The self-promotion was used for three centuries to avoid arguments that are systematic and aimed at Hobbes, Descartes, and the Royal Society by name.
- **"Cavendish rejected science."** She rejected the authority of uninterpreted instrument readings and the preference of experiment over reasoning — a position within natural philosophy, not a refusal of it.
- **"Conway was a mystic, not a metaphysician."** Kabbalah and Quakerism shaped her, but the treatise argues from God's attributes to conclusions about substance, against named opponents.
- **"Astell was a proto-liberal feminist."** She was a High-Church Tory who held passive obedience and rejected resistance; her feminism rests on the soul's rational and divine capacities, not on rights.
- **"These four form a school."** On substance, Cavendish's thinking matter and Conway's denial of matter are flatly opposed; the grouping reflects shared conditions and shared problems.

## What to read first

1. The Descartes–Elisabeth letters of May–June 1643 — short, and the clearest statement of the interaction problem.
2. Astell, *A Serious Proposal to the Ladies*, Part I — the argument from Cartesian method to women's education.
3. Astell, *Some Reflections upon Marriage*, with the political vocabulary of [Locke](john-locke.md)'s *Second Treatise* beside it.
4. Cavendish, *Observations upon Experimental Philosophy*, the chapters on micrography, then *The Blazing World*.
5. Conway, *The Principles of the Most Ancient and Modern Philosophy* — God's attributes first, then the chapters against dualism.
