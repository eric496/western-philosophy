## At a glance
- **Dates:** 1706–1749 (Gabrielle Émilie Le Tonnelier de Breteuil, marquise du Châtelet)
- **Place:** France (Paris; Cirey in Champagne; Lunéville in Lorraine, where she died)
- **School/tradition:** Mathematician, physicist, and philosopher working at the intersection of Newtonian science and Leibnizian metaphysics
- **Known for:** Her *Institutions de Physique* (1740), her defense of *vis viva* (living force, proportional to mass times velocity squared) against the Cartesian measure of force, and the French translation of Newton's *Principia* — with her own commentary — that remained the standard French version for centuries and is still the leading one

**Responds to:** [Leibniz](gottfried-wilhelm-leibniz.md) · **Answered by:** [Kant](immanuel-kant.md)

## Biography
Émilie de Breteuil was born in Paris on 17 December 1706. She grew up in a wealthy household where her father, a court official, unusually encouraged his daughter's education: she learned Latin, mathematics, and several modern languages, alongside the fencing and riding she enjoyed. On 12 June 1725, aged eighteen, she married the Marquis du Châtelet, a soldier frequently away on campaign. They had three children; the youngest son died in infancy in 1734. After an initial period of court life she turned seriously to study, taking lessons from leading mathematicians, Pierre-Louis Maupertuis from 1733 and then Alexis Clairaut.

In 1733 she began her long relationship with [Voltaire](bayle-and-voltaire.md). When his *Lettres philosophiques* (1734) was condemned, the couple settled at Cirey, her husband's château in Champagne, which was remodeled to include a laboratory and a theater. There they worked side by side on physics and the popularization of Newton. Voltaire's *Éléments de la philosophie de Newton* (1738) owed much to her, especially on technical points, and he implicitly acknowledged this. In these years she also made a free French version of Mandeville's *Fable of the Bees*, whose preface argues for women's education.

In 1737 both entered the Paris Academy of Sciences' prize competition on the nature of fire, she conducting her experiments in secret from him. Neither won (Leonhard Euler was among the three winners), but the Academy published both essays with the prize-winners. Hers included speculation that different colors of light carry different heating power, which some modern commentators read as an early glimpse beyond the visible spectrum.

The *Institutions de Physique* had a troubled birth. A first version had already passed the censor, who noted Leibnizian ideas in it, when in 1738 she withdrew it for a thorough redrafting. In 1739 the mathematician Samuel König came to Cirey to tutor her and brought the Leibnizian philosophy of Christian Wolff with him. The book appeared in 1740, framed as lessons for her son but in fact a systematic treatise engaging Newton, Leibniz, and Descartes. König then claimed she had taken his ideas, a charge the censor's earlier note makes hard to sustain but which shadowed her reputation for two centuries. A revised edition, *Institutions physiques*, followed in 1742, and German and Italian translations appeared in 1743.

The book's defense of *vis viva* drew a public reply from Jean-Jacques Dortous de Mairan, permanent secretary of the Academy, whose letter to her is dated 18 February 1741. Her *Réponse*, printed in Brussels the same year under the name "Madame ***", answered him point by point, drawing on published experiments.

In the 1740s she undertook her most demanding project: translating Newton's *Principia Mathematica* into French and adding an algebraic commentary. In 1748 she became pregnant by a new lover, the poet and officer Jean-François de Saint-Lambert, whom she had met at the court of Stanisław Leszczyński in Lunéville. At forty-two she feared she would not survive the birth, said so in her letters, and worked punishing hours to finish. She gave birth to a daughter on 4 September 1749 and died on 10 September. Copies of the translation dated 1756 exist, and the full two-volume work appeared in 1759, with a historical preface by Voltaire. Her *Discours sur le bonheur* was first printed in 1779.

## Major theories and concepts

### Foundations of physics: hypotheses, certainty, and metaphysics
**Claim.** The opening chapters of the *Institutions* are philosophy of science, not physics. Du Châtelet argues that physics needs metaphysical foundations — principles such as non-contradiction and sufficient reason — because experience alone underdetermines our theories.

**Reasoning.** Against a crude Newtonian suspicion of hypotheses, she defends them as legitimate and necessary tools: a hypothesis earns its keep by explaining phenomena systematically and surviving tests, though it never becomes certain merely by being useful. She distinguishes grades of certainty and warns against both dogmatism and the opposite vice of rejecting every unproven idea. A hypothesis must rest on many observed facts; it grows probable as it accounts for more of the circumstances of a phenomenon, and grows improbable when circumstances turn up that it cannot explain, as happened to Ptolemy's astronomy (*Institutions*, ch. 4).

**Example.** Copernicus's system began as a hypothesis and, by explaining ever more of the observations, became what she calls a moral certainty. Huygens's hypothesis of a ring around Saturn followed the same path.

**Tension.** This methodological middle path — empiricism disciplined by rational principles — is her characteristic position, and it lets her combine Newton on gravity with Leibniz on the foundations of mechanics where pure partisans chose sides. But it cuts against both camps. Many Newtonians in the Royal Society and the Paris Academy prized "facts" over hypotheses, while she treats Newton's own attraction as a hypothesis to be tested. In the SEP, Karen Detlefsen places her "at the start of the hypothetico-deductive tradition".

### The principle of sufficient reason
**Claim.** Du Châtelet takes exactly two first principles of knowledge from Leibniz and Wolff: the principle of contradiction, which separates the possible from the impossible, and the principle of sufficient reason (PSR), on which all contingent truths depend. The PSR says that nothing happens without a reason why it is so rather than otherwise.

**Reasoning.** When we ask someone to explain an action, we do not stop asking until we get a reason that satisfies us. Without the PSR, she argues, we could not tell an ordered world from chaos, nor argue from the order of the universe to a wise creator.

**Example.** Dreams show what a world without sufficient reason would look like. In her example she is writing in her room when her chair suddenly turns into a winged horse, and in an instant she is a hundred leagues away among people long dead. Waking life differs because its events have reasons (*Institutions* §8).

**Tension.** The principle rules out "unintelligible" scholastic causes such as plastic natures and vegetative souls (§§10–12). Yet she also concedes that we rarely know the full sufficient reason of a contingent fact. That leaves open how much work the PSR can do inside physics, as opposed to framing it.

### The vis viva controversy
**Claim.** The sharpest technical dispute of the period asked what quantity is conserved in collisions: the Cartesian measure, mass times velocity (mv, roughly what we now call momentum when direction is handled properly), or Leibniz's *vis viva*, "living force," mass times velocity squared (mv²). Du Châtelet, following Leibniz, argued for mv².

**Reasoning and example.** She recruited experiment. In *Institutions* §584 she reports Willem 's Gravesande's test in which brass balls were driven into a ball of soft clay. A ball of mass 1 moving at speed 3 always made a much deeper dent than a ball of mass 3 moving at speed 1. The two balls have the same mv, so on the Cartesian measure they should act alike. They differ in mv² (9 against 3), and the dents follow that difference. Her published exchange with de Mairan made the case to a wide audience.

**Tension.** The modern resolution is that both sides tracked real quantities — momentum and kinetic energy (our ½mv²) — and the dispute partly dissolved once the concepts were distinguished. Du Châtelet's insistence on deciding the question empirically, and her grasp of energy-like conservation, are often seen as forward-looking. How far she anticipated the later concept of energy is a matter of debate (see below).

### The Principia translation and commentary
**Claim.** Du Châtelet's *Principia* is not a bare translation. She rendered Newton's difficult geometric demonstrations into the algebraic language of continental calculus, making the work usable by French mathematicians.

**Reasoning.** French science could not absorb Newton through his Latin and his geometry alone. Her commentary summarizes the system of the world and adds results that postdate Newton, including Clairaut's work on the shape of the Earth and lunar motion.

**Example.** Voltaire's historical preface to the 1759 edition says that she worked on Clairaut's ideas, did all the calculations herself, and that Clairaut examined and corrected each chapter as she finished it.

**Tension.** In her hands translation was an act of scholarship and transmission: she decided what French science needed to assimilate Newton, and built it. But the same preface is also the main evidence that the mathematics was collaborative, which is why the commentary sits at the center of the originality debate.

### Happiness and the passions
**Claim.** The *Discours sur le bonheur* is a short essay in moral psychology, partly autobiographical. To be happy, she writes, one must be free of prejudices, virtuous, healthy, have tastes and passions, and be open to illusion.

**Reasoning.** Against moralists who tell us to repress our passions, she holds that we are happy only through satisfied tastes or passions. Illusions are not a defect to be burned away by reason but a source of most of our pleasures.

**Example.** The love of study is the passion that contributes most to happiness, because it depends least on other people. It matters more to women, she argues, because they are shut out of the other roads to glory, such as war and government.

**Tension.** The essay sits awkwardly beside the *Institutions*. The physicist demands demonstrations and warns against illusions of the imagination, while the moralist advises us to keep the varnish illusion lays over things. Marcy Lascano (2021) is among the scholars who have written on her account of illusion.

## Key works
- *Dissertation sur la nature et la propagation du feu* (written for the Academy's 1738 prize; published in the Academy's prize volume 1739, separately 1744) — her prize-essay on fire, notable for speculating about radiation beyond visible light.
- *Institutions de Physique* (1740; revised 1742 as *Institutions physiques*) — her systematic treatise: metaphysical foundations, method, and the physics of matter, motion, gravity, and force.
- *Réponse de Madame la Marquise du Châtelet* (1741) — her published reply to de Mairan in the vis viva dispute.
- *Principes mathématiques de la philosophie naturelle* (dated copies 1756; full edition 1759, posthumous) — her translation of Newton with commentary; the standard French *Principia*.
- *Discours sur le bonheur* (published 1779) — a candid essay on happiness, women, and the passions, written in the 1740s.

## In their own words

> Without the principle of sufficient reason, one would no longer be able to say that this universe, whose parts are so interconnected, could only be produced by a supreme wisdom, for if there can be effects without sufficient reason, all might have been produced by accident, that is to say, by nothing.

— Émilie du Châtelet, *Foundations of Physics* §8, in *Selected Philosophical and Scientific Writings*, ed. Judith P. Zinsser, trans. Isabelle Bour and Judith P. Zinsser (University of Chicago Press, 2009), p. 129; French checked against the 1740 Paris edition

> And so good hypotheses will always be the product of the greatest men. Copernicus, Kepler, Huygens, Descartes, Leibniz, and even Newton himself, have all devised useful hypotheses to explain complicated and difficult phenomena.

— *Foundations of Physics* §71 (end of ch. 4, "On Hypotheses"), as translated in Karen Detlefsen, "Émilie du Châtelet," *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*, §5; French checked against the 1740 Paris edition

> When it is a matter of a book of physics, one must ask whether it is good, and not whether the author is English, German, or French.

— *Institutions de Physique* (1740), Avant-propos §VII; translated for this entry from the 1740 Paris edition

> Remember, my son, in all your studies, that experience is the staff that nature has given to us blind men to guide us in our research.

— *Institutions de Physique* (1740), Avant-propos §IX; translated for this entry from the 1740 Paris edition

> By this reason of independence, the love of study is, of all the passions, the one that contributes most to our happiness.

— *Discours sur le bonheur* ("Réflexions sur le bonheur"), translated for this entry from the text in *Opuscules philosophiques et littéraires* (Paris, 1796)

The first two passages show the two halves of her method: a rational principle that makes the world intelligible, and hypotheses that do the work of explaining it. The last shows why study mattered to her personally.

## Contributions
Du Châtelet helped move the vis viva debate toward experiment and energy conservation, gave French readers their Newton, and produced one of the period's most philosophically sophisticated defenses of hypotheses and of rational principles within empirical science. She also modelled — in print and in life — rigorous scientific work by a woman in institutions closed to women.

## Impact and legacy
Her Newton anchored French Newtonianism and is still the leading French translation. Kant read the *Institutions* and the Mairan exchange: his first book, *Thoughts on the True Estimation of Living Forces* (1747), discusses the Marquise du Châtelet by name several times, sometimes agreeing and sometimes objecting. The *Institutions* also served, acknowledged and unacknowledged, as a source for articles in Diderot and d'Alembert's *Encyclopédie*.

Then her reputation narrowed: through the nineteenth and much of the twentieth century she was remembered mainly as Voltaire's companion, her physics treated as derivative. Ira O. Wade argued for her intellectual independence in 1947, while William Barber still called her thought "essentially derivative" in 1967.

Scholarship since the late twentieth century has reversed this, restoring her as an original natural philosopher. Judith Zinsser's biography (2006) and her edition of the *Selected Philosophical and Scientific Writings* (2009) made the texts available in English. Ruth Hagengruber edited *Émilie Du Châtelet between Leibniz and Newton* (Springer, 2011) and founded the Center for the History of Women Philosophers and Scientists at Paderborn University, which is preparing a digital edition of her manuscripts held in St Petersburg. Karen Detlefsen's *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy* entry (2013, revised 2014) treats her as a philosopher of science in her own right. The reassessment is still under way as her manuscripts and correspondence are edited; three complete copies of her *Essai sur l'optique*, long thought lost but for one chapter, have been found in recent years.

## Criticisms and debates
- **Originality.** Older accounts cast her as translator and popularizer, her metaphysics borrowed from Leibniz via Christian Wolff, her mathematics dependent on Clairaut and Maupertuis. Revisionist scholars reply that collaboration was normal in the period, that her synthesis of Leibnizian principles with Newtonian phenomena is itself an original philosophical position, and that her treatment of hypotheses and of vis viva shows independent judgment — including where she parts from her teachers. A balanced current view credits her as a creative systematizer and critic rather than a mere conduit, while acknowledging her dependence on collaborators for some advanced mathematics.
- **König's charge.** The 1740 plagiarism claim is now usually rejected, because the censor saw Leibnizian material in her draft before König arrived. The SEP adds that König's influence on her may have been overestimated, though she may have come to see the need for metaphysical foundations more forcefully after his visit.
- **Energy.** Popular accounts sometimes credit her with discovering the conservation of energy. Others are more cautious: she defended Leibniz's mv² with experiments and helped prepare the ground, but the modern concept of energy, with its factor ½, took shape later.
- **Leibnizian or Wolffian?** Interpreters disagree about how closely her first principles follow Wolff rather than Leibniz. Detlefsen reads her use of the principle of contradiction as closer to Leibniz on some points and to Wolff on others.
- **A Cartesian shape.** Lascano and Detlefsen note that the *Institutions* follows the order of Descartes's *Principles* (first principles, God, then the world), even though she rejects his physics. Whether this makes her a reformer of the Cartesian project or its replacement is a live question.

## Misreadings to avoid
- She was not "Voltaire's mistress who dabbled in science"; the scientific work is substantial, technical, and was recognized by the leading mathematicians of her day.
- Her Newton translation is not a transparent window onto Newton; it is an interpretation, recast in calculus and supplemented with commentary — which is precisely its value.
- She was not simply a Leibnizian or simply a Newtonian; her project was to test each against the other.
- She did not hold that hypotheses are certain. She held that they are indispensable and become probable, sometimes morally certain, as they explain more.
- The *Discours sur le bonheur* is not a treatise on women alone. It is a general account of happiness that includes a sharp argument about women's exclusion.

## What to read first
1. *Institutions de Physique*, Preface (Avant-propos) and chapters 1 and 4 (available in English translation in Zinsser's *Selected Philosophical and Scientific Writings*) — her method, first principles and hypotheses.
2. The vis viva exchange with de Mairan, in excerpt — the debate in action.
3. *Discours sur le bonheur* — short and vivid.
4. Karen Detlefsen's SEP entry, then a modern biography such as Zinsser's for the life.

## Study questions
1. Why does du Châtelet think physics needs metaphysical first principles? Could experience alone settle the vis viva dispute?
2. Use the dream example to explain the principle of sufficient reason. What would physics look like without it?
3. In the clay experiment, why do equal values of mv but unequal values of mv² count as evidence for Leibniz?
4. What makes a hypothesis good, on her account? Does her view fit Newton's "I frame no hypotheses"?
5. Voltaire says Clairaut checked each chapter of her commentary. How should that fact bear on the question of her originality?
6. Does the *Discours sur le bonheur*'s praise of illusion conflict with the *Institutions*'s demand for demonstration?
