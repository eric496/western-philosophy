## At a glance

- **Dates:** Francisco Suárez (1548–1617) · Hugo Grotius (1583–1645)
- **Place:** Granada, Salamanca, Rome and Coimbra; Delft, The Hague, the fortress of Loevestein, Paris
- **School/tradition:** natural law; Suárez the leading Jesuit of "second scholasticism", Grotius a Dutch Protestant jurist and humanist
- **Known for:** rebuilding the medieval theory of natural law for a Europe split by religion and war. Suárez: law needs a lawgiver's will, and political power starts with the consent of the community, not with a king's divine right. Grotius: a law of nature and of nations binding on every people, a systematic law of war, and the famous claim that natural law would hold "even if" there were no God

**Responds to:** [Aristotle](../01-ancient/aristotle.md), [Aquinas](../02-medieval/thomas-aquinas.md), [Ockham](../02-medieval/william-of-ockham.md) · **Answered by:** [Descartes](rene-descartes.md), [Hobbes](thomas-hobbes.md), [Locke](john-locke.md), [Leibniz](gottfried-wilhelm-leibniz.md), [Rousseau](jean-jacques-rousseau.md)

**Why they are grouped here.** A Spanish Jesuit and a Dutch Protestant who never met make an odd pair, and they disagreed on much. They belong together because they are the two hinges between [Aquinas's](../02-medieval/thomas-aquinas.md) natural law and the social-contract theories of [Hobbes](thomas-hobbes.md), [Locke](john-locke.md) and [Rousseau](jean-jacques-rousseau.md). Grotius read Suárez closely. In a letter of 1633 he praised him as a philosopher and theologian of such depth that "he hardly had an equal."

## Biography

**Francisco Suárez (1548–1617)** was born in Granada on 5 January 1548. He made a slow start. When he applied to the Society of Jesus at sixteen his was the only one of fifty applications rejected, for weak health and an unpromising mind; on appeal he was admitted at a low rank. Within a few years his gifts were obvious. He taught at Segovia, Ávila, Valladolid, Alcalá, Salamanca and the Jesuit college in Rome, and then for almost twenty years at Coimbra in Portugal, where Philip II insisted he take the chair. He was a poor preacher, prone to drifting into fine points of doctrine, but an original teacher who liked to go to "the very root" of a problem instead of dictating others' opinions. Critics accused him of straying from Aquinas, and a long rivalry with his fellow Jesuit Gabriel Vázquez outlived them both. He wrote enormously: about twenty-one million words by one count, more than twice the output of Aquinas. His *Metaphysical Disputations* appeared in 1597 and his treatise *On Laws and God the Lawgiver* (*De legibus ac Deo legislatore*) in 1612. In 1613, at the request of the papal nuncio, he published the *Defence of the Catholic Faith* against James I of England. It was burned in London that year and in Paris the next. He died in Lisbon on 25 September 1617.

**Hugo Grotius (1583–1645)**, in Dutch Hugo de Groot, was born in Delft on 10 April 1583. He wrote Latin verse at eight and entered the University of Leiden at eleven. At fifteen he went with the statesman Johan van Oldenbarnevelt on an embassy to France, where Henry IV is said to have called him "the miracle of Holland". He practised law in The Hague, with the Dutch East India Company (V.O.C.) among his clients, became Attorney General of Holland, Zeeland and West Friesland in 1607 and Pensionary of Rotterdam in 1613. Around 1603 the Company asked him to defend its seizure of a rich Portuguese merchant ship near what is now Singapore. The resulting *Commentary on the Law of Prize and Booty* stayed in manuscript, but one chapter appeared in 1609 as *Mare liberum* (*The Free Sea*).

In the quarrel between strict Calvinists and the more tolerant Remonstrants, Grotius and Oldenbarnevelt took the tolerant side. Prince Maurice's coup of August 1618 ended both careers. Oldenbarnevelt was beheaded in May 1619 and Grotius was sentenced to life imprisonment in the castle of Loevestein. What the records show is that he was allowed books, that his wife Maria van Reigersbergen joined him, and that in March 1621 he escaped hidden in a chest used for carrying books, then fled through Antwerp to Paris. Later retellings add colour, such as soldiers joking that the chest was heavy enough to hold an Arminian; those details come from the biographical tradition, not from the official record. In France, on a small and irregularly paid pension, he wrote *On the Law of War and Peace* (*De iure belli ac pacis*), published in Paris in 1625. A return home in 1631 ended with a price on his head. From 1634 he served Sweden as ambassador to France. Recalled to Stockholm in 1645, he survived a shipwreck in the Baltic, set out again, and died in Rostock on 28 August 1645.

## Major theories and concepts

### Suárez: law needs a lawgiver

Suárez defines a law as a command, not just a piece of advice. That puts him between two older views. On one side was what he called extreme naturalism, which he found in Gregory of Rimini: right and wrong are fixed by the nature of acts alone, so our duties would stand even if God gave no laws or did not exist. On the other side was the **voluntarism** he attributed to [Ockham](../02-medieval/william-of-ockham.md): an act is good or bad only because God wills it, so God could in principle command us to hate him.

Suárez takes something from each. With the voluntarists, he holds that natural law is real law only if a superior commands it, so its binding force comes from God's will. Against them, he holds that God's will "is not the whole reason" for good and evil. Some acts are good or wicked in themselves, and God commands or forbids them because of what they are. So the *content* of natural law comes from nature, while its *force as law* comes from God the lawgiver. Whether this is coherent is still argued. John Finnis thinks Suárez slips in duties that bind before any command; Thomas Pink and Terence Irwin offer readings that make the two parts fit.

### Suárez: the ius gentium

Between natural law and the laws of a single state Suárez places the **ius gentium**, the "law of nations." It is human law, not natural law: the laws common to many peoples, though not to all, resting on shared custom rather than on one legislator, and so changeable in principle. Natural law, for example, does not itself divide property or make anyone a slave; it permits these, and the ius gentium regulates them. The idea gave later lawyers a way to talk about rules that bind peoples who answer to no common ruler. It had a darker use too: the same category was invoked to justify punitive war and the enslavement of peoples on other continents.

### Suárez: consent and the critique of divine right

Suárez starts from the claim that each person is by nature free and "subject to no one, save only to the Creator." Political rule therefore needs explaining. Families cannot supply all the skills life requires, and families with no common authority could hardly keep the peace or right wrongs, a point that anticipates [Hobbes](thomas-hobbes.md). People form a political body by an "express or tacit pact" to help one another and to accept a superior. Power over the community belongs first to the community as a whole. It may hand that power to a king, and Suárez thought monarchy usually the best form, but the power remains "possessed immediately by the community." God is the source of political authority only through the people, not by appointing a dynasty.

That is a direct denial of the **divine right of kings**, the doctrine that a monarch receives authority straight from God and answers to God alone. James I held it, and Suárez's *Defence of the Catholic Faith* answered him, adding that a tyrant might in some cases be resisted. Hence the bonfires. Scholars still dispute how close this brings Suárez to the later social-contract tradition. Daniel Schwartz reads consent as the direct source of political obligation; Pink and others argue that for Suárez coercive authority is in the end divinely grounded and consent only settles who holds it.

### Grotius: a law of nature and of nations

Grotius opens *On the Law of War and Peace* by explaining why he wrote it. Across the Christian world he saw nations go to war "for slight causes, or no cause at all," and then fight with no respect for any law. Against the ancient skeptic Carneades, who argued that justice is a convention of the strong or else folly, Grotius claims that human beings have a natural desire for society, a peaceful common life suited to rational creatures. The source of natural law is human nature itself, which would draw us into society even if we lacked nothing. He defines natural law as a dictate of right reason showing that an act, by fitting or clashing with rational nature, is morally necessary or base, and so commanded or forbidden "by the author of nature, God." Such law is so fixed that not even God can change it, just as God cannot make two times two not equal four.

### The "etiamsi daremus" passage

In Prolegomena §11 Grotius adds a sentence that became the most quoted line of the book. All this, he says, would have some validity even if we granted, as we cannot without great wickedness, that there is no God or that God does not care about human affairs. The Latin begins *etiamsi daremus*, "even if we should grant." The next sentences go on to argue that we must obey God as our creator.

Many textbooks treat the passage as the moment natural law became **secular**, meaning detached from theology. The Italian scholar A. P. d'Entrèves argued that Grotius broke with scholasticism in method, not in content, and that his successors completed the job. Others reply that the thought was old: Suárez, writing thirteen years earlier, had already reported a version of it from the fourteenth-century Gregory of Rimini, and rejected it. Terence Irwin thinks Grotius took his theory mainly from Aquinas and Suárez and was "no pioneer"; J. B. Schneewind credits him instead with a new focus on unending conflict. The passage is a thought experiment inside a work full of scripture, by a man who also wrote, in prison, a treatise on the truth of the Christian religion. How much it changed is a live debate.

### Grotius: the free sea and just war

*Mare liberum* argues that the Portuguese hold no title to the eastern seas or their trade, whether by occupation, papal grant or long custom, and that by the law of nations "every nation is free to travel to every other nation, and to trade with it." It was written to defend the Dutch against Portuguese claims in the East Indies, and the English jurist John Selden replied in defence of closed seas. The free-sea principle later became part of international law.

On war, Grotius treats the causes of war as being as many as the causes of lawsuits, since war is how parties with no common judge pursue their rights. Following ancient authorities he names three just causes: defence, recovering what belongs or is owed to us, and punishment. Book III then sets out "moderations" that restrain even a lawful war, in killing, in laying waste, and in taking property. Suárez had already treated just war as a kind of punishment carried out by sovereigns acting as judges, and both men are roots of the modern laws of war.

## Key works

- Suárez, *Disputationes metaphysicae* (*Metaphysical Disputations*, 1597) — a vast survey of metaphysical problems, each with the rival views weighed before his own answer; studied in Jesuit, Lutheran and other universities for a century.
- Suárez, *De legibus ac Deo legislatore* (*On Laws and God the Lawgiver*, 1612) — law in general, natural law and God's will, the ius gentium, political power and custom.
- Suárez, *Defensio fidei catholicae* (1613) — the reply to James I; political power from the community, not from divine right.
- Suárez, *De bello* (a disputation in his treatise on charity) — one of the fullest scholastic theories of just war, covering the causes, the conduct and the aftermath of war.
- Grotius, *Mare liberum* (1609) — freedom of the seas and of trade.
- Grotius, *De iure belli ac pacis* (*On the Law of War and Peace*, 1625; revised in later editions) — the Prolegomena on natural law, then war, its causes, and its limits.

## Contributions

Suárez gave scholastic metaphysics its most complete modern form, sharpened the question of whether moral obligation comes from will or from nature, set out the ius gentium as a body of human law among peoples, and grounded political authority in the community's consent. Grotius turned natural law into a framework for relations between states, gave the first systematic treatise on the law of war and peace, and made the claim that its principles bind every people whatever their religion.

## Impact and legacy

The *Metaphysical Disputations* spread far beyond the Jesuits. Protestant scholastics such as Francis Turretin borrowed freely from it, and it has been called the standard metaphysics of seventeenth-century Lutheran universities. [Descartes](rene-descartes.md), [Leibniz](gottfried-wilhelm-leibniz.md) and Christian Wolff learned scholastic philosophy partly from Suárez, and Descartes, Leibniz and [Hobbes](thomas-hobbes.md) all name him. How much Descartes owed him is debated, since Descartes rarely cited sources. Robert Filmer, the leading English defender of divine right, attacked Suárez's view that peoples first agree on government and only then receive authority from God.

Grotius became the founding name of a modern school of natural law, carried on by Samuel Pufendorf and read for a century in Jean Barbeyrac's heavily annotated edition (1735), translated into English in 1738. [Locke](john-locke.md) belongs to the tradition he shaped. [Rousseau](jean-jacques-rousseau.md) attacked him in *The Social Contract*, saying that his "usual method" was to establish right by fact, and rejected his view that a whole people could give away its liberty to a king. In international law Grotius is often called its founder, though his own Prolegomena names forerunners such as Francisco de Vitoria, Balthazar Ayala and Alberico Gentili, and the Spanish scholastics, Suárez among them, had covered much of the ground.

## Criticisms and debates

- **Did Grotius secularize natural law?** For d'Entrèves the "even if" passage marks a shift of method. Critics note its scholastic precedents, Grotius's own denial that the hypothesis can be granted, and his definition of natural law as commanded by God. Irwin denies he was a pioneer; Schneewind sees something new in his acceptance that conflict cannot be removed.
- **Is Suárez's theory of obligation coherent?** If acts are wicked in themselves before God commands, do we not already have obligations without a lawgiver? Finnis says this is a contradiction; Pink and Irwin defend the theory.
- **Was Suárez a social-contract theorist?** Schwartz says yes in substance; Pink and others say his view lacks the essentials of the later contract theorists.
- **Absolutism.** Grotius allowed that a people might transfer sovereignty wholly and for good, which Rousseau saw as a defence of tyranny. Suárez kept power latent in the community, but also held that a transfer could not normally be withdrawn.
- **Empire.** Both men wrote amid European expansion. *Mare liberum* was a brief for a trading company, and the ius gentium was used to justify slavery and conquest even as Suárez rejected the claim that Native Americans were natural slaves.

## Misreadings to avoid

- **"Grotius was an atheist, or wrote natural law without God."** He calls the hypothesis wicked, argues against it at once, and wrote a treatise on the truth of Christianity.
- **"Suárez was a voluntarist like Ockham."** He thought God's commands are constrained by what acts are in themselves.
- **"The ius gentium means international law in the modern sense."** For Suárez it includes shared customs inside states, and it is human, changeable law.
- **"Grotius invented international law."** He names Vitoria, Ayala and Gentili as forerunners and leans constantly on Roman law and the scholastics.

## In their own words

> "What we have been saying would have a degree of validity even if we should concede that which cannot be conceded without the utmost wickedness, that there is no God, or that the affairs of men are of no concern to Him."

— Grotius, *De iure belli ac pacis*, Prolegomena §11; trans. F. W. Kelsey (Oxford, 1925), p. 13

> "Throughout the Christian world I observed a lack of restraint in relation to war, such as even barbarous races should be ashamed of; I observed that men rush to arms for slight causes, or no cause at all, and that when arms have once been taken up there is no longer any respect for law, divine or human"

— Grotius, *De iure belli ac pacis*, Prolegomena §28; Kelsey trans. (1925), p. 20

> "Just as even God, then, cannot cause that two times two should not make four, so He cannot cause that that which is intrinsically evil be not evil."

— Grotius, *De iure belli ac pacis*, I.1.10.5; Kelsey trans. (1925), p. 40

> "Every nation is free to travel to every other nation, and to trade with it."

— Grotius, *Mare liberum*, ch. I (1609); trans. R. Van Deman Magoffin, *The Freedom of the Seas* (New York, 1916), p. 7

> "man is by his nature free and subject to no one, save only to the Creator"

— Suárez, *De legibus* III.1.1 (1612); as quoted in the *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*, "Francisco Suárez," §4

> "this will of God—that is, this prohibition or precept—is not the whole reason for the goodness and badness that is found in observing or transgressing the natural law"

— Suárez, *De legibus* II.6.11; trans. Sydney Penner, *Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy*, "Francisco Suárez"

> "after that power has been transferred to some individual person, even if it has been passed on to a number of people through various successions or elections, it is still always regarded as possessed immediately by the community"

— Suárez, *De legibus* III.4.8; trans. Penner, *IEP*, "Francisco Suárez"

> "His usual method of reasoning is constantly to establish right by fact."

— Rousseau, *The Social Contract*, Book I, ch. 2, on Grotius; trans. G. D. H. Cole (1913)

## Study questions

1. Suárez says natural law gets its content from nature but its force as law from God's will. Can you have one without the other? Use Finnis's objection.
2. Read Prolegomena §11 with the sentences that follow it. Is Grotius secularizing natural law, or showing how firm it is?
3. If political power is "possessed immediately by the community," when, if ever, may a community take it back? Compare Suárez with [Locke](john-locke.md).
4. Grotius says even God cannot make evil good, as he cannot make two times two not four. What does this suggest about the relation of morality to God's power?
5. Is Rousseau fair to say that Grotius establishes right by fact?

## What to read first

1. Grotius, *On the Law of War and Peace*, Prolegomena (short; Kelsey translation, or the 1738 Barbeyrac translation in Richard Tuck's Liberty Fund edition).
2. Grotius, *On the Law of War and Peace*, Book I, ch. 1 — war, right, and the definition of natural law.
3. Suárez, *On Laws*, Book II, ch. 6, and Book III, chs. 1–4 — natural law and God's will, then political power (in the English *Selections from Three Works of Francisco Suárez*).
4. Rousseau, *The Social Contract*, Book I, chs. 2–4 — to see how the next century answered.
