## At a glance

- **Dates:** Anthony Ashley Cooper, third Earl of Shaftesbury (1671–1713) · Francis Hutcheson (1694–1746) · Joseph Butler (1692–1752)
- **Place:** Dorset and London; Ulster, Dublin and Glasgow; London's Rolls Chapel, then the sees of Bristol and Durham
- **School/tradition:** British moralists; moral sentimentalism; the case against "the selfish theory"
- **Known for:** answering [Hobbes](thomas-hobbes.md) by arguing that human nature includes real benevolence and a built-in power to tell right from wrong — Shaftesbury's "sense of right and wrong", Hutcheson's "moral sense" and "the greatest happiness for the greatest numbers", Butler's supreme conscience and his analysis of self-love

**Responds to:** [Hobbes](thomas-hobbes.md), [Locke](john-locke.md) · **Answered by:** [Hume](david-hume.md), [Adam Smith](adam-smith.md), [Reid](thomas-reid.md), [Kant](immanuel-kant.md), [Bentham](../04-19th-century/jeremy-bentham.md), [Sidgwick](../04-19th-century/henry-sidgwick.md)

**Why they are grouped here.** These three never formed a school in the sense of a master and pupils, and Butler preferred to speak of conscience rather than a moral sense. They belong together because they made one argument in three stages. Shaftesbury attacked the view, which he found in Hobbes and in Locke, that people are moved only by self-interest and are moral only because someone rewards or punishes them. Hutcheson turned Shaftesbury's elegant essays into a theory with a name: the **moral sense**. Butler, a preacher rather than a theorist of the senses, gave the case its sharpest logic. When [Hume](david-hume.md) listed in 1739 the "late philosophers in England" who had put "the science of man on a new footing," his footnote named Locke, Shaftesbury, Mandeville, "Hutchinson" and Butler.

## Biography

**Anthony Ashley Cooper, third Earl of Shaftesbury (1671–1713)** grew up in the household of his grandfather, the first Earl, Whig politician and patron of [John Locke](john-locke.md). The first Earl put Locke in charge of the boy's education. The two stayed friends until Locke's death, though the pupil came to reject much of the tutor's philosophy. Shaftesbury sat in the Commons and then the Lords, but poor health ended his political career around the age of thirty. His first publication (1698) was an edition of sermons by the Cambridge Platonist Benjamin Whichcote, whose preface praised human "good nature" against Hobbes. In 1699 John Toland published an early version of his *Inquiry concerning Virtue*, which Shaftesbury disowned as unauthorized. Most of his famous essays date from 1705–1710, and in 1711 he gathered them, with long new commentaries, as *Characteristicks of Men, Manners, Opinions, Times*. He moved to Naples for the warm air and died there in 1713, still revising; the corrected edition appeared in 1714.

**Francis Hutcheson (1694–1746)** was born in Ulster to a family of Presbyterian ministers of Scottish descent. He studied at Glasgow from 1711, returned to Ireland in 1716, and instead of taking a parish ran a dissenting academy in Dublin. There he wrote his main books. The *Inquiry into the Original of our Ideas of Beauty and Virtue* appeared anonymously in 1725. Its title page announced that "the Principles of the late Earl of Shaftesbury" were "Explain'd and Defended, against the Author of the Fable of the Bees," Bernard Mandeville, who had argued that private vices produce public benefits. The *Essay on the Nature and Conduct of the Passions, with Illustrations on the Moral Sense* followed in 1728. In 1729 Glasgow elected him Professor of Moral Philosophy, the chair of his old teacher Gershom Carmichael. [Adam Smith](adam-smith.md) was among his students, and Smith later held the same chair. Hutcheson died in 1746; his longest work, *A System of Moral Philosophy*, was published posthumously in 1755.

**Joseph Butler (1692–1752)** was born at Wantage in Berkshire, the son of a Presbyterian linen draper, and trained at a dissenting academy. As a student he wrote anonymously to the philosopher Samuel Clarke, pointing out flaws in Clarke's proofs of God, and their respectful correspondence was printed in 1716. He then joined the Church of England and went to Oriel College, Oxford. From 1718 to 1726 he preached at the Rolls Chapel, the chapel of the London equity courts, to an audience of lawyers, which may explain the sermons' close, argumentative style. He published *Fifteen Sermons Preached at the Rolls Chapel* in 1726 and added an important Preface to the second edition (1729). His *Analogy of Religion* (1736), a defense of Christianity against the deists, was what made him famous in his lifetime. He became Bishop of Bristol in 1738 and Bishop of Durham in 1750, and died in 1752.

## Major theories and concepts

### Against "the selfish theory"

The common target was what the eighteenth century called **the selfish theory**, now called *psychological egoism*: the claim that every human motive is at bottom a concern for one's own good. Hobbes was the famous source. Shaftesbury answered first with irony. Hobbes, he wrote, was a "good sociable man" who spent his life warning others of their danger, which is not what a purely selfish man would do; the very act of preaching egoism refuted it. Shaftesbury also placed Locke with Hobbes. In a 1709 letter he charged that Locke's denial of innate ideas made virtue a matter of fashion and law, so that "'Twas Mr. Locke that struck at all Fundamentals." His positive picture was of human beings as social animals whose natural affections aim at the good of their kind. A creature is **virtuous**, on this account, when its affections are in the right balance and it also approves of that balance on reflection.

### Shaftesbury: the "sense of right and wrong"

Shaftesbury's key claim is that the ability to approve and disapprove of motives is part of human nature, not learned from rewards or imposed by a ruler. We reflect on our own affections and those of others, and feel a liking or aversion toward them, much as we respond to beauty in a form or proportion in music. He calls this a **sense of right and wrong** and says it is as natural to us as natural affection itself. Because it is a first principle of our make, no theory can simply argue it away. Only contrary habit can wear it down.

The tension is easy to see. If virtue is like taste, how do we settle disagreements? Shaftesbury's answer leans on harmony: just as beauty depends on real proportion, the right balance of affections is a real fact about a creature, which the sense detects. Whether that makes him a realist about morality or a theorist of feeling is still debated.

### Hutcheson: the moral sense

Hutcheson turned this into theory using [Locke's](john-locke.md) language of ideas. Locke had held that ideas come from the senses and from reflection. Hutcheson added **internal senses**: powers to receive ideas of beauty, order and harmony. The **moral sense** is the power to be pleased by benevolent motives and displeased by malicious ones, whether in ourselves or in others. It works immediately, before any reasoning about our own advantage. Two examples carry the argument (Treatise II, Sect. I). If approval were only a sense of advantage, we would feel the same toward a fertile field or a comfortable house as toward a generous friend, since both are useful to us; we plainly do not. And we admire actions in a distant country or age, whose influence could never reach us. Self-interest cannot explain either response, so there must be a distinct sense.

What the moral sense approves, Hutcheson argues, is **benevolence**, a disinterested desire for the good of others. That led him to a rule for ranking actions. Virtue rises with the amount of good done and the number who enjoy it, "so that, that Action is best, which accomplishes the greatest Happiness for the greatest Numbers." The 1725 title page even promised "an Attempt to introduce a Mathematical Calculation in Subjects of Morality." This is why Hutcheson is called a **proto-utilitarian**, an early forerunner of the utilitarian principle later made central by [Bentham](../04-19th-century/jeremy-bentham.md) and [Mill](../04-19th-century/john-stuart-mill.md). The label needs care. For Hutcheson the calculation ranks acts, but what makes a person good is the motive of benevolence that the moral sense approves.

### Butler: human nature as a system, and the authority of conscience

Butler takes up Shaftesbury's idea that human nature is a structured whole. Its parts are the particular passions and appetites (hunger, resentment, compassion, the desire for esteem), two general principles, **self-love** and **benevolence**, and, above them, **conscience**, "that principle by which we survey, and either approve or disapprove our own heart, temper, and actions." His central move is to separate *strength* from *authority*. A passion may be the strongest motive at a given moment, but conscience by its nature claims to judge and direct the rest. To act naturally, in Butler's sense, is to act as that structure requires, not to follow whichever impulse is loudest. Hence his most famous line: had conscience strength as it had right, "it would absolutely govern the world." In Sermon III he names reasonable self-love and conscience as the chief or superior principles, and argues that in the long run they point the same way.

### Butler: self-love, benevolence and the hedonistic paradox

Butler's analysis of self-love is the part philosophers still teach. **Self-love** is a general, calm concern for one's own happiness. The particular passions are directed at things outside us: hunger at food, compassion at another's relief. Pleasure comes from satisfying a passion, but that pleasure presupposes a prior desire for the object itself. Food tastes good only because we already want food, so there could be no more delight in eating than in "swallowing a stone" without an appetite for one rather than the other (Sermon XI). That the passions are *ours* does not make their *object* ourselves. The selfish theorist confuses the two.

Two consequences follow. First, benevolence is no more opposed to self-love than any other particular passion is. Wanting a friend's good is as compatible with one's own happiness as wanting dinner. Second, happiness is reached mainly by caring about things other than happiness. Someone fixed on their own good loses the very enjoyments they seek: "Immoderate self-love does very ill consult its own interest." Butler himself calls this a paradox; the idea is now known as the **paradox of hedonism**. The 1729 Preface states the general lesson in a sentence that became famous: "Every thing is what it is, and not another thing." Self-love and benevolence are to be distinguished, not opposed.

## Key works

- Shaftesbury, *An Inquiry concerning Virtue, or Merit* (pirated 1699; revised 1711) — the most systematic of his essays: virtue as balanced affection plus reflective approval.
- Shaftesbury, *Sensus Communis: An Essay on the Freedom of Wit and Humour* (1709) — on ridicule as a test of truth, with the ironic reply to Hobbes.
- Shaftesbury, *Characteristicks of Men, Manners, Opinions, Times* (1711; revised 1714) — the collected essays, including *The Moralists*, with Shaftesbury's own *Miscellaneous Reflections* on them.
- Hutcheson, *An Inquiry into the Original of our Ideas of Beauty and Virtue* (1725; revised 1726, 1729, 1738) — Treatise I on beauty, Treatise II on the moral sense and the greatest-happiness rule.
- Hutcheson, *An Essay on the Nature and Conduct of the Passions, with Illustrations on the Moral Sense* (1728) — the passions, and a reply to rationalist critics such as John Balguy.
- Hutcheson, *A System of Moral Philosophy* (posthumous, 1755) — the Glasgow lectures as a full system, including politics.
- Butler, *Fifteen Sermons Preached at the Rolls Chapel* (1726; Preface added 1729) — Sermons I–III on human nature and conscience, XI–XII on self-love and love of neighbor.
- Butler, *The Analogy of Religion* (1736), with the dissertations "Of Personal Identity" and "Of the Nature of Virtue."

## Contributions

Shaftesbury made benevolence and reflective approval the starting point of British ethics and put the analogy between moral and aesthetic judgment on the agenda. Hutcheson named and analyzed the moral sense, offered the first systematic sentimentalist ethics, and wrote down the greatest-happiness formula decades before Bentham. Butler gave the clearest refutation of psychological egoism in the language, distinguished the strength of a motive from its authority, and showed that pursuing happiness directly tends to defeat itself.

## Impact and legacy

The line of descent runs through Scotland. [Hume](david-hume.md) titled a section of *Treatise* Book III "Moral distinctions derived from a moral sense," though he explained moral feeling through sympathy rather than a special faculty. [Adam Smith](adam-smith.md), Hutcheson's student, rebuilt moral approval around sympathy and the impartial spectator. [Reid](thomas-reid.md) treated the moral faculty as a power of judgment rather than a feeling, and his objection to Locke on personal identity used a circularity charge he credited to Butler. [Kant](immanuel-kant.md) cited Hutcheson, and later made the rejection of feeling as morality's foundation central to his own ethics. Hutcheson's formula passed to [Bentham](../04-19th-century/jeremy-bentham.md). [Sidgwick](../04-19th-century/henry-sidgwick.md) faced Butler's pairing of reasonable self-love and conscience in a new form: his "dualism of practical reason" can be read as the worry that the two may not, after all, be reconciled by argument. [G. E. Moore](../05-20th-century/g-e-moore.md) put Butler's "Everything is what it is, and not another thing" on the title page of *Principia Ethica* (1903). Shaftesbury, through his aesthetics and his idea of enthusiasm, was also influential in Germany.

## Criticisms and debates

- **Feeling or reason?** Rationalists such as Samuel Clarke's follower John Balguy objected in 1728 that making virtue depend on an implanted sense makes it arbitrary: a differently built creature would approve of cruelty, and nothing would be wrong with it. Whether Hutcheson was a realist who thought the sense detects moral facts, or a forerunner of views on which moral words express feelings, is a live scholarly dispute.
- **Is benevolence all of virtue?** In Sermon XII Butler seems to say it is; in the dissertation "Of the Nature of Virtue" (1736) he criticizes theories on which overall happiness alone makes an act good, holding that conscience approves and condemns acts apart from their consequences. Most readers take Butler as an early critic of utilitarianism, but whether his position is consistent, or changed, is debated.
- **Did Butler refute egoism?** C. D. Broad thought Butler killed psychological egoism so thoroughly that he seemed to be flogging a dead horse. Later writers, including Elliott Sober, doubt that the "stone" argument is valid, and disagree about what it shows.
- **Conscience and self-love.** Some passages make conscience supreme; others seem to rank reasonable self-love level with it or above it. Interpreters still argue over which principle has the last word in Butler.

## Misreadings to avoid

- **"The moral sense is a sixth sense organ."** It is a power to feel approval at motives, modeled on Locke's "ideas of reflection," not a literal organ.
- **"Hutcheson was a utilitarian."** He supplied the formula, but his theory of virtue rests on approved motives and on God's design, not on consequences alone.
- **"Butler says we should love ourselves less."** He defends reasonable self-love as one of the chief principles; what he attacks is immoderate self-love, which defeats itself.
- **"Shaftesbury denied self-interest."** He held that virtue and interest coincide. His target was the claim that self-interest is the only motive.
- **"These were secular moralists."** All three were religious; Butler was a bishop. Their claim was that morality is grounded in human nature, which they took to be designed.

## Study questions

1. Shaftesbury argues that Hobbes's own conduct refutes his theory. Is this a good argument, or could a careful egoist explain Hobbes's behavior?
2. Hutcheson thinks our approval of a generous act in a distant country shows that approval is not self-interested. What would a Hobbesian say?
3. If the moral sense is part of our make, could a creature built differently be right to approve of cruelty? How might Shaftesbury, Hutcheson and Balguy each answer?
4. Explain Butler's distinction between the strength and the authority of a motive. Why does it matter for what "acting according to nature" means?
5. Reconstruct Butler's "stone" argument. Does it show that psychological hedonism is false, or only that pleasure is not the *object* of our desires?
6. Why might the most direct pursuit of happiness be the least successful? Give an example from your own life.

## What to read first

1. Butler, *Fifteen Sermons*, Sermons I–III and XI — short, rigorous and the best introduction to the whole debate.
2. Hutcheson, *Inquiry*, Treatise II, Sections I–III — the moral sense, benevolence and the greatest-happiness rule.
3. Shaftesbury, *Inquiry concerning Virtue*, Book I — virtue as balanced affection and the "sense of right and wrong."
4. Butler's 1729 Preface, then Hume, *Treatise* Book III, Part I — to see where the argument went next.
