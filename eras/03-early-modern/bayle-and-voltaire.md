## At a glance

- **Dates:** Pierre Bayle (1647–1706) · Voltaire, born François-Marie Arouet (1694–1778)
- **Place:** Le Carla, Toulouse, Geneva, Sedan, then Rotterdam; Paris, England, Cirey, Prussia, Geneva and Ferney
- **School/tradition:** Huguenot refugee scholarship and the "Republic of Letters"; the French Enlightenment; skepticism, deism and the case for religious toleration
- **Known for:** Bayle's footnoted *Historical and Critical Dictionary*, his argument that even a mistaken conscience has rights, and his claim that reason cannot solve the problem of evil; Voltaire's popularising of Locke and Newton, *Candide* against optimism, the Calas campaign and the *Treatise on Tolerance*, and the motto "Écrasez l'infâme"

**Responds to:** [Montaigne](michel-de-montaigne.md), [Descartes](rene-descartes.md), [Spinoza](baruch-spinoza.md), [Malebranche](nicolas-malebranche.md), [Locke](john-locke.md), [Leibniz](gottfried-wilhelm-leibniz.md), [Pascal](blaise-pascal.md) · **Answered by:** [Leibniz](gottfried-wilhelm-leibniz.md), [Hume](david-hume.md), [Diderot](denis-diderot.md), [Rousseau](jean-jacques-rousseau.md), [Lessing](gotthold-ephraim-lessing.md), [Kant](immanuel-kant.md)

**Why they are grouped here.** Bayle and Voltaire never met: Bayle died when Voltaire was twelve. They belong together because the second built on the first. Bayle gave the early Enlightenment its two sharpest weapons, a principled argument for toleration and a habit of showing that every rational system of theology runs into contradictions. Voltaire read him closely, borrowed many of his arguments on evil for *Candide*, and turned them from learned footnotes into satire, pamphlets and public campaigns. Bayle wrote for scholars; Voltaire wrote for everyone.

## Biography

**Pierre Bayle (1647–1706)** was born on 18 November 1647 at Le Carla in the south of France, the son of a poor Huguenot (French Protestant) minister. Money was short, so he did not begin formal schooling until about twenty. At the Jesuit university in Toulouse he converted to Catholicism, apparently sincerely, then returned to Protestantism within days of finishing his studies. That made him a "relapsed" heretic under French law, and he fled to Geneva to work as a tutor. In 1675 he returned to teach philosophy at the Protestant Academy of Sedan, helped by the theologian Pierre Jurieu.

Louis XIV closed Sedan in 1681. Bayle and Jurieu moved to Rotterdam, where the city founded an "Illustrious School" for them. There Bayle published his *Various Thoughts on the Comet* (1682), which attacks superstition and argues that a society of atheists could be morally decent. From 1684 to 1687 he edited, and largely wrote, *News from the Republic of Letters*, one of the first journals of book reviews. In 1685 his brother Jacob was imprisoned in his place over a book Pierre had written, and soon died in his cell; the same year Louis revoked the Edict of Nantes, ending legal toleration of Protestants in France. Bayle answered with his *Philosophical Commentary* (1686–88). His friendship with Jurieu turned into a long feud; Jurieu accused him of atheism and disloyalty, and in 1693 the city took away his chair. Free of teaching, he wrote the *Historical and Critical Dictionary* (1697; enlarged 1702). He spent his last years defending it, and died in Rotterdam on 28 December 1706, still writing. He reportedly said he was dying as a Christian philosopher; whether he meant it is part of the dispute described below.

**Voltaire (1694–1778)** was born François-Marie Arouet in Paris, the son of a prosperous official, and was educated by the Jesuits at the Collège Louis-le-Grand. Witty verse about the powerful earned him a stay in the Bastille as a young man, and his tragedy *Oedipe* (1718) made him famous under his new pen name. In 1726 a quarrel with a powerful aristocrat of the Rohan family led to a second spell in the Bastille and then exile in England (1726–29). There he met English writers, read Locke and the Newtonians, and saw a country where many religious groups lived side by side.

The result was the *Letters Concerning the English Nation* (English 1733; French *Lettres philosophiques*, 1734). In France the book was burned by the public hangman, and Voltaire fled to Cirey, the country house of [Émilie du Châtelet](emilie-du-chatelet.md). Until her death in 1749 the two worked side by side on Newtonian physics. Voltaire then spent a few unhappy years at the court of Frederick the Great of Prussia, settled near Geneva, and in 1759 moved for good to Ferney, just inside France near the Swiss border. That year he published *Candide*. From Ferney he ran a campaign against religious persecution, signing letters with the slogan *Écrasez l'infâme*, "crush the infamous thing." In 1778 he returned to Paris to a hero's welcome and died there the same year. In 1791 the revolutionaries reburied him in the Panthéon.

## Major theories and concepts

### Bayle: the footnote method

The *Dictionary* looks like a reference book of short entries on people from history and the Bible. Most of each page, though, is filled with long footnotes, which Bayle called **remarks**, set in two columns beneath a thin strip of main text. The remarks are where the philosophy happens. Bayle piles up sources, tests them against each other, points out where a famous argument fails, and often stages a debate whose winner he leaves unclear. In the remarks on "Zeno" he presses the old paradoxes of motion and infinite divisibility; in a famous remark to "Pyrrho" a skeptical abbé argues that Christian doctrines such as the Trinity contradict the most evident axioms of reason, so evidence cannot be the mark of truth; in "Spinoza" he raises objections to Spinoza's metaphysics. The form itself teaches a lesson: check sources, hold judgment, and expect the wise to disagree.

### Bayle: toleration and the erring conscience

Bayle's argument for toleration starts from a simple test. If the true religion has a right to persecute, then every religion has it, since each believes it is the true one. That conclusion is monstrous, so no religion has that right. The *Philosophical Commentary* takes the argument to the Bible. Persecutors since Augustine had read Luke 14:23, where a master tells his servant "compel them to come in" to a feast, as permission to force heretics into the Church. Bayle states a rule of natural reason: any literal reading of Scripture that would oblige us to commit a wrong must be false. Forcing belief is a wrong, so the verse must be read figuratively.

The deeper claim is about **conscience**, a person's own judgment of what God requires. We can only act on what seems true to us after honest inquiry. Someone who sincerely, but wrongly, believes a doctrine is not guilty for acting on it; acting against one's conscience would be the real sin. So an **erring conscience**, one that is sincerely mistaken, has the same rights as a correct one. Bayle draws the conclusion that alarmed his allies: the "heretic" has as much right to be left alone as the orthodox believer, and a chapter of the *Commentary* (Part II, ch. VII) argues that toleration must be general, extending to Jews, Muslims, pagans and Socinians (Christians who denied the Trinity). Compare [Locke's](john-locke.md) *Letter Concerning Toleration* (1689), which excludes atheists. How far Bayle's own toleration reached Catholics and atheists is debated.

The obvious worry is that a sincere persecutor also acts on conscience. Bayle's reply is that persecution cannot look right to anyone who examines it honestly by natural reason, so the persecutor's conscience is not merely mistaken but corrupted. Whether that reply holds is still debated.

### Bayle: the problem of evil and the Manichees

The **problem of evil** asks how a God who is all-good and all-powerful could allow suffering and sin. In the articles "Manicheans" and "Paulicians" Bayle revives the ancient **Manichaean** view that the world is ruled by two rival principles, one good and one evil. He thinks the view is absurd as metaphysics, yet he argues that it explains our experience of a mixed world far better than orthodox Christianity does. Then he works through the standard answers, such as the appeal to human free will, and argues that each either limits God or makes God the author of sin. His conclusion is that reason cannot solve the problem; the believer must rely on faith. Leibniz wrote the *Theodicy* (1710) largely to answer him.

### Voltaire: the English lesson

The *Letters* present England as a model. Their opening letters describe the Quakers with amused respect; letter VI praises a country with many sects living in peace; later letters explain Newton's physics and Locke's theory of mind. Locke, Voltaire writes in Letter XIII, did for the soul what an anatomist does for the body: instead of a "romance of the soul," he offered its history, built from experience. The book taught a French public to admire experimental science, empiricism and religious variety, which is why the authorities treated it as an attack on Church and Crown.

### Voltaire: against optimism

[Leibniz](gottfried-wilhelm-leibniz.md) held that God, being perfect, must have created the best of all possible worlds, and the English poet Alexander Pope popularised a similar optimism in verse. Voltaire came to find this offensive. After the Lisbon earthquake of 1 November 1755, which killed tens of thousands on All Saints' Day, he wrote his *Poem on the Lisbon Disaster* (published 1756), asking what sin the dead children had committed. [Rousseau](jean-jacques-rousseau.md), who held to a version of the optimism, replied defending providence. *Candide* (1759) followed: a naïve young man and his tutor Pangloss, who keeps insisting all is for the best, suffer war, earthquake, the Inquisition and slavery. The book ends with the travellers on a small farm, where, as an old Turkish farmer tells them, labour keeps away weariness, vice and want, and Candide's last words replace theory with practice: "we must cultivate our garden."

### Voltaire: deism, the Calas affair and tolerance

Voltaire was a **deist**: he believed in a God who designed the world and stands behind morality, as Newton's ordered universe seemed to show, but he rejected revelation, miracles and Church authority. His target, "l'infâme," was not belief in God but superstition and fanaticism backed by state power.

The cause that made this concrete was the case of Jean Calas, a Protestant merchant in Toulouse. In October 1761 his son was found dead at home; a rumour spread that the father had killed him to stop a conversion to Catholicism, and in March 1762 Calas was broken on the wheel. Voltaire took up the family's cause in pamphlets and letters, and published the *Treatise on Tolerance* (1763) while the case was before the royal Council in Paris. The treatise argues from history, from the Gospels and from common humanity that persecution is cruel and self-defeating. The *Philosophical Dictionary* (1764, expanded later) sets the same views out in short, ironic alphabetical articles.

## Key works

- Bayle, *Various Thoughts on the Occasion of a Comet* (1682) — against superstition; the virtuous atheist and the society of atheists.
- Bayle, *Philosophical Commentary on These Words of Jesus Christ, "Compel Them to Come In"* (1686–88) — the case for toleration from the rights of conscience.
- Bayle, *Historical and Critical Dictionary* (1697; 2nd ed. 1702) — the footnoted encyclopedia; see "Manicheans", "Paulicians", "Pyrrho", "Spinoza", "Zeno".
- Voltaire, *Letters Concerning the English Nation* / *Philosophical Letters* (1733–34) — Quakers, toleration, Newton and Locke.
- Voltaire, *Elements of Newton's Philosophy* (1738) — Newtonian physics for French readers, written in the Cirey years.
- Voltaire, *Poem on the Lisbon Disaster* (1756) and *Candide, or Optimism* (1759) — the earthquake in verse, then the satire of optimism.
- Voltaire, *Treatise on Tolerance* (1763) — the Calas affair and the case against persecution.
- Voltaire, *Philosophical Dictionary* (1764, enlarged editions to 1770) — short articles on religion, morals and politics.

## Contributions

Bayle grounded toleration in the rights of the sincere conscience rather than in prudence or a list of acceptable churches, and made it cover heretics and non-Christians. He sharpened the problem of evil into the form later debated by Leibniz and Hume, and showed through the *Dictionary* that critical scholarship could itself be a way of doing philosophy. Voltaire made the philosopher a public figure who intervenes in particular injustices, carried Newton and Locke into French culture, and gave the critique of optimism its most memorable form.

## Impact and legacy

The *Dictionary* was a commercial success and a scandal, and its influence on Voltaire, Hume, Rousseau and Kant is well established. [Hume](david-hume.md) advised a friend to read its more metaphysical articles, such as "Zeno" and "Spinoza", and several arguments in his *Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion* follow Bayle on evil. The *Encyclopédie* of [Diderot](denis-diderot.md) and d'Alembert belongs to the same tradition of critical reference writing. Kant's essay on the failure of all philosophical attempts at theodicy (1791) may also descend from Bayle's challenge. Voltaire's campaigns helped shape the language of toleration and judicial reform; he promoted Cesare Beccaria's book against torture and the death penalty. Bayle's toleration argument is now read alongside Locke's; Voltaire remains a symbol of free thought for some and of superficial anticlericalism for others.

## Criticisms and debates

- **What did Bayle believe?** Scholars call this the "Bayle enigma." Gianluca Mori reads him as a concealed atheist; Richard Popkin as a "superskeptic"; Elisabeth Labrousse and Walter Rex as a sincere Calvinist whose attacks on reason point to faith (**fideism**, the view that faith, not reason, is the ground of religious belief); others stress his loyalty to rational first principles. Each reading has textual support.
- **Can toleration rest on conscience?** If sincere belief confers rights, the sincere persecutor seems protected too. Bayle's answer depends on a natural moral light that every honest mind shares, which critics find too optimistic.
- **Is *Candide* fair to Leibniz?** Pangloss is a cartoon. Leibniz never said each evil is good, only that the whole world is the best possible. Voltaire's real argument is that such a claim is untestable and morally numbing in the face of real suffering.
- **How tolerant was Voltaire?** Critics point to his scorn for popular religion and to hostile passages about Jews, the subject of a scholarly literature (for example Harvey Mitchell's *Voltaire's Jews and Modern Jewish Identity*); defenders reply that his campaigns protected actual people. The tension is real.

## Misreadings to avoid

- **"I disapprove of what you say, but I will defend to the death your right to say it."** Voltaire never wrote it. Evelyn Beatrice Hall coined the line in *The Friends of Voltaire* (1906) as her own summary of his attitude; see [Quotes in context](../../quotes-in-context.md).
- **"Voltaire was an atheist."** He was a deist who attacked atheism as well as the churches.
- **"Bayle was simply a skeptic who doubted everything."** He held firm moral principles, and his skepticism was aimed mainly at rational theology.
- **"*Candide* refutes Leibniz."** It satirises a popular version of optimism; see the [Leibniz entry](gottfried-wilhelm-leibniz.md).

## In their own words

> "an erroneous Conscience has the same Rights as an enlighten'd Conscience"

— Bayle, *Philosophical Commentary*, Part II, ch. VIII (chapter title in the Contents); anonymous English translation (London, 1708), vol. 1, p. 272

> "Behold the Manichees, with an hypothesis altogether absurd and contradictory, explain what we experience, a hundred times better than the Orthodox do"

— Bayle, *Historical and Critical Dictionary*, abridged English edition (London: Hunt and Clarke, 1826), vol. 2, article "Evil", p. 59

> "If one religion only were allowed in England, the Government would very possibly become arbitrary; if there were but two, the people would cut one another's throats; but as there are such a multitude, they all live happy and in peace."

— Voltaire, *Letters on England*, Letter VI, "On the Presbyterians"; English version of 1733, Cassell edition (1894)

> "they who assert that all is well have said a foolish thing, they should have said all is for the best."

— Voltaire, *Candide*, ch. I, Pangloss speaking (New York: Boni and Liveright, 1918), p. 2

> "Did fallen Lisbon deeper drink of vice / Than London, Paris, or sunlit Madrid?"

— Voltaire, *Poem on the Lisbon Disaster* (1756), opening section; tr. Joseph McCabe, *Toleration and Other Essays* (1912), pp. 255 ff.

> "One does not need great art and skilful eloquence to prove that Christians ought to tolerate each other--nay, even to regard all men as brothers."

— Voltaire, *Treatise on Tolerance* (1763), ch. XXII, "Of Universal Toleration"; tr. McCabe (1912)

> "We are all formed of frailty and error; let us pardon reciprocally each other's folly--that is the first law of nature."

— Voltaire, *Philosophical Dictionary*, "Tolerance"; Carlton House edition (New York, n.d.), p. 302

> "but let us cultivate our garden."

— Voltaire, *Candide*, ch. XXX, Candide's last words (Boni and Liveright, 1918)

## Study questions

1. Bayle says the persecutor's argument proves too much, since every church thinks it is the true one. Is this a good argument? What would a persecutor who is certain of the truth reply?
2. Can a sincerely mistaken conscience really have rights? What follows for a sincere fanatic?
3. Why might Bayle think the Manichaean two-principle view explains experience better, yet still reject it? What does that say about the relation of reason and faith?
4. Is *Candide* an argument against Leibniz, or a different kind of reply? Compare it with Bayle's footnotes as ways of doing philosophy.
5. What does "cultivate our garden" recommend: withdrawal from public life, or a kind of practical work? How does it fit with Voltaire's own Calas campaign?

## What to read first

1. Voltaire, *Candide* — short, funny, and the quickest way into the debate about evil.
2. Voltaire, *Letters on England*, Letters I–VII and XIII — toleration and Locke.
3. Voltaire, *Treatise on Tolerance*, the opening account of Calas and ch. XXII.
4. Bayle, *Philosophical Commentary*, Part I, chs. I–IV — the case against forced belief.
5. Bayle, *Dictionary*, "Manicheans" and "Paulicians" (in any modern selection), then Leibniz's *Theodicy*, Preface.
