## At a glance

- **Dates:** Wilhelm Dilthey (1833–1911) · R. G. (Robin George) Collingwood (1889–1943)
- **Place:** Biebrich on the Rhine, Basel, Kiel, Breslau and Berlin; the English Lake District and Oxford
- **School/tradition:** Hermeneutics (the theory of interpretation) and the philosophy of history; the case for the human sciences as a distinct kind of knowledge
- **Known for:** arguing that studying human beings is not the same activity as studying nature — Dilthey's contrast between *explaining* nature and *understanding* human life, Collingwood's claim that the historian re-thinks the thoughts behind past actions, and his logic of question and answer

**Responds to:** [Vico](../03-early-modern/giambattista-vico.md), [Hamann and Herder](../03-early-modern/hamann-and-herder.md), [Kant](../03-early-modern/immanuel-kant.md), [Schleiermacher](friedrich-schleiermacher.md), [Hegel](g-w-f-hegel.md), [Comte](auguste-comte.md), [Mill](john-stuart-mill.md) · **Answered by:** [Husserl](../05-20th-century/edmund-husserl.md), [Heidegger](../05-20th-century/martin-heidegger.md), [Gadamer](../05-20th-century/hans-georg-gadamer.md), [Ricoeur](../05-20th-century/paul-ricoeur.md)

**Why they are grouped here.** Dilthey belongs to the nineteenth century; Collingwood did all his work in the twentieth and died in 1943, so this grouping is thematic, not chronological. They never met, and Collingwood was sharply critical of Dilthey. They belong together because they asked the same question and gave rival answers. The nineteenth century had made history a philosophical subject (Hegel, Marx) and had also made natural science the model of knowledge (Comte, Mill). If history is knowledge, what kind is it? Is it a weaker version of physics, or something different in kind? Dilthey answered by grounding history in *lived experience*; Collingwood answered by grounding it in *thought*. Reading them side by side shows the choice that later hermeneutics, from Heidegger to Gadamer, inherited.

## Biography

**Wilhelm Dilthey (1833–1911)** was born at Biebrich on the Rhine, two years after Hegel's death, into a clerical family. Following family tradition he began by studying theology at Heidelberg, where Kuno Fischer introduced him to the German idealists. He moved to Berlin and came under two students of [Schleiermacher](friedrich-schleiermacher.md): the philosopher Friedrich Adolf Trendelenburg and the classical philologist August Boeckh. Schleiermacher became his first great subject. In 1859 Dilthey was asked to finish editing Schleiermacher's letters, and his prize essay on Schleiermacher's hermeneutics (1860) led to a commission to write the biography; the first volume appeared in 1870 and the second was never finished in his lifetime. After his doctorate and habilitation (both 1864) he held chairs at Basel (1867), Kiel and Breslau, and in 1882 returned to Berlin to the chair Hegel had once held. He taught there until his retirement and died in 1911 at Seis am Schlern in the Dolomites, while on holiday.

Dilthey was famous for unfinished projects. The *Introduction to the Human Sciences* (1883) was announced as the first of two volumes; the second never appeared. Much of his best work survives as essays, lecture notes and drafts, and his students (Georg Misch, Herman Nohl and others) began publishing the *Gesammelte Schriften* (Collected Writings) after his death; the edition eventually ran to 26 volumes. Readers in English now mostly use the *Selected Works* edited by Rudolf Makkreel and Frithjof Rodi (Princeton, 1985–2019), cited below as SW.

**R. G. Collingwood (1889–1943)** was born at Cartmel Fell, Lancashire, and grew up on the shore of Coniston Water. His father, W. G. Collingwood, was an archaeologist and artist and secretary to John Ruskin. He was taught at home until thirteen, then went to Rugby and to University College, Oxford, and was elected a Fellow of Pembroke College in 1912. He was trained among the Oxford realists (John Cook Wilson, E. F. Carritt), but by about 1916 had turned against them, partly through reading the Italian idealists Benedetto Croce and Giovanni Gentile. In 1913 he translated Croce's book on [Vico](../03-early-modern/giambattista-vico.md). Unusually for a philosopher, he was also a working archaeologist and the leading authority of his day on Roman Britain.

In 1935 he became Waynflete Professor of Metaphysical Philosophy at Oxford. While correcting the proofs of *The Principles of Art* (1938) he suffered the first of a series of strokes, and from then on wrote fast, aware that he might not finish: *An Autobiography* (1939), *An Essay on Metaphysics* (1940) and *The New Leviathan* (1942). He died at Coniston on 9 January 1943, aged 53, and Gilbert Ryle succeeded him in the chair. His most famous book is posthumous. *The Idea of History* (1946) was assembled by his former pupil T. M. Knox from lectures and manuscripts; the manuscript of *The Principles of History*, long thought lost, was found only in 1995 and published in 1999. Knox's editing shaped how Collingwood was read for decades, as the debates below show.

## Major theories and concepts

### Dilthey: explaining and understanding

Dilthey's starting point was a problem left by [Kant](../03-early-modern/immanuel-kant.md). Kant had shown what makes natural science possible; nobody had done the same for history, law, economics, literature and religion. Dilthey called these disciplines the **human sciences** (*Geisteswissenschaften*, literally "sciences of spirit"), meaning both what we call the humanities and the social sciences. His project, sometimes called a "critique of historical reason," was to give them their own foundation instead of forcing them into the mould of physics, as positivists such as [Comte](auguste-comte.md) and [Mill](john-stuart-mill.md) proposed.

His most quoted distinction is between **explaining** (*Erklären*) and **understanding** (*Verstehen*). The natural sciences explain: they treat events from outside and bring them under causal laws. The human sciences understand: they grasp the meaning of human actions and products from within, because the inquirer is the same kind of being as the people studied. In the 1894 *Ideas for a Descriptive and Analytic Psychology* Dilthey puts it in a well-known slogan, roughly "nature we explain, mental life we understand." The historian Johann Gustav Droysen had already drawn a similar contrast in the 1850s and 1860s; Dilthey made it the basis of a whole theory of knowledge. He did not, though, think the human sciences avoid general laws: against Wilhelm Windelband's neat split between law-seeking and individual-describing sciences, he argued that economics and linguistics seek regularities too, and that individual cases only make sense against general patterns.

### Lived experience, expression and understanding

The raw material of the human sciences is **lived experience** (*Erlebnis*): experience as it is lived through, already shot through with feeling, will and value, before it is broken down into neutral sense data. Dilthey also insists that the individual is never an isolated starting point. People are points where many social and cultural systems, and an individual considered apart from history and society is an abstraction.

In his later work (from the 1900 essay "The Rise of Hermeneutics" onward) Dilthey decided that inner experience alone cannot give real understanding, even of oneself. We understand ourselves, as we understand others, through **expressions**: words, gestures, actions, laws, artworks, institutions. His late formula is the triad of *lived experience, expression and understanding*. Understanding is the process of giving an "inside" to signs first met from outside. Here he revived Hegel's idea of **objective spirit**, the shared world of language, custom and institutions in which past minds are deposited and through which we can reach them.

### The hermeneutic circle

Dilthey took from Schleiermacher the **hermeneutic circle**: we understand the parts of a text, a life or an epoch through the whole, but we only reach the whole through its parts. A sentence makes sense in light of the book, and the book only through its sentences. The circle is not vicious; understanding grows by moving back and forth between part and whole. Dilthey extended it from texts to history itself. Biography is the model case: each episode in a life gets its meaning from the life as a whole, which is only visible in its episodes, and in principle the meaning of a life is only complete at its end.

### Worldviews and the charge of historicism

In his last years Dilthey studied **worldviews** (*Weltanschauungen*): comprehensive outlooks that combine a picture of the world, a sense of what is valuable, and an ideal of how to act. He sorted metaphysical worldviews into three recurring types: **naturalism** (Democritus, Hobbes), the **idealism of freedom** (Plato, Kant) and **objective idealism** (Heraclitus, Leibniz, Hegel). Each expresses one side of life, none can be proved against the others, and every metaphysical system has only relative validity.

This invited the charge of **historicism** and **relativism**: if every philosophy is the expression of a historically situated type of life, how can any philosophy, including Dilthey's own, claim to be true? [Husserl](../05-20th-century/edmund-husserl.md) pressed the objection in "Philosophy as Rigorous Science" (1911), and the two exchanged letters about it in the last months of Dilthey's life. Dilthey denied that he was a sceptic. He held that seeing the relativity of worldviews frees us from dogma, and that the human sciences can still reach objective, if never final, results.

### Collingwood: the inside of events and re-enactment

Collingwood drew the line between history and nature in a different place. An event, he says, has an **outside**, everything describable as bodies and movements (Caesar crossing a river), and an **inside**, what can only be described in terms of thought (Caesar defying Republican law). Natural events have only an outside. Historical events are actions, and an action is the unity of both. So the historian, unlike the scientist, does not look for a law covering the event; to know what Brutus thought when he decided to stab Caesar is already to know why the event happened.

How can thoughts long past be known? Only by **re-enactment**: the historian re-thinks them. To understand Plato's argument is to follow it in one's own mind, and to understand Caesar's decision is to work through Caesar's situation as he saw it. Collingwood's slogan is that "all history is the history of thought." This is narrower than it sounds: "thought" means reasoning that can be re-thought, not feelings or sensations, which he held cannot be relived in the same way. And re-enactment is critical, not passive. The historian re-thinks past reasoning in the context of his own knowledge, and so judges it as he re-enacts it.

### The logic of question and answer and absolute presuppositions

In *An Autobiography* (1939) Collingwood describes what he called the **logic of question and answer**, which he says he learned partly from archaeology. You cannot tell what a statement means, or whether it is true, until you know the question it was meant to answer. One consequence is that philosophy has no permanent problems. Plato's *Republic* and Hobbes's *Leviathan* look like rival answers to one question about "the state," but on Collingwood's view they answer different questions, because what their authors meant by a state differed.

*An Essay on Metaphysics* (1940) pushes the logic further back. Every question rests on presuppositions. Some are **relative**: they can themselves be questioned and tested, like a surveyor's assumption that the tape is accurate. Others are **absolute**: they make a whole line of inquiry possible and are never answers to any question within it, like the working scientist's assumption that every event has a cause. Absolute presuppositions, Collingwood argues, are neither true nor false. Metaphysics becomes the historical study of which absolute presuppositions were made by which people at which times.

## Key works

- Dilthey, *Introduction to the Human Sciences*, vol. 1 (1883) — the case for the human sciences as a distinct, non-metaphysical body of knowledge; SW I.
- Dilthey, "Ideas for a Descriptive and Analytic Psychology" (1894) — the explaining/understanding distinction; SW II.
- Dilthey, "The Rise of Hermeneutics" (1900) — a short history of interpretation from antiquity to Schleiermacher; SW IV.
- Dilthey, *The Formation of the Historical World in the Human Sciences* (*Der Aufbau*, 1910) — lived experience, expression, understanding, and objective spirit; SW III.
- Dilthey, "The Types of Worldview" (1911) — the three types of metaphysical worldview; SW VI.
- Collingwood, *The Idea of History* (posthumous, 1946; ed. T. M. Knox; revised ed. 1993) — a history of historical thought from Herodotus to Croce, then the "Epilegomena" on re-enactment and historical imagination.
- Collingwood, *An Autobiography* (1939) — his intellectual life, the attack on Oxford realism, and the logic of question and answer.
- Collingwood, *An Essay on Metaphysics* (1940) — relative and absolute presuppositions, with case studies on the idea of causation.

## Contributions

Dilthey gave the humanities and social sciences their first systematic account of themselves as knowledge, with a vocabulary (*Verstehen*, *Erlebnis*, the human sciences, the hermeneutic circle, worldview) that has been used ever since. He turned hermeneutics from a technique for reading Scripture and classics into a general theory of how humans understand one another and their past. Collingwood gave the clearest English-language argument that history is an autonomous form of knowledge, not applied natural science; showed that meaning depends on the question a statement answers; and proposed a historical, non-dogmatic conception of metaphysics.

## Impact and legacy

Dilthey's influence ran through German philosophy and social theory. [Heidegger](../05-20th-century/martin-heidegger.md) studied him closely, and *Being and Time* (1927) closes its discussion of historicality with Dilthey and his correspondence with Count Paul Yorck von Wartenburg. Max Weber's sociology of *Verstehen*, Georg Misch, Ernst Cassirer and the young Rudolf Carnap all drew on him. [Gadamer's](../05-20th-century/hans-georg-gadamer.md) *Truth and Method* (1960) is in large part an argument with Dilthey, praising his hermeneutics while charging that he still tried to make understanding a method that gives objective knowledge like science. [Ricoeur](../05-20th-century/paul-ricoeur.md) tried to bring explaining and understanding back together in interpretation.

Collingwood's ideas of history fed postwar debates about historical explanation. When Carl Hempel argued in 1942 that historical explanations, like physical ones, need general laws, William Dray and others defended a Collingwoodian alternative: explaining an action by reconstructing the agent's reasons. Quentin Skinner and the "Cambridge School" of the history of political thought adopted the logic of question and answer, reading texts as moves in the arguments of their own time. His work on absolute presuppositions has been compared with Kuhn's paradigms and Wittgenstein's late writings, though these are parallels, not influences.

## Criticisms and debates

- **Psychologism.** Collingwood himself charged in *The Idea of History* that Dilthey, by treating philosophies as expressions of psychological types, reduced history to psychology, a natural science, and so never explained how knowledge (as opposed to reliving) of the individual past is possible. Defenders reply that this fits the middle Dilthey, but not the late work, where understanding goes through public expressions rather than inner states.
- **Relativism.** Husserl, the neo-Kantians and many since ask whether a historicist can avoid self-refutation. Whether Dilthey succeeds is a live dispute; Collingwood faces a parallel charge over absolute presuppositions.
- **Telepathy and empathy.** Early critics such as Patrick Gardiner read re-enactment as a claim that historians can enter other minds by intuition. Recent scholars (for example Giuseppina D'Oro) argue that Collingwood was making a conceptual point about what history studies, the thought as opposed to the act of thinking, not proposing a mysterious method.
- **Over-rationalizing history.** If history is the history of thought, what of passions, unintended consequences, economic forces and irrationality? Collingwood allowed that working historians study much more, but critics doubt he can give those things historical status.
- **One Collingwood or two?** Knox's preface claimed that around 1936–38 Collingwood took a "historicist turn" that made philosophy dissolve into history. Lionel Rubinoff labelled this the "radical conversion hypothesis" and rejected it; most recent studies stress continuity, but the debate is not closed.

## Misreadings to avoid

- **"Verstehen means empathy."** For the late Dilthey understanding works through public expressions and contexts; feeling-with is only a part of it.
- **"Dilthey rejected science."** He wanted the human sciences to be rigorous and empirical. He objected only to copying the methods of physics.
- **"Collingwood says history is only about ideas."** Thought here means the reasoning behind actions, including political and military ones, not just the history of philosophy.
- **"Re-enactment is a technique for checking evidence."** Collingwood insisted on critical use of sources. Re-enactment names what is known, not a substitute for evidence.

## Study questions

1. Dilthey says we explain nature but understand human life. Is there anything in psychology or economics that fits neither side cleanly?
2. Take an episode from your own life. How does the hermeneutic circle describe the way its meaning has changed for you over time?
3. If every philosophy expresses a worldview, can Dilthey's own theory claim to be true? How might he answer?
4. Collingwood says that once the historian knows what Brutus thought, he knows why Caesar was stabbed. What would Hempel say is missing?
5. Pick a famous text and ask what question it was answering. Does it still answer a question we ask today, or a different one?
6. Is "every event has a cause" an absolute presupposition, as Collingwood claims? What would follow if it were simply false?

## What to read first

1. Collingwood, *The Idea of History*, Epilegomena §1 "Human Nature and Human History" and §2 "The Historical Imagination" — short and clear.
2. Collingwood, *An Autobiography*, the chapters on question and answer and on the history of political theory — the logic of question and answer and his account of his own development.
3. Dilthey, "The Rise of Hermeneutics" (1900) — the best short way into Dilthey, and a bridge from Schleiermacher.
4. Dilthey, *Introduction to the Human Sciences*, Book I, opening chapters; then Gadamer, *Truth and Method*, Part II, on Dilthey.
