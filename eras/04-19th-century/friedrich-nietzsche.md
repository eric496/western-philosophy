# Friedrich Nietzsche

*Era: 19th-Century Philosophy (1801 – 1900) · [Era overview](README.md) · [Master index](../../index.md)*

## At a glance

- **Lifespan:** 1844–1900
- **Place:** Röcken and Naumburg, Germany; professor at Basel, Switzerland; then a wandering life in Switzerland and Italy
- **Movement / tradition:** No school in his lifetime; later claimed by existentialism and post-structuralism
- **Known for:** The diagnosis that European culture's Christian values have collapsed ("God is dead"), and the demand to create new values through genealogy, self-overcoming, and affirmation of life as it is.

## Biography

Friedrich Nietzsche was born in 1844 in Röcken, Saxony, the son of a Lutheran pastor who died when Friedrich was four, leaving him to be raised in Naumburg by his mother, grandmother, and aunts — a pious, intensely female household he both loved and fled. A brilliant schoolboy at the elite academy of Schulpforta, he studied philology (classical languages and texts) at Bonn and Leipzig, where he discovered Schopenhauer's *World as Will and Representation* in a bookshop in 1865 and read it like scripture.

His rise was meteoric: at twenty-four, on the strength of his teacher Ritschl's recommendation, he was appointed professor of classical philology at Basel before even completing his doctorate. There he befriended the cultural historian Jacob Burckhardt and, fatefully, Richard Wagner, whose music dramas he adored and whose household at Tribschen became a second home. His first book, *The Birth of Tragedy* (1872), yoked scholarship to Wagnerian enthusiasm and was savaged by professional philologists; his academic reputation never fully recovered.

The break came slowly, then completely. Nietzsche's *Human, All Too Human* (1878), a cool book of aphorisms dedicated to Voltaire, marked his turn from romantic pessimism toward an Enlightenment-style free-spirit critique — and ended the Wagner friendship in mutual bitterness (Wagner's anti-Semitism and German nationalism, which Nietzsche despised, were part of it). Chronic illness — crippling migraines, near-blindness, digestive collapse, probably rooted in conditions still debated — forced him to resign his chair in 1879, aged thirty-four. He spent a decade as a stateless wanderer between Swiss mountains (Sils Maria in the Engadine, where the thought of eternal recurrence came to him in 1881) and Italian and French coastal towns, writing with astonishing fertility in cheap rented rooms. A brief, painful entanglement in 1882 with Lou Salomé — whose hand he sought, proposing more than once and on one occasion by proxy through Rée, and who refused him — left him embittered toward his friend Paul Rée and his sister Elisabeth, who had worked against the match.

In January 1889, in Turin, he collapsed mentally (the story that he embraced a horse being beaten is beloved and poorly documented). He never recovered his sanity. He was cared for first by his mother and then by his sister Elisabeth in Weimar, where she built the Nietzsche Archive, curated his image, welcomed nationalist and later Nazi admirers, and — most consequentially — assembled his notebooks into a book he never wrote (see below). He died in 1900, famous at last, and unable to know it.

### The Nachlass and Elisabeth's editing

Nietzsche left thousands of pages of notebooks (*Nachlass*). After his collapse, Elisabeth Förster-Nietzsche — widow of a prominent anti-Semite, whose views Nietzsche had detested — took control of the estate and, with editors, arranged selections from the notebooks as *The Will to Power* (1901; expanded 1906), presenting it as his masterwork. It is not a book Nietzsche published or even planned in that form; modern critical editions (Colli and Montinari) print the notebooks chronologically and demote *The Will to Power* to a historical artefact of his sister's project. Her promotion of Nietzsche to völkisch and Nazi audiences, despite his explicit contempt for anti-Semites and German nationalism, is regarded by most scholars as a falsification of his legacy — while his genuinely anti-egalitarian, anti-democratic views remain on the page for anyone to read, and should not be whitewashed either.

## Major theories and concepts

### "God is dead" and nihilism

In *The Gay Science* §125 (1882), a madman announces to amused market-goers that "God is dead. God remains dead. And we have killed him." This is a cultural diagnosis, not a celebration of a metaphysical discovery: the Christian God has become unbelievable to educated Europe — undermined by science and by Christian truthfulness itself — yet the whole system of values, meanings, and moral authority built on that belief is still standing, hollow. When it crumbles, Europe faces *nihilism*: the discovery that its highest values devalue themselves and that existence seems to have no goal or answer to "why?" The madman's audience laughs because they do not yet grasp what has happened; Nietzsche's entire later project is the attempt to overcome nihilism rather than enjoy it.

### Genealogy; master and slave morality

The *Genealogy of Morals* (1887) asks a new kind of question about morality: not "is it true?" but "where did it come from, and whom does it serve?" In the First Essay, Nietzsche contrasts *master morality* — the self-valuing of the noble, who calls himself and his like "good" spontaneously and only secondarily calls the low and common "bad" — with *slave morality*, born of *ressentiment*: the powerless creatively invert the masters' values, redefining the masters' strength as "evil" and their own humility, patience, pity, and weakness as "good." The slave revolt in morality (which Nietzsche traces to priestly Judaism and its heir, Christianity) is, in his telling, a triumph of spiritual revenge that conquered the Roman world and still defines European conscience. Note: this master/slave pair is a typology of value-creation, not Hegel's lordship-and-bondage dialectic; the two are often confused and must be kept apart.

The Second Essay traces *bad conscience*: instincts of cruelty that a civilised, enclosed society can no longer discharge outward turn inward, and the soul learns to torture itself — the raw material of guilt. The Third examines the *ascetic ideal* — the priestly, scholarly, and philosophical glorification of self-denial — which gave suffering a meaning ("man prefers to will nothingness than not will") while poisoning life in the process.

### Will to power

Nietzsche's most famous and most contested idea: life, and perhaps the world, is best interpreted as *will to power* — drives everywhere seeking not mere survival but growth, discharge of strength, and overcoming of resistance, including self-overcoming. Whether this is (a) a psychological hypothesis about motivation, (b) a full metaphysics of the world, or (c) a heuristic lens Nietzsche offers for interpretation is a genuine scholarly dispute, made worse because the "book" titled after it is his sister's posthumous construction. What it is *not*, in his texts, is a doctrine of crude political domination or brute force: power is primarily self-mastery and creation.

### Eternal recurrence

Introduced in *The Gay Science* §341 ("the greatest weight") and at the heart of *Thus Spoke Zarathustra*: imagine a demon tells you that this life, in every detail, will recur eternally, unchanged. Would you despair, or could you will it again? Read primarily as an existential test of affirmation — can you say yes to life so completely that you would choose its exact repetition forever? — rather than as a doctrine of cosmology, though Nietzsche sketched physical arguments in his notebooks and the literal reading has defenders.

### Übermensch and the last man

Zarathustra's *Übermensch* (overman) is the figure who creates values after the death of God, remaining "faithful to the earth" rather than inventing heavens. He is an aspiration or regulative ideal, not a biological master race and not a programme. His opposite is the *last man*: the comfortable, riskless, medicated creature of modern mediocrity who "invented happiness" and blinks.

### Perspectivism

Knowledge, for Nietzsche, is always from a perspective, structured by drives, affects, and interests; there is no view from nowhere, no "immaculate perception." But perspectivism is not "anything goes": more perspectives, honestly acknowledged and set against each other, yield a more objective picture ("the more eyes… we are able to employ for the same thing, the more complete will our 'concept' of the thing be"). It is a discipline of intellectual honesty, not a licence for relativism.

### Apollo and Dionysus

The early *Birth of Tragedy* (1872) opposes Apollo — form, image, individuation, dream — to Dionysus — intoxication, dissolution of the individual, the primal unity of suffering and joy. Greek tragedy, at its peak, fused both; Socratic rationalism, he argues, killed tragedy by insisting that existence must be intelligible to be justified.

## Key works

- *The Birth of Tragedy* (1872) — The Apollonian/Dionysian account of Greek art and its death by Socratism; flawed scholarship, electrifying philosophy.
- *Human, All Too Human* (1878) — The free-spirit turn: aphoristic, psychological, anti-romantic.
- *The Gay Science* (1882; expanded 1887) — Contains the madman (§125), eternal recurrence (§341), and amor fati (§276). His most joyful book.
- *Thus Spoke Zarathustra* (1883–85) — A literary, parodic gospel for the overman and recurrence; read late, and not alone.
- *Beyond Good and Evil* (1886) — A "prelude to a philosophy of the future": critique of philosophers' prejudices, master/slave morality in brief.
- *On the Genealogy of Morals* (1887) — His most systematic and teachable work; three essays on the origins of morality.
- *Twilight of the Idols*, *The Antichrist*, *Ecce Homo* (all written 1888; published 1889, 1895, 1908) — The final, incandescent year: compressed polemics and a strange, brilliant self-portrait.

## Contributions

Nietzsche added the *genealogical method* — unmasking ideals by their origins and psychological functions — and made suspicion of morality itself philosophically respectable. He diagnosed nihilism as modernity's central problem, anticipated depth psychology's picture of a self driven by unconscious forces, and reinvented philosophical writing as aphorism, mask, and performance. His concepts — ressentiment, bad conscience, slave morality — became permanent tools.

## Impact and legacy

His influence runs through existentialism (Heidegger, Sartre, Camus), psychoanalysis (Freud insisted he had "more penetrating knowledge of himself than any other man"), French post-structuralism (Foucault took genealogy directly from him; Deleuze and Derrida rebuilt readings of him), and modernist literature from Mann to Musil. The Nazi appropriation, engineered by his sister's archive and selective editing, collapsed under post-war scholarship but still colours his popular image.

## Criticisms and debates

Habermas and others charge that his critique of reason is itself irrationalist and self-refuting: from what standpoint does the genealogist speak? Feminist readers have wrestled with his hostile remarks about women, some treating them as mask and provocation, others as plain prejudice. Scholars debate whether will to power is metaphysics or psychology, whether recurrence is cosmology or test, and how literally to take his anti-egalitarianism — which is unmistakably present and uncomfortable, and which honest readers confront rather than edit away. The largest debate remains interpretive: is Nietzsche a systematic philosopher in disguise, or an anti-systematic thinker whose aphorisms defeat every attempt at a settled doctrine?

## Misreadings to avoid

- "God is dead" is a diagnosis of cultural collapse and coming nihilism, not a triumphant slogan.
- Master/slave morality is not Hegel's master–slave dialectic.
- *The Will to Power* is a posthumous editorial construction, not a book he wrote.
- The Übermensch is not a biological master race; Nietzsche despised anti-Semitism and German nationalism.
- Perspectivism is not the claim that all opinions are equally true.
- Will to power is not simply political domination or brute force.

## What to read first

1. *On the Genealogy of Morals*, First Essay — genealogy and master/slave morality in his clearest prose.
2. *The Gay Science* §§125, 276, 341 — the madman, amor fati, eternal recurrence.
3. *Beyond Good and Evil* selections — then, and only then, *Zarathustra*, ideally with a commentary.
