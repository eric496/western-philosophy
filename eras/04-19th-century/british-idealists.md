## At a glance

- **Dates:** Thomas Hill Green (1836–1882) · Francis Herbert Bradley (1846–1924)
- **Place:** Both at Oxford: Green at Balliol College, Bradley at Merton College
- **School/tradition:** British Idealism (also called Absolute Idealism), drawing on [Kant](../03-early-modern/immanuel-kant.md) and [Hegel](g-w-f-hegel.md)
- **Known for:** Green's attack on [Hume's](../03-early-modern/david-hume.md) empiricism, his "eternal consciousness", his ethics of self-realization and his "positive" freedom, which helped found the New Liberalism; Bradley's "My Station and Its Duties", his argument that relations lead to an endless regress, and his single all-inclusive reality, the Absolute

**Responds to:** [Kant](../03-early-modern/immanuel-kant.md), [Hegel](g-w-f-hegel.md), [Hume](../03-early-modern/david-hume.md), [J. S. Mill](john-stuart-mill.md) · **Answered by:** [Sidgwick](henry-sidgwick.md), [G. E. Moore](../05-20th-century/g-e-moore.md), [Bertrand Russell](../05-20th-century/bertrand-russell.md), [John Dewey](../05-20th-century/john-dewey.md)

**Why they are grouped here.** Green and Bradley were not teacher and pupil, and they disagreed about a good deal: Green was a political reformer and a liberal Christian, Bradley a recluse who distrusted reform and wrote metaphysics of a stranger kind. They belong together because between them they made idealism the leading philosophy in British universities from the 1870s to about 1900. **Idealism** here means the view that reality is in some way mental or spiritual: that what exists is, at bottom, experience or thought, not dead matter standing outside all minds. Both men turned against the native British tradition of empiricism (knowledge built up from sense impressions) and utilitarianism (morality as the pursuit of the greatest pleasure), and both drew on German philosophy to do it. Their story also has a famous ending, the "revolt" of Moore and Russell, which this entry treats as partly a story the victors told.

## Biography

**Thomas Hill Green (1836–1882)** was born at Birkin in Yorkshire, the son of a clergyman. His mother died when he was very young, and his father taught him at home until he went to Rugby School in 1850. At Balliol College, Oxford, from 1855, he was taught by Benjamin Jowett, who introduced him to Greek and to German philosophy. He stayed at Balliol as a lecturer and fellow and in 1877 became Whyte's Professor of Moral Philosophy. He was a powerful teacher whose students remembered his moral seriousness more than his prose, which even admirers found heavy. He also worked in public life. He was a Liberal activist, served as an assistant commissioner on the Taunton Commission on secondary schools in 1865–66, and in 1876 became the first Oxford don elected to the city council by the townspeople rather than by the university. He campaigned for state-funded schools, temperance and university places for women and working men. He married Charlotte Symonds in 1871. He died of blood poisoning in March 1882, aged forty-five. Most of his philosophy appeared after his death: *Prolegomena to Ethics* (1883), edited by A. C. Bradley, F. H. Bradley's younger brother, and three volumes of *Works* (1885–88) edited by R. L. Nettleship.

**Francis Herbert Bradley (1846–1924)** was born at Clapham, near London, into a large and gifted evangelical family. He studied at University College, Oxford, from 1865, and in 1870 was elected to a fellowship at Merton College. It required no teaching and he kept it for life, on condition, under the rules of the time, that he did not marry. In 1871 a severe kidney illness left him in poor health for the rest of his life. He lived quietly in college, often spent the winter at seaside resorts in southern England and on the Mediterranean, and avoided public occasions. He wrote with wit and sharp irony, often against named opponents. His books were *Ethical Studies* (1876), *The Principles of Logic* (1883), *Appearance and Reality* (1893) and *Essays on Truth and Reality* (1914). In 1924 he became the first philosopher to receive the Order of Merit. He died in Oxford in September of that year.

## Major theories and concepts

### Green against Hume: relations need a mind

Green's first target was empiricism, the view that all knowledge is built from simple sense impressions that the mind receives passively. In his long introduction to Hume's *Treatise* (1874) he argued that Hume was the only consistent empiricist, and that Hume's skepticism showed where empiricism really led. Green's key point concerns **relations**: being before, being to the left of, being the cause of. Even the simplest experience, such as seeing that this patch is redder than that one, involves comparing and relating. A bare sensation with no relations to anything else could not be known at all. Relations, Green held, are not given by the senses; they are the work of a mind that holds different items together. Here he follows Kant, who argued that experience needs the activity of a unifying self.

### Green's eternal consciousness

Green then goes beyond Kant. If relations are the work of a mind, whose mind makes the order of nature, which stays the same while each person's knowledge of it grows and fades? Not yours or mine. In *Prolegomena* Book I he argues that there must be a single **eternal consciousness**, outside time, for which the whole system of relations already exists, and that each person's growing knowledge is a gradual sharing in it. He identified this principle with God, understood as present in human minds and history rather than as a being beyond the world. The obvious tension is that the argument seems to prove too much: it is hard to see how a timeless mind can also be the self that slowly learns, and critics then and now doubt that Green's reasoning reaches anything more than the conditions of knowledge.

### Green's ethics: self-realization and the common good

Green's ethics is a form of **perfectionism**, the view that the good life is the development of our best capacities. He calls it **self-realization**: the good for a person is to become what, as a rational and self-conscious being, they are capable of being. He argued against the hedonism of Mill and Sidgwick that pleasure cannot be the end, since a desire for a lasting good is a desire for a kind of self, not for a run of feelings. His most distinctive claim is that the true good is a **common good**. Because we realize ourselves only in relations with others, in families, professions and political communities, the true good cannot be won by one person at another's expense. It "does not admit of the distinction between good for self and good for others." He saw his view as joining Greek ethics, with its focus on a flourishing life, to Kant's ethics of rational duty.

### Green's positive freedom and the New Liberalism

In his 1881 Leicester lecture "Liberal Legislation and Freedom of Contract", Green defended laws such as the Irish Land Act and limits on factory labor against the objection that they interfered with freedom of contract. His reply was a new account of freedom. Freedom is not merely the absence of restraint, being left alone to do as one likes. It is **positive freedom**: a real power to do something worth doing, a power that a society secures for all its members. Freedom of contract, on this view, is valuable only as a means to that end, and where unequal bargaining power leaves people unable to develop themselves, the state may act. This argument gave a philosophical basis to the **New Liberalism** of the 1880s to 1910s, the turn of British liberals toward state schooling, social insurance and labor law. Bosanquet, D. G. Ritchie and L. T. Hobhouse are often named among Green's political heirs, though their views differed.

### Bradley's ethics: "My Station and Its Duties"

*Ethical Studies* works by pushing one-sided views until they break. Essay III attacks "pleasure for pleasure's sake": hedonism cannot give a satisfying end, since pleasures are passing feelings that never add up to a whole self. Essay IV attacks Kant's "duty for duty's sake": a purely formal duty gives no concrete guidance. Bradley's own starting point is that morality means to realize oneself as a whole, and Essay V gives the first answer to what that whole is. A person is not an isolated atom who later joins society; we are made what we are by family, community and nation. To find ourselves we must find our place in that social body, with its concrete duties. The essay draws heavily on Hegel's idea of the ethical life of a community. Bradley then shows its limit in Essay VI: a society may be corrupt, and a person may aim at ideals that no existing station contains. So "my station and its duties" is a stage in the argument, not his final word, though readers often take it as his creed.

### Bradley's regress argument against relations

*Appearance and Reality* Book I argues that the ordinary ideas through which we think the world, such as things with qualities, relations, space, time and the self, all contradict themselves when examined. The best known argument concerns relations. Suppose A and B are related by a relation R. If R is nothing to A and B, they are not really related. If R is something to them, then R must itself be related to A and to B, which needs two further relations, and so on without end. Bradley compares it to links that each need new links to join them. So relations, taken as ultimately real, are unintelligible. Whether this regress is really vicious, or only a puzzle created by treating relations as extra things, is still disputed.

### Bradley's Absolute

What survives the criticism is the **Absolute**: a single, all-inclusive reality which is one harmonious experience, without the divisions our thought imposes. Ordinary things and relations are **appearances**, not illusions but partial and distorted aspects of the whole. They have "degrees of truth and reality" according to how far they approach it. We cannot know the Absolute in detail, but Bradley thought we know its general character, partly from our own experience of feeling before it is divided into subject and object. Unlike Green, he denied that the Absolute is the God of religion, which he counted as one more appearance.

## Key works

- Green, "Introduction" to Hume's *Treatise of Human Nature*, in Green and Grose's edition of Hume's *Philosophical Works* (1874) — the case that consistent empiricism ends in skepticism.
- Green, "Lecture on Liberal Legislation and Freedom of Contract" (1881) — short and readable; positive freedom and the case for reform law.
- Green, *Prolegomena to Ethics* (posthumous, 1883) — Book I on metaphysics and the eternal consciousness, Book III on the common good.
- Green, *Lectures on the Principles of Political Obligation* (delivered 1879–80; in *Works* II, 1886) — rights, the state and the common good.
- Bradley, *Ethical Studies* (1876) — seven essays, including "Pleasure for Pleasure's Sake", "Duty for Duty's Sake" and "My Station and Its Duties".
- Bradley, *The Principles of Logic* (1883) — judgment, inference and a sharp critique of the empiricist psychology of ideas.
- Bradley, *Appearance and Reality* (1893; 2nd ed. 1897) — Book I takes ordinary concepts apart; Book II builds the Absolute.
- Bradley, *Essays on Truth and Reality* (1914) — later papers, including replies to pragmatists and to Russell.

## Contributions

Green brought Kant and Hegel into the center of British philosophy, gave the most searching nineteenth-century critique of Hume's empiricism, and offered an ethics of the common good that replaced pleasure with self-development. His idea of positive freedom changed British liberalism. Bradley gave the sharpest idealist critiques of hedonism and of formal duty, a social account of the self that anticipates later communitarian thought, and a set of arguments about relations that set the agenda for the next generation, including the philosophers who rejected his conclusions.

## Impact and legacy

Through their students, idealism shaped British universities, the civil service and politics for a generation. Bernard Bosanquet, Edward Caird, J. M. E. McTaggart and others developed it, and in the United States Josiah Royce and the young [John Dewey](../05-20th-century/john-dewey.md) worked under Green's influence. [Sidgwick](henry-sidgwick.md) answered both men from the utilitarian side, and in 1902 his *Lectures on the Ethics of T. H. Green, Mr. Herbert Spencer, and J. Martineau* appeared. T. S. Eliot wrote his Harvard doctoral thesis on Bradley.

The usual textbook story then runs as follows. Around 1898, [G. E. Moore](../05-20th-century/g-e-moore.md) and [Bertrand Russell](../05-20th-century/bertrand-russell.md) rebelled at Cambridge, Moore with "The Nature of Judgment" (1899) and "The Refutation of Idealism" (1903), Russell with attacks on the "doctrine of internal relations". That doctrine says that every relation a thing has belongs to its nature, so that nothing could be what it is apart from everything else. Against it they defended **external relations**, which leave their terms unchanged, and a world of many independent things that logic could analyze. Russell and Bradley argued in print from 1900 to 1911. In this story idealism was simply refuted, and analytic philosophy was born from the refutation.

Recent historians question much of this. Stewart Candlish's *The Russell/Bradley Dispute and its Significance for Twentieth-Century Philosophy* (2007) argues that Russell attacked a position Bradley did not hold: Bradley rejected internal relations as ultimately real just as firmly as external ones, since his regress was aimed at relations of every kind. W. J. Mander's *British Idealism: A History* (2011) shows that idealism did not collapse in 1903 but remained strong at Oxford and in Scotland into the 1920s and beyond. On this reading the "revolt against idealism" is partly a founding myth that Russell and Moore told about themselves, most fully in Russell's later memoirs. Historians still disagree about how far to revise it, but few now accept the old version unchanged.

## Criticisms and debates

- **Did Green's argument prove a divine mind?** Sidgwick and later critics accepted that knowledge involves relating, but denied that this shows anything beyond the activity of finite minds. If, as Moore and Russell held, relations can be given in experience, Green's case loses its first step.
- **Is positive freedom dangerous?** Isaiah Berlin's "Two Concepts of Liberty" (1958) warned that talk of a "true" freedom can license coercing people for their own good. Defenders reply that Green meant opportunities the state secures, not a higher self it imposes.
- **Is "My Station and Its Duties" conservative?** Read alone it can seem to tell everyone to keep to their place. Read with Essay VI, it is one stage in an argument that Bradley himself declared incomplete. How conservative his ethics remains is still debated.
- **Is Bradley's regress vicious?** Russell thought it rested on treating relations as things that need further relations to connect them. Defenders such as Candlish argue that Bradley's real point, about how relations unify their terms, has never been fully answered.
- **Was the "revolt" a refutation?** See above: whether Moore and Russell defeated idealism by argument, or whether fashion, new logic and generational change did more of the work, is a live question in the history of philosophy.

## Misreadings to avoid

- **"Idealism means the outside world is imaginary."** Green and Bradley did not deny tables and trees. They held that reality is a system of experience or thought, not that the world is a private dream.
- **"Bradley believed in internal relations."** That label came from Russell. Bradley argued that no relations, internal or external, are ultimately real.
- **"Green was a socialist."** He defended private property and markets. He argued that their value lay in the self-development they made possible, which could justify reform.
- **"'My Station and Its Duties' is Bradley's final ethics."** It is the fifth of seven essays and is criticized in the sixth.
- **"Idealism died in 1903."** It remained a strong presence in British philosophy for decades; the quick death is part of the later legend.

## In their own words

> "there is a consciousness for which the relations of fact, that form the object of our gradually attained knowledge, already and eternally exist; and that the growing knowledge of the individual is a progress towards this consciousness"

— Green, *Prolegomena to Ethics*, Bk I, ch. II, §69; ed. A. C. Bradley (Oxford, 1883), p. 84

> "the idea does not admit of the distinction between good for self and good for others"

— Green, *Prolegomena to Ethics* (1883), Bk III, ch. IV, §235

> "a positive power or capacity of doing or enjoying something worth doing or enjoying, and that, too, something that we do or enjoy in common with others"

— Green, "Lecture on Liberal Legislation and Freedom of Contract" (1881), defining freedom; *Works*, ed. R. L. Nettleship, vol. III (London, 1888), p. 371

> "we have found ourselves, when we have found our station and its duties, our function as an organ in the social organism"

— Bradley, *Ethical Studies*, Essay V, "My Station and Its Duties" (London, 1876), p. 148

> "we are hurried off into the eddy of a hopeless process, since we are forced to go on finding new relations without end"

— Bradley, *Appearance and Reality*, Bk I, ch. III, "Relation and Quality"; 2nd ed., corrected impression (London, 1916), p. 33

> "Metaphysics is the finding of bad reasons for what we believe upon instinct, but to find these reasons is no less an instinct."

— Bradley, *Appearance and Reality*, Preface (1893), quoting his own notebook; 1916 impression, p. xiv

## Study questions

1. Green says even "this is redder than that" involves relations the senses cannot supply. Is he right? How might Hume reply?
2. Does the need for a stable, shared order of nature really require an eternal consciousness? What else could explain it?
3. Green claims the true good admits no distinction between good for self and good for others. Can you think of a good that seems to be purely competitive? Does it count against him?
4. Compare Green's positive freedom with Mill's harm principle in *On Liberty*. Which better explains why compulsory schooling might increase freedom?
5. Reconstruct Bradley's regress argument against relations. Where would Russell stop it, and does that answer Bradley?
6. Is the standard story of Moore and Russell "refuting" idealism a fair account of what happened, or a story told by the winners? What evidence would settle it?

## What to read first

1. Green, "Lecture on Liberal Legislation and Freedom of Contract" — short and concrete; the best way in.
2. Bradley, *Ethical Studies*, Essays V and VI — "My Station and Its Duties" and its sequel.
3. Green, *Prolegomena to Ethics*, Book III, chapters III–IV — the common good.
4. Bradley, *Appearance and Reality*, chapters II–III — qualities, relations and the regress.
5. Then Moore's "The Refutation of Idealism" and a modern history such as Mander's *British Idealism*, to judge the "revolt" for yourself.
