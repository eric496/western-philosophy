# Negative Theology and Mysticism: Pseudo-Dionysius and Meister Eckhart

## At a glance

- **Dates:** Pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite (wrote c. 485 – c. 528; real name and life unknown) · Meister Eckhart (c. 1260 – 1328)
- **Place:** Probably Syria or Palestine, in the Greek-speaking East; Thuringia, Paris, Strasbourg and Cologne
- **School/tradition:** Christian Neoplatonism; apophatic (negative) theology; the Rhineland mystics of the Dominican order
- **Known for:** the *via negativa*, the path to God by denying every name we give him, and God as a "darkness" beyond being and knowing (Dionysius); the ground of the soul, detachment, and the birth of the Word in the soul, and a papal condemnation after his death (Eckhart)

**Responds to:** [Plotinus](../01-ancient/plotinus.md) and [Proclus](../01-ancient/late-neoplatonists.md) (Dionysius); [Augustine](augustine-of-hippo.md), [Maimonides](maimonides.md) and [Thomas Aquinas](thomas-aquinas.md) (Eckhart) · **Answered by:** [Eriugena](john-scotus-eriugena.md) and [Aquinas](thomas-aquinas.md) (on Dionysius); [Nicholas of Cusa](nicholas-of-cusa.md) (on both)

**Why they are grouped here.** Eight centuries separate these two, and they never shared a school, a language or a church. They belong together because they are the two great Western masters of **negative theology**: the claim that God so exceeds every concept that the truest thing we can say of God is what God is *not*. Dionysius gave the West the method and its vocabulary. Eckhart, who knew Dionysius in Latin and quotes him often, pushed the method inward: if God is beyond every name, the soul that would meet God must itself let go of every image and possession. Reading them together shows one idea at its source and at its most daring.

## Biography

**Pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite.** In Acts 17:34, after Paul preaches in Athens, one convert is named: "Dionysius the Areopagite," a member of the city's council of the Areopagus. Around 500 a Greek author wrote under that name, addressing letters to Paul's companion Timothy and to the apostle John, and describing himself as an eyewitness of the eclipse at the Crucifixion. This was a deliberate literary persona; whether it was meant to deceive or followed a convention of writing under an ancient name is debated. What the texts show is that the author knew the Athenian Neoplatonism of **Proclus** (died 485), the last great head of the pagan Platonic school, extremely well. In 1895 Hugo Koch and Josef Stiglmayr showed independently that the chapter on evil in the *Divine Names* follows Proclus' essay *On the Existence of Evils* closely. The first clear citations come from Severus of Antioch between 518 and 528. So the corpus was written between about 485 and 528, probably by a Syrian monk or bishop, and possibly by a former pupil of Proclus' school.

The pseudonym made the works almost apostolic, which is why they mattered so much. Hypatius of Ephesus doubted them in the sixth century, but defenders such as John of Scythopolis and Maximus the Confessor secured them a place in Greek theology. A manuscript sent to the Frankish king Louis the Pious in 827 was translated into Latin by Hilduin, abbot of Saint-Denis, who identified the author with Saint Denis, first bishop of Paris. Because Hilduin's version was nearly unreadable, [Eriugena](john-scotus-eriugena.md) made a new one, completed in 862. Later medieval doubts (Nicholas of Cusa had some) became an open challenge in 1457, when the humanist Lorenzo Valla argued the works could not be apostolic. John Grocyn agreed in 1501, and [Erasmus](../03-early-modern/renaissance-humanists.md) publicized the case from 1504. Some still resisted. John Parker, whose 1897–99 translation is used below, believed the writings genuine, so his notes are unreliable on the dating question even though his translation is serviceable.

**Meister Eckhart (c. 1260 – 1328)**, also called Eckhart of Hochheim, was born around 1260, probably at Tambach near Erfurt in Thuringia, and joined the Dominican order as a teenager. He may have studied at Cologne under Albert the Great before Albert's death in 1280. In Paris he lectured on Peter Lombard's *Sentences* in 1293. He then served as prior at Erfurt, where he gave the *Talks of Instruction* to young friars. In 1302 he returned to Paris as *magister*, master of theology, the title that made him "Meister"; his *Parisian Questions* date from this term. He then led the new Dominican province of Saxony. In 1311 Paris called him back for a second term as master, an honor previously granted only to [Thomas Aquinas](thomas-aquinas.md). From 1313 he worked in Strasbourg as vicar for the Master General, preaching to Dominican nuns and Beguines (lay religious women), and he moved to Cologne about 1323–24.

There the trouble began. In 1326 two friars gave Archbishop Henry II of Virneburg a list of 74 suspect propositions from Eckhart's Latin and German works. Eckhart answered the commission that September. He said a mistaken opinion is an error of the intellect, whereas heresy requires a stubborn will, so he could err but could not be a heretic. In early 1327 he publicly declared himself ready to retract any error that was found, and he appealed to Pope John XXII at Avignon. A papal commission cut the list to 28 articles. Eckhart most likely died on 28 January 1328, before a verdict. On 27 March 1329 the pope's bull *In agro dominico* ("In the field of the Lord") condemned all 28 articles at graded levels of severity, with eleven treated as only suspect, and ordered it published in Cologne. The bull never declared him personally a heretic. It says he submitted before his death and revoked the articles insofar as they could carry a heretical sense; older biographers such as Evans held that he never recanted beyond his conditional offer, and how to read the bull's claim is disputed. The condemnation pushed his writings underground. His German sermons survived in many anonymous copies, which is why the authenticity of individual pieces is still checked one by one, and his Latin works were largely forgotten until the nineteenth century.

## Major theories and concepts

### Affirmation, negation and the God beyond being

Dionysius sets out two complementary ways of speaking about God. The **cataphatic** way (from Greek *kataphasis*, affirmation) praises God by the names Scripture gives: Good, Being, Life, Wisdom, One. The *Divine Names* explains how each fits: God is good not as one good thing among others, but as the cause of all goodness. The **apophatic** way (from *apophasis*, denial) then removes every name, because each was drawn from creatures and God is the cause of all creatures, not one of them. We should affirm all attributes of God "as Cause of all", and "more properly" deny them all, "as being above all" (*Mystical Theology* I.2). Even the negations fail in the end. God is "above every abstraction" too, so the last word is neither yes nor no but silence. Dionysius' compressed phrase for this is that God is *hyperousios*, "super-essential" or beyond being.

The example he uses is a sculptor, who does not add to the stone but reveals a statue by cutting away what hides it (*Mystical Theology* II). Negation is a way of removing, not of saying that God is nothing. The tension is plain. If every name is denied, why prefer "good" to "drunken"? Dionysius answers that denials begin with the remotest names: God is more evidently not stone or anger than not life or goodness (*Mystical Theology* III). The higher names stay "more cognate", or closer kin, even after they too are cancelled.

### Unknowing: the divine darkness

The *Mystical Theology* reads Moses' ascent of Sinai as a map of the soul's ascent. Moses is purified, sees lights and hears trumpets, and climbs, but at the summit he still does not see God, only "the place where He is". Then he "enters into the gloom of the Agnosia", unknowing, and is united to the wholly unknown "by knowing nothing, knowing above mind" (I.3). The goal is not a better concept but a union beyond concepts, which Dionysius calls ecstasy, a "standing outside" oneself. This is the root of the Western language of the "cloud of unknowing" and the "dark night".

### Hierarchy

Dionysius coined the word **hierarchy** (*hierarchia*, "sacred rule"). In the *Celestial Hierarchy* it means "a sacred order and science and operation" that makes its members as like God as each can be (III.1). Nine choirs of angels in three triads (seraphim, cherubim and thrones at the top) pass divine light down to the Church's own hierarchy of bishops, priests and deacons, and through the sacraments to the faithful. Each rank is purified, illuminated and perfected by the one above it. The whole is Proclus' chain of emanations, rebuilt as a Church: in place of a ladder of lesser gods, a ladder of light from a single creator.

### Eckhart: the ground of the soul

Eckhart turns negative theology inward. Beneath the soul's powers (memory, understanding, will) lies its **ground** (Middle High German *grunt*), the soul in its bare essence. No image enters it, and Eckhart sometimes calls it something uncreated in the soul, or a spark. In the ground, the soul and God are one, or as he puts it in a famous sermon, "The eye wherein I see God is the same eye wherein God sees me". He distinguishes too between **God**, the God of names and relations whom creatures know, and the **Godhead** (*gotheit*), the silent ground of God beyond all names. Hence the startling prayer of the sermon on the poor in spirit: "I pray God to rid me of God". The aim is not atheism but a breakthrough past every idea of God to God's own ground.

### Detachment and the birth of the Word in the soul

How does one reach the ground? By **detachment** (*abegescheidenheit*, "being cut off" from things) and **releasement** (*gelâzenheit*, "letting be"). These mean neither despising creatures nor running from the world. They mean letting go of the self-will that clings to things, to experiences and even to one's own holiness. In the treatise *On Detachment*, Eckhart ranks detachment above love itself, because a detached heart leaves room only for God.

The fruit of detachment is the **birth of the Word in the soul**. Christians celebrate at Christmas the eternal Son born in time. Eckhart's question is what that birth profits me if it does not also happen in me. He answers that when the soul is empty of images, the birth happens in the soul "exactly as it does in eternity", for "it is the same birth", and it happens in the soul's ground and essence (Sermon I). Tension marks every step. If the soul's ground is uncreated and one with God, is the line between creature and creator still standing? Eckhart says yes, and much of his trial turned on whether that answer holds.

## Key works

- Dionysius, *The Divine Names* — the longest treatise: what Scripture's names for God (Good, Light, Being, Life, Wisdom, One) mean, with chapter IV on evil as a lack rather than a thing.
- Dionysius, *The Mystical Theology* — five short chapters, the classic statement of the via negativa and the divine darkness.
- Dionysius, *The Celestial Hierarchy* and *The Ecclesiastical Hierarchy* — the nine angelic choirs and the sacramental order of the Church.
- Dionysius, ten *Letters* — short pieces, several on unknowing and the divine darkness.
- Eckhart, *Talks of Instruction* (c. 1294–98) — practical advice to young friars on obedience, self-will and detachment.
- Eckhart, *Parisian Questions* (1302–03) — scholastic disputations arguing that in God understanding is prior to being.
- Eckhart, *Opus tripartitum* ("Three-Part Work", begun in the 1300s; unfinished) — the planned Latin system, of which the prologues and biblical commentaries survive.
- Eckhart, *Book of Divine Consolation* and *On the Noble Man* (date disputed) — consolation in suffering through detachment.
- Eckhart, *On Detachment* — the treatise ranking detachment above all virtues; its authenticity, once doubted, is now generally accepted.
- Eckhart, German sermons — over a hundred judged authentic in the critical edition; Pfeiffer's 1857 edition, which C. de B. Evans translated in 1924, mixes genuine and doubtful pieces.

## Contributions

Dionysius fused Proclus' Neoplatonism with Christian doctrine more completely than anyone before him. He fixed the vocabulary of Christian mystical theology, including the words "hierarchy" and "mystical theology" in their lasting sense, and gave the paired method of affirmation and negation its classic form. Eckhart turned scholastic metaphysics into preaching in the vernacular, helped make German a language of philosophy, and offered the most radical account in the Latin tradition of the soul's union with God, together with an ethic of detachment that shaped later spirituality.

## Impact and legacy

Through Eriugena's translation, Dionysius became a leading authority of the Latin Middle Ages. Hugh of St Victor, Robert Grosseteste, Albert the Great and Aquinas commented on him, and Aquinas, who wrote a commentary on the *Divine Names*, cites him constantly. His threefold scheme of purification, illumination and perfection, later recast as purgation, illumination and union, became the standard map of the spiritual life; the fourteenth-century *Cloud of Unknowing* and the Spanish mystics owe him their darkness imagery. Dionysius also stands alongside [Maimonides](maimonides.md) as a source of medieval negative theology, and [Nicholas of Cusa](nicholas-of-cusa.md)'s learned ignorance draws on both him and Eckhart; Cusa defended Eckhart in his *Apology for Learned Ignorance* (1449) while urging that his works be kept from the uneducated. His student Henry Suso and the Strasbourg preacher John Tauler carried his teaching into popular devotion. Hegel and Franz von Baader hailed him as a father of German speculation; Schopenhauer compared him to Buddhist and Vedantic thought; Heidegger took up his word *Gelassenheit*; and twentieth-century comparative studies set him beside Shankara and Zen. The Nazi period's attempt to recruit him as a specifically "Germanic" mystic is now a cautionary tale of misuse.

## Criticisms and debates

- **Christian or Platonist?** Luther complained that Dionysius "platonizes more than he Christianizes". Critics find too little of Christ and too much of Proclus' hierarchy in him. Defenders reply that he deliberately reworked Proclus to serve Christian worship and the Incarnation, and so is an original thinker rather than a pagan in disguise.
- **Forgery or convention?** Was the persona a fraud or a recognized way of writing as a voice from the apostolic age? The answer shapes how far his authority should have counted, and scholars still disagree.
- **Was Eckhart orthodox?** One side reads the condemned articles as he meant them, as hyperbole in sermons and as statements true "insofar as" the soul is considered in God, and finds him a careful Dominican in the line of Albert and Aquinas. The other side holds that his claims about an uncreated element in the soul and about the Godhead beyond God really do strain the creature–creator distinction. Within the order, efforts to have the censure formally reconsidered continued into modern times.
- **Can negation say anything?** Critics since Aquinas ask whether a theology of pure denials says anything at all. Aquinas kept analogy, so that some names apply to God properly but in a higher way; defenders of Dionysius reply that this is exactly what "super-essential" affirmation was meant to capture.

## Misreadings to avoid

- **"Negative theology says God doesn't exist."** It denies that God is a being alongside others, because God is the cause of all beings. Its "not" means "more than", not "less than".
- **"Mysticism means vague feelings."** Both men wrote tightly argued metaphysics; Eckhart was a Paris master of theology.
- **"Detachment means hating the world."** Eckhart's model is Martha, who works in the world without clinging to it, as much as Mary.
- **"Eckhart was condemned as a heretic."** Propositions were condemned after his death; the bull never named him a heretic.
- **"Dionysius was Paul's convert."** That was the persona; the author wrote around 500 and drew on Proclus.

## Study questions

1. Dionysius says we should both affirm and deny every name of God. Is this a contradiction, or are the two claims about different things?
2. Why does Dionysius begin his negations with the "remotest" names (stone, anger) and not the highest (life, goodness)?
3. Compare Dionysius' ascent into "unknowing" with Maimonides' negative attributes. Is the goal knowledge, union, or something else?
4. What does Eckhart mean by praying "to rid me of God"? Does the God/Godhead distinction make this coherent?
5. Is Eckhart's detachment compatible with an active life in the world? Use his treatment of Martha and Mary.
6. Read two or three of the condemned propositions in context. Were they heresy, hyperbole, or something in between?

## What to read first

1. Dionysius, *Mystical Theology* (Parker's translation, Part I) — the whole is under ten pages.
2. Dionysius, *Divine Names* chapters I and XIII, then *Celestial Hierarchy* chapter III.
3. Eckhart, Sermon I in Evans (the eternal birth), then the sermon on the poor in spirit (LXXXVII).
4. Eckhart, *On Detachment* and the *Talks of Instruction*.
5. The SEP entries on Pseudo-Dionysius and on Meister Eckhart, for the dating evidence and the trial.
