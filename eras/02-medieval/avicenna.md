# Avicenna (Ibn Sīnā)

*Era: Medieval Philosophy (c. 400 – c. 1400) · [Era overview](README.md) · [Master index](../../index.md)*

## At a glance

- **Lifespan:** 980 – 1037
- **Place:** Born near Bukhara (modern Uzbekistan); worked across Persia, dying in Hamadan. Wrote in Arabic (and some Persian).
- **Tradition:** Islamic philosophy (*falsafa*) — the Arabic Aristotelian tradition inherited from al-Kindī and al-Fārābī, systematised with Neoplatonic elements.
- **Known for:** The essence/existence distinction and proof of the Necessary Existent; the Flying Man argument; the *Canon of Medicine*.

## Biography

Ibn Sīnā — Avicenna in Latin — was born in 980 in Afshana, near Bukhara, then a flourishing city of the Samanid Empire. His autobiography (preserved by his student al-Jūzjānī) is a famous document of precocity: he had memorised the Qur'an by ten, mastered law, mathematics, and Indian arithmetic as a boy, and turned to medicine at sixteen — which he later called easy — and metaphysics, which was not. He tells how he read Aristotle's *Metaphysics* forty times without grasping its purpose until a chance purchase of al-Fārābī's commentary unlocked it, and how, still a teenager, he cured the Samanid emir Nūḥ ibn Manṣūr of an illness the court physicians could not treat, winning access to the royal library, whose catalogued rooms of Greek and Arabic science he devoured before it burned.

The Samanid collapse scattered him into the itinerant life of a court intellectual: scholar, physician, and vizier to successive rulers in Khwarizm, Gurgan, Rayy, Hamadan, and Isfahan, repeatedly displaced by war and court intrigue, once imprisoned for months, and composing major works on the road, at night after court duties, sometimes (his student reports) dictating while travelling. In Isfahan, under ʿAlāʾ al-Dawla, he found his most stable patronage and completed the *Canon of Medicine*. He died in 1037 in Hamadan; al-Juzjani reports that he died of a colic after treating himself too aggressively. The scale of the output is staggering — some two hundred works are attributed to him — and his summa, the *Book of Healing*, is among the largest philosophical works by a single author before the modern era.

## Major theories and concepts

### Essence and existence; the Necessary Existent

Avicenna's signature distinction: for everything other than God, **what** a thing is (its essence or quiddity — e.g., horseness, triangularity) is neutral toward, and does not include, **that** it exists. You can completely grasp the essence of a phoenix, or of a triangle, without thereby knowing whether anything corresponding exists; whenever such a thing does exist, its existence has been *given* to it by a cause. Such a being is "possible in itself" — considered alone it need not exist — and "necessary through another" when its cause actualises it. But a chain of such beings, however long, merely postpones the question: the totality of merely possible beings cannot explain its own existence. There must therefore be a being whose existence is not received: the **Necessary Existent** (*wājib al-wujūd*), whose essence simply *is* to exist. From this Avicenna deduces, with austere rigour, the classical divine attributes: the Necessary Existent is one (two would have to differ, and the differentiator would make one of them composite and caused), simple (no essence/existence composition, no genus and difference — hence strictly indefinable), incorporeal, and the source of all else. Example of the engine at work: ask why this horse exists — its parents; why they — theirs; the regress objection ("why not infinitely?") fails, he argues, because an infinite series of possible beings is still a series of beings none of which explains existence as such. This framework gave the entire medieval world — Muslim, Jewish, and Latin Christian — its sharpest instrument; Aquinas's essence/*esse* composition and Third Way are unthinkable without it, and his terminology of necessity and possibility enters Latin as *necesse esse*.

### The Flying Man

Avicenna's most famous thought experiment: imagine a person created all at once, fully formed, floating in air or a void, limbs spread so they touch nothing, eyes covered, hearing no sound — sensing nothing at all, inside or out. Such a person, Avicenna claims, "would not doubt the existence of his self" even while affirming nothing of his body, his limbs, or any external thing. The conclusion: **self-awareness is primitive** — the soul's awareness of itself is not derived from sensation or from awareness of the body, and the soul (the "self" affirmed here) is not identical with the body or any bodily organ. He deploys the experiment in his psychology (*Book of Salvation*; the *Healing*'s *De Anima*) to establish the soul's distinctness and immateriality. The comparison with Descartes' *cogito* six centuries later is unavoidable and instructive, with caution: Descartes argues from doubt to a thinking substance as an epistemic foundation; Avicenna's point is phenomenological and psychological — the self is self-present prior to all perception — and his argument's target is the nature of the soul, not scepticism. Internal debate persists: does the Flying Man show the soul is a distinct substance, or only that self-awareness doesn't *require* concurrent body-awareness?

### Emanation and the Agent Intellect

From the Necessary Existent, the world proceeds eternally by **emanation**: because the One knows itself, a first Intellect overflows from it; that Intellect's multiple self-relations (thinking its source, thinking itself as caused, as possible) generate a further Intellect, a celestial soul, and a sphere — cascading down through the heavens to the tenth intellect, the **Agent Intellect**, which administers the sublunary world: it actualises human potential intellects (giving us abstract thought) and, with celestial motion, gives forms to suitably prepared matter. Hence Avicenna's world is eternal — creation is eternal dependence, not a temporal start — a thesis theologians attacked for compromising divine freedom and the Qur'anic creation, and which al-Ghazālī targeted in the *Incoherence of the Philosophers*. For human cognition the Agent Intellect doctrine mattered enormously: all humans think by "conjoining" with the same separate intellect, raising the question Averroes would answer radically and Aquinas would fight to re-individualise.

### Modal metaphysics and logic

Beneath these doctrines sits Avicenna's systematic modal framework — necessary, possible, impossible — worked into every branch of philosophy, and a logic of his own: he reformed Aristotelian syllogistic with a sophisticated theory of temporal and modal propositions, treated in the huge *Logic* of the *Healing*, long overshadowed in the West by his metaphysics but increasingly studied. He also fixed the "common nature" analysis of essences — horseness is just horseness, in itself neither one nor many, neither in souls nor in things — which, transmitted through Latin translation, became Scotus's doctrine of the common nature.

## Key works

- **The Book of Healing** (*Kitāb al-Shifāʾ*, c. 1020–27) — vast encyclopedia in four parts: logic, natural philosophy, mathematics, metaphysics. Despite the title it is not a medical book; the "healing" is of the soul from ignorance. Its *Metaphysics* (*Ilāhiyyāt*) contains the essence/existence doctrine and Necessary Existent proof.
- **The Book of Salvation** (*Kitāb al-Najāt*, c. 1020s) — a shorter digest, largely drawn from the *Healing*; the Flying Man appears in its psychology section and is accessible here.
- **The Canon of Medicine** (*al-Qānūn fī al-Ṭibb*, c. 1025) — five-book medical encyclopedia; translated into Latin, it remained a European university textbook into the seventeenth century.
- **Remarks and Admonitions** (*al-Ishārāt wa-l-Tanbīhāt*, c. 1030–34) — his dense late summa, the focus of centuries of commentary (notably by Naṣīr al-Dīn al-Ṭūsī).

## Contributions

Avicenna was no transmitter but an original system-builder. He gave philosophy the essence/existence distinction in its precise form, the first rigorously modal proof of God, a canonical thought experiment about self-awareness, and a complete re-foundation of Aristotelian science (logic, physics, psychology, metaphysics) that both the Islamic East and the Latin West studied for centuries. The theses Latin scholastics fought over — eternity, emanation, the Agent Intellect — were his.

## Impact and legacy

In the Islamic world, al-Ghazālī's attack was aimed largely at Avicenna's system; Averroes wrote to correct it; and post-Avicennan Islamic philosophy (Suhrawardī, Mullā Ṣadrā) is a centuries-long negotiation with him — modern scholarship stresses that philosophy in Islam did not "end" with Ghazālī but transformed around Avicenna. In Latin Europe, the twelfth-century Toledo translations made the *Healing*'s psychology and metaphysics foundational: Aquinas argues with him on nearly every page, Scotus takes over the common nature, and Latin psychology wholesale absorbed his internal senses and intellect theory. The *Canon* shaped European medicine for half a millennium.

## Criticisms and debates

Al-Ghazālī charged the philosophers with unbelief on three points: the world's eternity, God's knowing only universals (on the emanation scheme, does God know particulars?), and denial of bodily resurrection. Avicenna's defenders (and Ṭūsī) argue his position on God's knowledge is subtler — God knows particulars "in a universal way" — which critics reply is precisely the problem. Modern debates: the exact structure of the Necessary Existent proof (is it cosmological, or ontological in a modal key?), the Flying Man's intended conclusion, and how much of his system is Neoplatonic versus Aristotelian in inspiration.

## Misreadings to avoid

- That Avicenna merely "preserved" Greek thought for Europe: he rebuilt it; the agenda of Latin scholasticism is substantially his list of problems.
- That the *Book of Healing* is a medical work: the title is a metaphor; only the *Canon* is medicine.
- That the Flying Man *is* the cogito: related, but different argument, setting, and purpose — the resemblance is a starting point for analysis, not an identity.
- That he wrote as a secular rationalist: he wrote in Arabic within the Islamic intellectual world, engaging Qur'anic theology throughout, however independently.
