## At a glance

- **Dates:** 1364 – c. 1430
- **Place:** Born in Venice; raised at the French royal court in Paris; spent her last years, probably, at the Dominican convent of Poissy
- **School/tradition:** Late-medieval moral and political writing in French; early humanism; the defense of women
- **Known for:** *The Book of the City of Ladies* (1405), an allegory in which Reason, Rectitude and Justice help her refute the learned tradition of misogyny; her part in the debate over the *Romance of the Rose*; mirrors for princes and princesses; the only poem celebrating Joan of Arc written in Joan's lifetime

**Responds to:** [Aristotle](../01-ancient/aristotle.md), [Augustine](augustine-of-hippo.md), [Boethius](boethius.md), [Aquinas](thomas-aquinas.md) · **Answered by:** [Elisabeth, Cavendish, Conway & Astell](../03-early-modern/early-modern-women-philosophers.md), [Mary Wollstonecraft](../03-early-modern/mary-wollstonecraft.md), [Simone de Beauvoir](../05-20th-century/simone-de-beauvoir.md)

## Biography

Christine was born in Venice in 1364 as Cristina da Pizzano. Her father, Tommaso da Pizzano, came from a family named after the village of Pizzano near Bologna and worked as a physician and astrologer. In 1368 he accepted a post as astrologer to Charles V of France, and the family moved to Paris when Christine was four. Charles V was a famous book collector, and the court she grew up in valued learning. She later wrote that her father encouraged her studies while her mother wanted her kept to spinning, a story we know only from her own telling.

At fifteen, in 1379, she married Etienne du Castel, a notary and royal secretary. The marriage was by her own account a happy one, and they had three children. Then the world fell in. Charles V died in 1380, and her father lost his standing and died about a year before her husband. In 1389 her husband died of plague, leaving her, at about twenty-five, with her mother and her children to support. She spent years in lawsuits trying to recover money owed to his estate.

She turned to writing to earn a living, which is what makes her unusual. By the early 1390s she was writing love ballads that found patrons among the royal family: Queen Isabeau of Bavaria, Louis of Orléans, the dukes of Berry and Burgundy. Over the next two decades she produced some forty works in verse and prose and supervised the copying and illustration of her own manuscripts. Philip the Bold of Burgundy commissioned her life of Charles V (1404). In 1414 she presented Queen Isabeau with a lavish collection of her works, now British Library Harley MS 4431, the source of the portrait above. She is often called the first professional woman writer in Europe; the claim is hard to test, but she was certainly the first in France known to live by her pen.

Her life coincided with a political collapse. From 1392 Charles VI suffered recurring bouts of madness. The rivalry between Orléans and Burgundy ended in the murder of Louis of Orléans in 1407 and in civil war, and Henry V's English army won at Agincourt in 1415. Much of her later writing is political advice to a kingdom tearing itself apart. After the Burgundians seized Paris in 1418 she seems to have withdrawn to the convent at Poissy, where her daughter was a nun, and fell silent. In 1429 she broke that silence with a poem on Joan of Arc. She probably died around 1430; no record of her death survives.

**What the sources are.** Most of what we know about her life comes from her own autobiographical passages, especially *L'Avision Cristine* (1405) and *Le Livre de la mutacion de Fortune* (1403). The *Avision* ends, on the model of [Boethius](boethius.md)'s *Consolation*, with Lady Philosophy consoling her for her misfortunes. Archival records confirm only scattered facts, such as her widowhood in a 1389 lawsuit and payments from the Burgundian court. Her self-portraits are literary: in the *Mutacion* she says Fortune turned her into a man so she could steer her household's ship. Read that as allegory about taking on a man's role, not as a biographical event.

## Major theories and concepts

### The City of Ladies: a counter-library

*The Book of the City of Ladies* opens in Christine's study. She picks up the *Lamentations* of Matheolus, a thirteenth-century satire against women and marriage, and is led to a wider question: why have so many learned men, philosophers, poets and orators, agreed that women are prone to every vice? She checks the claim against her own experience and against the women she knows, and finds it false. Yet she nearly concludes that so many authorities must outweigh her experience, and she despairs at having been born a woman.

Three crowned ladies then appear: **Reason**, **Rectitude** (in Middle French *Droiture*, uprightness) and **Justice**, daughters of God. They tell her to build a city, a fortified place where women of good reputation can live safe from slander. Each lady supplies one part, matching the book's three parts. Reason clears the ground and lays the walls (Part I, women rulers, warriors and inventors), Rectitude builds the houses (Part II, prophets, faithful wives, virtuous daughters), and Justice completes the towers and brings in the Virgin Mary and the female saints (Part III). The bricks are stories of individual women, many taken from Boccaccio's *On Famous Women*, rewritten to answer charges against women. The title deliberately echoes [Augustine](augustine-of-hippo.md)'s *City of God*.

### How she argues against the authorities

Christine's method is worth noticing because it is philosophical, not just a list of examples. Reason tells her first that the authorities disagree with one another. Aristotle refutes Plato in the *Metaphysics*, Augustine and the Church Fathers correct earlier writers, and philosophers are not articles of faith that cannot err. Poets often write in fables or by antiphrasis (saying the opposite of what they mean). So the weight of authority is not unanimous testimony, and experience is a fair witness against it.

She then supplies reasons why men slander women: some out of genuine concern to keep men from folly, some from their own vices, some from envy of women's abilities, some because old age has left them bitter, and some only because they found it in books. Reason also attacks a treatise on women's bodies, *On the Secrets of Women*, that some attributed to [Aristotle](../01-ancient/aristotle.md); she says the philosopher did not write it. On the Aristotelian idea that a woman is a defective male, she replies that God, who fashioned both sexes, made no mistake, and that nature serves God and does not overrule him.

### Education, not nature

Reason argues that if girls were schooled as boys are, they would learn as well. Women seem to know less because they stay at home and lack the varied experience that teaches people, much as country people seem simple for lack of instruction, though nature made them as capable as anyone. This is an argument from circumstance, and it anticipates [Astell](../03-early-modern/early-modern-women-philosophers.md) and [Wollstonecraft](../03-early-modern/mary-wollstonecraft.md). Notice its limits: Reason also says women need not take up men's public offices, because each sex has its own task.

### The querelle de la Rose

The *Romance of the Rose*, begun by Guillaume de Lorris and continued around 1275 by Jean de Meun, was the most popular French poem of the age. Jean's part includes long speeches attacking women and marriage. In 1401 the royal secretary Jean de Montreuil praised the poem, and a debate in letters followed, lasting until about 1403. On one side were Montreuil and the brothers Gontier and Pierre Col; on the other, Christine and Jean Gerson, chancellor of the University of Paris, who wrote his own treatise against the poem. Christine objected that the work slandered women and taught vice under the name of learning. In 1402 she gathered the letters into a dossier and sent it to Queen Isabeau, making a private quarrel public. It is often called the first literary debate in French.

### The querelle des femmes

Historians use **querelle des femmes** ("the debate about women") for the centuries-long argument, from about 1400 to the French Revolution, over women's nature, abilities and proper place. Joan Kelly's essay "Early Feminist Theory and the Querelle des Femmes, 1400–1789" (*Signs*, 1982) made Christine its starting point, and that framing is now standard. Others note that defenses and attacks on women were older, so "starting" means giving the debate its shape and its first sustained woman's voice.

### The Book of the Three Virtues

*The Book of the Three Virtues* (1405), also called *The Treasure of the City of Ladies*, is the sequel. The same three ladies return to teach the women who will live in the city how to live. It is a practical conduct book, addressed in turn to princesses, women of the court, townswomen, and finally poor and working women. It was dedicated to Margaret of Burgundy, wife of the dauphin. Its advice is prudent rather than rebellious: a princess should keep the peace, manage the household finances and protect her reputation. Read alongside the *City*, it shows Christine's aim was to defend women's virtue and competence within the existing social order.

### Political writing: the body politic

*The Book of the Body Politic* (c. 1407), written for the dauphin Louis of Guyenne, uses an old image, found in John of Salisbury, of the kingdom as a body: the prince is the head, the knights and nobles are the arms and hands, and the common people are the belly, legs and feet. Each part must do its work for the whole to be healthy, and the prince's virtues, especially justice and wisdom, come first. The *Book of Deeds of Arms and of Chivalry* (1410) applies just-war thinking to warfare, and the *Book of Peace* (1413) pleads for reconciliation in the civil war. Following [Aquinas](thomas-aquinas.md), she treats wisdom as the highest intellectual virtue.

### The Ditié de Jehanne d'Arc

The *Ditié de Jehanne d'Arc* (dated July 1429, just after Charles VII's coronation at Reims) is a poem of 61 stanzas celebrating Joan of Arc. It is usually described as the only major literary work about Joan written during her life. Christine presents Joan as fulfilling prophecies attributed to Merlin, the Sibyl and Bede, and as proof of what God could do through a woman, the argument of the *City* turned into current events. She does not seem to have lived to see Joan captured and burned in 1431.

## Key works

- *Epistle to the God of Love* (1399) — Cupid receives women's complaints about men's lies; her first poem in their defense.
- *Letter of Othea to Hector* (c. 1400) — A mirror for princes in verse and prose.
- *The Debate of the Romance of the Rose* (letters, 1401–1403) — Her side of the querelle.
- *The Book of the Mutation of Fortune* (1403) — Autobiography and universal history.
- *The Book of the Deeds and Good Conduct of the Wise King Charles V* (1404) — Commissioned biography of the king.
- *The Book of the City of Ladies* (1405) — The allegorical defense of women.
- *The Book of the Three Virtues* (1405) — Its sequel, practical advice for women of every rank.
- *L'Avision Cristine* (1405) — A dream vision with her most personal autobiography.
- *The Book of the Body Politic* (c. 1407) — Political theory for the dauphin.
- *Book of Deeds of Arms and of Chivalry* (1410) and *Book of Peace* (1413) — War, justice and civil peace.
- *Ditié de Jehanne d'Arc* (1429) — Her last known work.

## In their own words

All quotations are from the first English translation, Brian Anslay's *Boke of the Cyte of Ladyes* (1521; public domain), as transcribed by the Text Creation Partnership. The spelling is early Tudor; a plain modern rendering follows each.

> "so many dyuers men clerkes and others haue ben and ben enclyned to say by mouthe / & in theyr treatyse and wrytynges so many slaundres and blames of women"

— *City of Ladies*, Part I, ch. 1. The question that starts the book: why so many learned men have spoken and written so much slander against women.

> "it semeth that trowest that al the wordes of Phylosophres ben artycles of the fayth of Ihesu cryst / and that they maye not arre"

— Part I, ch. 2. Reason chides Christine for treating philosophers as if they were articles of faith that cannot err.

> "For knowe wel yt all this euyll saynges generally of womē hurteth ye sayers & not ye womē."

— Part I, ch. 2. Reason: blanket slanders against women harm those who speak them, not women.

> "I am Iustyce the synguler doughter of god"

— Part I, ch. 6. The third lady introduces herself.

> "go we in to the felde of scryptures"

— Part I, ch. 8. Reason leads Christine into "the field of writings" to dig the city's foundations: the city is built out of texts.

> "yf it were the custome to put the lytel maydens to the scole ... that they sholde lerne as parfytely / and they sholde be as wel entred in to the subtyltes of al the artes and scyences as they be"

— Part I, ch. 27. If it were the custom to send little girls to school, they would learn as well as boys and master the arts and sciences as fully.

## Contributions

Christine is the first woman in the Latin West known to have answered the learned tradition of misogyny at length, in writing, by name, and with an argument about how to weigh authority against experience. She turned a literary quarrel into a public one, and gave the long querelle des femmes its first framework. She also wrote serious political theory in French, a vernacular, for princes in a failing state.

## Impact and legacy

Her books were copied and printed after her death. The *Three Virtues* was used by royal women in France and Portugal; Anne of France drew on it for her advice to her daughter (1504). The *City* was translated into Dutch in the fifteenth century and into English by Anslay in 1521, and Caxton printed an English *Deeds of Arms* in 1489. Interest then faded until nineteenth- and twentieth-century scholars revived her. [Simone de Beauvoir](../05-20th-century/simone-de-beauvoir.md) wrote in *The Second Sex* (1949) that the *Epistle to the God of Love* was the first time a woman took up her pen in defense of her sex. Since the 1980s, scholars such as Charity Cannon Willard, Earl Jeffrey Richards, Rosalind Brown-Grant and Kate Langdon Forhan have edited and translated her and studied her as a political thinker. Compare [Hildegard of Bingen](hildegard-of-bingen.md), who two centuries earlier wrote with the authority of visions from inside the cloister; Christine wrote as a lay widow, with the authority of reading and experience.

## Criticisms and debates

- **"The first feminist"?** The label is common and disputed. "Feminism" is a nineteenth-century word and names a political movement for equal rights, which Christine did not seek. She accepted a hierarchy of estates, separate roles for the sexes, and wives' obedience, and the *Three Virtues* tells unhappy wives to be patient. Defenders answer that she named misogyny as a structured tradition, blamed lack of education rather than nature, and argued on women's behalf as a group. Scholars such as Sheila Delany have stressed her conservatism; others, following Kelly, see "early feminist theory" in her. The fair summary is that she is a defender of women whose terms are medieval, and calling her a feminist is an anachronism some find useful and others misleading.
- **Was she a philosopher?** She wrote no treatise in the schools' manner and is usually shelved under literature. Her case for inclusion rests on her argument about authority and experience, her moral psychology of slander, and her political theory.
- **Sources and originality.** Much of the *City* rewrites Boccaccio and other compilations, and much of her political writing draws on Aristotle, Vegetius and John of Salisbury. Scholars debate how far her selection and reframing make the work original.
- **The querelle de la Rose.** Was Jean de Meun's misogyny his own, or the voice of his characters, as Pierre Col argued? The debate over whether an author answers for his characters remains open.

## Misreadings to avoid

- **"She was French."** Born in Venice to Italian parents, she wrote in French and identified fiercely with France.
- **"The City of Ladies argues that women are superior."** It argues that women are capable of every virtue and that the slanders are false, and it usually defends women's virtue within traditional roles.
- **"She turned into a man."** The *Mutacion de Fortune* uses that image as allegory for taking on a man's work after widowhood.
- **"She started the debate about women."** Attacks and defenses existed before her; she gave the later querelle its shape and first major woman's voice.

## What to read first

1. *The Book of the City of Ladies*, Part I, chs. 1–10 (the opening, the three ladies, Reason's reply to the authorities), in Earl Jeffrey Richards's or Rosalind Brown-Grant's translation.
2. *City of Ladies*, Part I, chs. 27–38 (women's learning and invention).
3. *The Treasure of the City of Ladies* (*Three Virtues*), Part I, on the princess's duties, in Sarah Lawson's translation.
4. *The Book of the Body Politic*, Part I, in Kate Langdon Forhan's translation.
5. The *Ditié de Jehanne d'Arc*, the whole poem.

## Study questions

1. Christine almost concludes that so many learned men must be right and her own experience wrong. What does Reason say that changes her mind, and is it a good argument about authority?
2. Why does Christine build a city instead of writing a treatise? What does the allegory let her do?
3. How does Reason explain why women seem to know less than men? Does that argument commit her to equal public roles?
4. Compare the *City of Ladies* with the *Three Virtues*. Is the advice in the second a retreat from the first, or its practical consequence?
5. Is "the first feminist" a fair description of Christine? What would you need to know about her aims to decide?
