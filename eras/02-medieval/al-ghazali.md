# Al-Ghazali (Abu Hamid al-Ghazali)

*Era: Medieval Philosophy (c. 400 – c. 1400) · [Era overview](README.md) · [Master index](../../index.md)*

## At a glance

- **Dates:** c. 1058 – 1111 (medieval historians give 1058 or 1059; Frank Griffel argues for 1055/56 from remarks in his letters and autobiography)
- **Place:** Born and died in Tus, Khorasan (north-east Iran); taught at Nishapur and Baghdad; travelled to Damascus, Jerusalem and Mecca. Wrote in Arabic and Persian.
- **School/tradition:** Sunni Islam; Ashʿarite theology (*kalām*), Shāfiʿī law, and Sufism
- **Known for:** *The Incoherence of the Philosophers*, which argued that the philosophers had not proved their key claims and condemned three of them as unbelief; the denial that causes necessitate their effects (the cotton and the fire); and *The Deliverance from Error*, his account of a skeptical crisis cured not by argument but by a "light" from God

**Responds to:** [Avicenna](avicenna.md) · **Answered by:** [Averroes](averroes.md), [Maimonides](maimonides.md), [Thomas Aquinas](thomas-aquinas.md)

## Biography

Abu Hamid Muhammad ibn Muhammad al-Ghazali was born at Tabaran, one of the two towns of Tus, near modern Mashhad in north-east Iran. He and his brother Ahmad, who became a famous Sufi preacher, received their early schooling in Tus. He then studied at the Nizamiyya college in Nishapur with al-Juwayni (1028–1085), the leading theologian of the Ashʿarite school. The **Ashʿarites** were a school of *kalām*, rational theology in defence of Islamic doctrine, who held that God directly creates everything that happens.

Through al-Juwayni he came into contact with the court of the Seljuq sultan Malikshah and his vizier Nizam al-Mulk. In 1091 Nizam al-Mulk appointed him to the Nizamiyya college in Baghdad, the most prestigious teaching post in the Sunni world. There, he says, he studied the works of the philosophers on his own for about two years and spent a further year reflecting on them. Griffel thinks this account is apologetic, and that he had met philosophy earlier under al-Juwayni. Around 1095 he published a set of works on the philosophers, including the *Incoherence*.

In 1095, at the height of his fame, he left Baghdad. His autobiography describes a crisis: he had come to think that his teaching served reputation and worldly gain, and for a time he could not speak to lecture. He went to Damascus and Jerusalem, and at Abraham's tomb in Hebron vowed never again to serve rulers or teach at state-funded schools. After the pilgrimage to Mecca in 1096 he returned to Tus, where he founded a small school and a Sufi lodge. In these years he wrote his largest work, *The Revival of the Religious Sciences*.

In 1106 he broke his vow and returned to state teaching at the Nizamiyya in Nishapur, justifying this by the religious confusion of the times. His teaching there drew charges that his *Revival* was tainted by philosophy, and the governor Sanjar acquitted him. He died in Tus in December 1111. Many of his works survive. One of them, the *Doctrines of the Philosophers*, a summary of Avicenna's system, became the only one of his books translated into Latin in the Middle Ages. It circulated without his note that it was only a report, so Latin readers knew "Algazel" as a follower of Avicenna, the very philosopher he had attacked.

## Major theories and concepts

### The Incoherence of the Philosophers: twenty discussions

The *Tahāfut al-falāsifa* (Incoherence of the Philosophers) targets the **falāsifa**, the Arabic-writing philosophers who carried on Greek philosophy, above all al-Farabi and Avicenna. They claimed their teachings rested on **demonstration** (*burhān*): proof from certain premises, the highest standard in Aristotle's logic. Al-Ghazali accepts the standard and turns it against them. In twenty "discussions" he argues that their arguments for twenty theses fall short of it. Sixteen concern metaphysics and four natural science. Often he does not claim the thesis is false, only that it has not been proved. His charge is that the philosophers practise *taqlīd*, uncritical acceptance of authority, the very fault they accused theologians of.

The longest discussion is the first, on the **eternity of the world**. The philosophers argued that an eternal, unchanging God whose will is complete cannot "wait" and then create. Al-Ghazali replies that this draws an analogy between God's will and human resolve that they have not justified. An eternal will could fix a beginning for the world, and he adds arguments, going back to John Philoponus, that an infinite series of past events is impossible.

### The three condemned theses

At the end of the book he sorts the twenty theses. Seventeen are errors and "innovations" (*bidʿa*), but a Muslim might hold them. Three, all from Avicenna, contradict revelation and amount to **unbelief** (*kufr*):

1. **The world has no beginning in time.** Against the Qur'an's teaching that God created it.
2. **God knows particulars only "in a universal way."** Avicenna held that God's knowledge cannot change, so God cannot know a particular event as it happens, such as an eclipse now beginning. Al-Ghazali argues that this would mean God does not know whether a person named Zayd obeys or disobeys Him, and that one unchanging knowledge can cover an event before, during and after it.
3. **There is no bodily resurrection.** The philosophers held that only the soul survives death. In the twentieth discussion al-Ghazali argues that God could return the soul to a body, from the old matter or new, and that the person would be the same because the soul is the same.

As a jurist he added that anyone who teaches these three openly is an apostate. Defining the limits of tolerated belief, here and in his *Decisive Criterion* (*Fayṣal al-tafriqa*), was part of his project.

### Causation and the cotton and the fire (17th discussion)

The seventeenth discussion is the most famous. Its purpose is to defend the possibility of the miracles reported in the Qur'an. If fire must burn whatever it touches, then the story of Abraham thrown into the fire unharmed is impossible. So al-Ghazali denies that the link between a cause and its effect is **necessary**. Take any two different events, such as contact with fire and burning, drinking and the quenching of thirst, or decapitation and death. Affirming one never logically entails affirming the other. What links them is God's prior decree, and God creates them "side by side."

His first argument is about observation. When cotton touches a flame, we see the burning happen *with* the contact; we never see it happen *through* the contact. On the "first position" he opposes, fire alone is the agent. He answers that the agent of the burning is God, acting directly or through angels, and that fire, being lifeless, "has no action." Our confidence that fire will burn comes from **habit**: the regular repetition of the pairing fixes the belief in our minds.

The obvious objection, which he raises himself, is skepticism. If nothing is necessary, a book left at home might turn into a boy, or ashes into musk. His reply is that God has chosen to create a regular, habitual course of nature, so our ordinary expectations remain reliable. Scholars dispute what positive theory he held. The standard reading calls him an **occasionalist**: God is the only true cause, and created things are merely the occasions on which God acts. Griffel and others point out that in the same discussion he offers a second approach, in which created things are real but secondary causes, and leaves both open. What he rules out is that the world could not have been otherwise.

### The Deliverance from Error: doubt and its cure

*Al-Munqidh min al-ḍalāl* (The Deliverance from Error), written late in life, after his return to teaching in 1106, tells the story of his search for certainty. He noticed that children of Christians, Jews and Muslims each grow up holding their parents' religion, and asked how knowledge differs from such inherited belief. His standard is **certain knowledge**, so clear that no possibility of error remains. Sense perception fails it: sight judges a shadow to be still, then finds it has moved. Reason's first truths seem to pass. Then comes the dream doubt. In dreams we trust what we see and on waking find it groundless, so there might be a state beyond waking in which our rational beliefs look like dreams.

He could not argue his way out, because any proof would rest on the very first truths in question. The skepticism lasted, he says, about two months. The cure came when God cast a light into his heart, and he again trusted reason's first truths. Commentators read this as a form of direct experience, and stress that unlike Descartes he does not defeat doubt by his own reasoning.

### Sufism: knowing versus tasting

Having recovered trust in reason, he surveyed four groups of seekers: theologians, philosophers, the Ismaʿili "authoritative teaching" movement, and the Sufis. He judged the philosophers' mathematics and logic religiously neutral and useful, and their metaphysics full of errors. Only the Sufis, he concluded, reached what cannot be learned from books. Their distinctive knowledge is ***dhawq***, literally "taste": direct experience of the states one has only read about, as being drunk differs from knowing the definition of drunkenness. The *Revival* builds an ethics on this, in which character is trained by discipline and habit, much as in Aristotle, towards purity of heart and the afterlife.

## Key works

- *Doctrines of the Philosophers* (*Maqāṣid al-falāsifa*, c. 1095) — A clear summary of Avicenna's logic, metaphysics and physics; in Latin it made "Algazel" an authority on Arabic philosophy.
- *The Incoherence of the Philosophers* (*Tahāfut al-falāsifa*, c. 1095) — Twenty discussions testing the philosophers' claims to demonstration; contains the eternity, God's-knowledge, resurrection and causation arguments. English: Marmura (BYU, 1997; 2nd ed. 2000).
- *Moderation in Belief* (*al-Iqtiṣād fī l-iʿtiqād*, c. 1095) — His positive Ashʿarite theology.
- *The Revival of the Religious Sciences* (*Iḥyāʾ ʿulūm al-dīn*, c. 1096–1100s) — Forty books on worship, social life, vices and virtues; his most widely read work.
- *The Niche of Lights* (*Mishkāt al-anwār*) — A late mystical commentary on the Qur'an's "Light Verse."
- *The Deliverance from Error* (*al-Munqidh min al-ḍalāl*, after 1106) — Intellectual autobiography: doubt, the survey of seekers, and the turn to Sufism. English: McCarthy (1980) or Watt (1953).

## Contributions

Al-Ghazali produced the first full internal critique of Islamic Aristotelianism: he accepted its logic and showed where its proofs fell short of its own standard. He separated the parts of philosophy a believer could adopt (logic, mathematics, much of physics and ethics) from the parts he rejected, which helped bring Aristotelian logic into Sunni theology and law. He gave the sharpest medieval argument that causal connections are not logically necessary, and wrote one of the first first-person accounts of radical doubt. He also made Sufism respectable within mainstream Sunni learning.

## Impact and legacy

In the Islamic world he became known as the "Proof of Islam" (*Ḥujjat al-Islām*), and the *Revival* remains widely read. [Averroes](averroes.md) answered the *Incoherence* point by point in *The Incoherence of the Incoherence* (c. 1180). He argued that denying real natures and necessary causes would destroy knowledge itself, and defended the world's eternity as Aristotle had argued it. He also argued, in his *Decisive Treatise*, that philosophy is commanded by Islamic law and that al-Ghazali's condemnation of al-Farabi and Avicenna was not conclusive. [Avicenna's](avicenna.md) system did not disappear: later thinkers such as Fakhr al-Din al-Razi absorbed it into theology, and a new genre of philosophical handbooks grew from the *Doctrines*.

In Jewish thought, [Maimonides](maimonides.md) in the *Guide of the Perplexed* (Part I, chs. 71–76) set out and criticised the *kalām* theologians' view that God directly creates every event. [Thomas Aquinas](thomas-aquinas.md) knew "Algazel" mainly as Avicenna's summariser, and in the *Summa contra Gentiles* (III, ch. 69) rejected the view that created things have no actions of their own. The causal argument has a famous later parallel in [David Hume](../03-early-modern/david-hume.md), who also held that we never observe necessary connection and that our expectation comes from custom. The *Deliverance* has often been compared with [Descartes's](../03-early-modern/rene-descartes.md) method of doubt, though no translation existed in Descartes's time.

## Criticisms and debates

- **Did he kill philosophy in Islam?** A popular story, repeated by the physicist Steven Weinberg, says the *Incoherence* turned Islam against science. Montgomery Watt replied that the eastern Aristotelian tradition was already declining after Avicenna's death in 1037. Griffel and others go further and argue that philosophy did not decline but was absorbed into theology.
- **Occasionalist or not?** Marmura read the seventeenth discussion as committed to occasionalism; Lenn Goodman asked whether al-Ghazali denied causality at all; Griffel argues that he deliberately left occasionalism and secondary causation both open.
- **The Hume comparison.** Both deny that we perceive necessary connection and both appeal to habit. But al-Ghazali's argument is theological and logical, and God's decree grounds the regularity; Hume's is psychological and offers no ground beyond custom. Scholars dispute how close the parallel is.
- **Averroes' reply.** Averroes charged that if things lack natures that make them act as they do, nothing can be known through its causes, and he asked whether the "habit" belongs to God or to us. Defenders reply that al-Ghazali never denied a regular order, only its necessity.
- **The autobiography as evidence.** The *Deliverance* is a carefully shaped, partly apologetic work, and historians do not take its account of his studies or his crisis at face value.

## Misreadings to avoid

- **"He was against philosophy."** The title attacks the philosophers, not philosophy. He used Aristotle's logic and adopted much of philosophical ethics and psychology.
- **"He denied that anything causes anything."** He denied that causes *necessitate* their effects and kept a regular, divinely chosen order; whether creatures are real secondary causes is left open.
- **"All twenty theses are heresy."** Only three are condemned as unbelief; the other seventeen are errors.
- **"He was a Cartesian skeptic."** His doubt is cured by God's light and direct experience, not by a proof such as "I think, therefore I am."
- **"Algazel the Avicennan."** The medieval Latin picture came from a summary translated without his disclaimer.

## What to read first

1. *The Deliverance from Error* (McCarthy or Watt translation) — short and personal, the best way in.
2. *The Incoherence of the Philosophers*, Marmura's translation: the introductions and the seventeenth discussion, on causation.
3. *Incoherence*, the first discussion (eternity of the world) and the closing verdict on the three theses.
4. Averroes, *The Incoherence of the Incoherence*, his reply to the seventeenth discussion, read side by side.
5. Frank Griffel's SEP entry "al-Ghazali" for the debates.

## Study questions

1. Al-Ghazali accepts the philosophers' standard of demonstration and argues that they fail it. Why is this a stronger strategy than rejecting their standard altogether?
2. Reconstruct the cotton and fire argument. What exactly does he deny: that fire burns cotton, or that it must?
3. If God could turn ashes into musk, can we know anything about tomorrow? Assess al-Ghazali's reply and Averroes' objection.
4. Compare his account of habit with Hume's custom. What does each say grounds the regularity of nature?
5. Why can no argument cure the dream doubt in the *Deliverance*? What does the "light" do that a proof could not?
6. Why did he count only three of the twenty theses as unbelief? What makes a philosophical error religiously dangerous on his view?
