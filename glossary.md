# Glossary

Plain-language definitions for the terms used across the entries. Each philosopher entry also explains its key terms in context; this glossary is the quick-reference version.

- **a priori / a posteriori:** knowable independently of experience (e.g. "all bachelors are unmarried") / only through experience ("it rained yesterday"). Kant adds the crucial third category: *synthetic a priori*.
- **Abjection:** Kristeva's term for the violent rejection of whatever threatens the boundary between self and not-self — vomit, filth, an open wound, a corpse — which provokes a revulsion stronger than dislike. The abject is neither a subject nor an object but what one must expel in order to become an "I" at all; the first thing so expelled, she argues, is the infant's fusion with the mother's body. See [Irigaray, Kristeva and Butler](eras/05-20th-century/feminist-philosophy-after-beauvoir.md).
- **Alienation:** Marx's term for the worker's estrangement under capitalism — from the product of their labor, from the activity of working, from their own human capacities, and from other people. See [Karl Marx](eras/04-19th-century/karl-marx.md).
- **Actual occasion:** Whitehead's ultimate unit of reality: a momentary drop of experience that "prehends" (feels, takes account of) its whole past world and unifies it into one satisfaction — after which it perishes and becomes data for its successors. Persons, electrons, and empty regions alike are societies of such occasions. See [Alfred North Whitehead](eras/05-20th-century/alfred-north-whitehead.md).
- **Analytic / synthetic (judgments):** true by the meanings of the terms alone / true by how the world is. Quine famously attacks the distinction itself, arguing "analytic" cannot be defined without circularity.
- **Apophatic (negative) theology:** the way of speaking about God by denying every name we give, since each name was drawn from creatures and God is the cause of all creatures, not one of them. It pairs with the *cataphatic* way, which affirms the names Scripture gives; its "not" means "more than," not "less than." Pseudo-Dionysius gave the method its classic form, and Eckhart turned it inward. See [Pseudo-Dionysius and Meister Eckhart](eras/02-medieval/pseudo-dionysius-and-eckhart.md).
- **Archē:** Greek for origin/principle — the Presocratics' sought first principle of everything.
- **Ataraxia:** untroubledness of mind; the shared *goal* of Epicureans and Pyrrhonian skeptics, reached by opposite routes.
- **Aufhebung (sublation):** Hegel's term for a stage's being at once cancelled, preserved and raised into a new stage.
- **Authenticity:** in Kierkegaard and Heidegger, owning one's existence — facing one's finitude and choices rather than drifting with "the crowd" or "the They." Not mere sincerity or self-expression. See [Martin Heidegger](eras/05-20th-century/martin-heidegger.md).
- **Bad faith (mauvaise foi):** Sartre's term for self-deception about one's own freedom — pretending to be a fixed thing ("I had no choice; that's just how I am") to escape responsibility. See [Jean-Paul Sartre](eras/05-20th-century/jean-paul-sartre.md).
- **Biopower:** Foucault's term for modern power exercised over life itself — populations, health, birth and death rates — through institutions and statistics rather than the sovereign's threat of death. See [Michel Foucault](eras/05-20th-century/michel-foucault.md).
- **Boundary situation:** Jaspers's term for the limits — death, suffering, struggle, guilt, chance — that cannot be removed, managed, or seen around, and in which Existenz first becomes possible by foundering against them. See [Karl Jaspers](eras/05-20th-century/karl-jaspers.md).
- **Capabilities:** Sen's (and Nussbaum's) measure of advantage: what people are actually able to do and be — their real freedoms — rather than the income or resources they hold. Raised against Rawls in the debate over what justice should distribute. See [John Rawls](eras/05-20th-century/john-rawls.md).
- **Categorical imperative:** Kant's unconditional moral law (vs a *hypothetical* imperative: "if you want X, do Y").
- **Category mistake:** Ryle's diagnosis of treating something as belonging to one logical category when it belongs to another — his charge against Descartes' "ghost in the machine" picture of mind. See [Gilbert Ryle](eras/05-20th-century/gilbert-ryle.md).
- **Cogito:** Descartes' indubitable "I think / I am" found by methodical doubt.
- **Communicative action:** Habermas's term for interaction aimed at mutual understanding through reasons, contrasted with strategic action aimed at getting one's way. The health of a society depends on keeping the first from being crowded out by the second. See [Frankfurt School](eras/05-20th-century/frankfurt-school.md).
- **Consequentialism:** the family of moral theories that judge acts (or rules) solely by their outcomes. Utilitarianism is its best-known member; the label is broader. See [John Stuart Mill](eras/04-19th-century/john-stuart-mill.md).
- **Dasein:** Heidegger's term for the human being as the being for whom Being is an issue; being-there.
- **Deontology:** moral theory centered on duty and rules: some acts are required or forbidden regardless of their outcomes. Kant is the paradigm case; contrast consequentialism. See [Immanuel Kant](eras/03-early-modern/immanuel-kant.md).
- **Detachment / releasement (abegescheidenheit / gelâzenheit):** Eckhart's terms for letting go of the self-will that clings to things, to experiences and even to one's own holiness, so that the soul's ground is empty enough for the birth of the Word in it. Neither despising creatures nor running from the world: his model is Martha, who works in the world without clinging to it. See [Pseudo-Dionysius and Meister Eckhart](eras/02-medieval/pseudo-dionysius-and-eckhart.md).
- **Dialectic:** (variously) Socratic question-and-answer; Hegel's development through internal contradiction; Marx's materialist version. **Always ask which sense an author means.**
- **Différance:** Derrida's coined term (with an *a*) for the play of differences and deferral by which meaning is produced in language — never fully present, always referring onward to other signs. See [Jacques Derrida](eras/05-20th-century/jacques-derrida.md).
- **Discipline (disciplinary power):** Foucault's term for power that trains bodies through surveillance, timetables, examinations and norms — schools, barracks, hospitals, prisons — producing "docile bodies" rather than merely repressing them. See [Michel Foucault](eras/05-20th-century/michel-foucault.md).
- **Double consciousness:** Du Bois's term for the split self of the Black American, who is made to see himself through the eyes of a world that looks on with contempt or pity, and so feels a "twoness," American and Negro. The veil that produces it also gives "second-sight": a clearer view of white America than it has of itself. See [W. E. B. Du Bois and Frantz Fanon](eras/05-20th-century/du-bois-and-fanon.md).
- **Effective altruism:** the movement, associated with Singer, of using evidence and reasoning to do the most good one can — e.g. comparing charities by measurable impact. See [Peter Singer](eras/05-20th-century/peter-singer.md).
- **Elenchus:** Socrates' refutative cross-examination of a definition.
- **Empiricism / Rationalism:** see §3 headnote; a spectrum about the sources of knowledge, not two party memberships.
- **Encompassing, the (das Umgreifende):** Jaspers's name for whatever horizon contains a subject–object split without itself being an object within it — Being itself, or our own Existenz as we live it. It can be pointed to and clarified, never known as a thing is known. See [Karl Jaspers](eras/05-20th-century/karl-jaspers.md).
- **Epochē:** suspension of judgment (Pyrrhonian skeptics; Husserl reuses the word for phenomenological bracketing — different purpose, note the difference).
- **Erring conscience:** a conscience that is sincerely but wrongly mistaken after honest inquiry; the term comes from medieval moral theology (Aquinas asks "whether an erring conscience binds"), and Bayle made it the hinge of his case for toleration. Since we can only act on what seems true to us, and acting against one's conscience would be the real sin, he argued that an erring conscience has the same rights as a correct one — so the "heretic" has as much right to be left alone as the orthodox believer. See [Bayle and Voltaire](eras/03-early-modern/bayle-and-voltaire.md).
- **Esoteric writing:** Strauss's term for the practice, under persecution or its threat, of writing with two layers: an exoteric teaching acceptable to the many and the authorities, and an esoteric one, recoverable by careful readers, between the lines. See [Leo Strauss](eras/05-20th-century/leo-strauss.md).
- **Eudaimonia:** flourishing / living well over a whole life; mistranslated if rendered simply "happiness" as a feeling.
- **Expressivism:** Charles Taylor's name for the view of Herder and Hamann that thought and meaning are not complete inside us before we express them but take shape in outward expression — for Herder above all in language. From it follows pluralism about cultures: each people expresses itself in its own language, poetry and customs. See [Hamann and Herder](eras/03-early-modern/hamann-and-herder.md).
- **Falsifiability:** Popper's criterion of science: a theory is scientific only if some possible observation could count against it. Unfalsifiable claims (his targets included psychoanalysis and vulgar Marxism) explain everything and so predict nothing. See [Karl Popper](eras/05-20th-century/karl-popper.md).
- **Family resemblance:** Wittgenstein's alternative to essences: members of a category (his example: games) can be united by overlapping similarities with no single feature common to all. See [Wittgenstein (late)](eras/05-20th-century/wittgenstein-late.md).
- **Feeling of absolute dependence:** Schleiermacher's definition of the religious affection: the immediate self-consciousness that one's entire existence comes from and depends on a "Whence" outside oneself — not an emotion, and not inferred from anything. See [Friedrich Schleiermacher](eras/04-19th-century/friedrich-schleiermacher.md).
- **Forms (Platonic):** eternal, perfect patterns of which sensible things are copies and by which they are intelligible.
- **Gaze (the Look):** being seen as an object by another consciousness. For Sartre, the Other's look steals one's world and fixes one's identity; Beauvoir turns the idea on women's situation — living under a male gaze that makes them objects. See [Simone de Beauvoir](eras/05-20th-century/simone-de-beauvoir.md).
- **Genealogy:** Nietzsche/Foucault's method: explain a value or category by the contingent history and interests that produced it.
- **General will:** Rousseau's will of the citizen body aimed at the common good — distinct from the mere sum of private wills ("will of all").
- **Governmentality:** Foucault's term for the whole apparatus of governing — institutions, procedures, calculations and mentalities aimed at managing a population's welfare and conduct. See [Michel Foucault](eras/05-20th-century/michel-foucault.md).
- **Haecceity:** Scotus's "thisness," what makes an individual this individual.
- **Hermeneutics:** originally the theory of textual interpretation (above all scripture); Gadamer generalizes it into an account of all understanding, which always works from within a tradition and its "prejudices." See [Hans-Georg Gadamer](eras/05-20th-century/hans-georg-gadamer.md).
- **Hermeneutic circle:** the structure of all interpretation: the whole of a text (or life) is understood through its parts, and the parts through the whole, so understanding advances by circling between them rather than by starting from zero. An old principle of text interpretation, made central to modern hermeneutics by Schleiermacher (after Ast), radicalized by Heidegger and Gadamer. See [Friedrich Schleiermacher](eras/04-19th-century/friedrich-schleiermacher.md).
- **Idealism:** (many senses — disambiguate!) Berkeley: only minds and ideas exist. Kant: we know things only as structured by our cognition (transcendental idealism ≠ Berkeley). Hegel: reality is the self-development of Spirit/Reason.
- **Intentionality:** the mind's "aboutness" — the fact that thoughts, perceptions and desires are directed at objects. Brentano revived the scholastic term as the mark of the mental; Husserl made it phenomenology's central theme. See [Franz Brentano](eras/04-19th-century/franz-brentano.md).
- **Internal / external questions:** Carnap's distinction, once we adopt a linguistic framework (a set of words and rules for talking about a kind of thing, such as numbers): internal questions are asked and answered inside it ("Is there a prime between 10 and 20?"), while external questions ask whether the whole system of entities is real apart from any framework ("Do numbers really exist?"). Taken literally the external ones have no cognitive content; what makes sense is the practical question of whether to adopt the framework. See [Rudolf Carnap](eras/05-20th-century/rudolf-carnap.md).
- **Internal relations (doctrine of):** the view that every relation a thing has belongs to its nature, so that nothing could be what it is apart from everything else; contrast *external relations*, which leave their terms unchanged. Russell attacked it as the idealists' doctrine, but Bradley himself held that no relations, internal or external, are ultimately real. See [T. H. Green and F. H. Bradley](eras/04-19th-century/british-idealists.md).
- **Intuitive cognition:** for Scotus and Ockham, direct cognition of a thing as existing and present, contrasted with *abstractive* cognition, which considers a thing without settling whether it exists. A small distinction that drives their whole epistemology. See [John Duns Scotus](eras/02-medieval/john-duns-scotus.md).
- **Invisible hand:** Smith's metaphor for how self-interested action can unintentionally produce public benefit (in the *Wealth of Nations*, a merchant preferring domestic to foreign industry for his own security) — a phrase he used rarely and later readers inflated. See [Adam Smith](eras/03-early-modern/adam-smith.md).
- **Ius gentium:** Latin for "law of nations." For Suárez, human (not natural) law common to many peoples though not all, resting on shared custom rather than on one legislator and so changeable in principle — it regulates, for example, the property and slavery that natural law permits but does not itself establish. Not international law in the modern sense. See [Suárez and Grotius](eras/03-early-modern/suarez-and-grotius.md).
- **Language-game:** Wittgenstein's picture of language as a family of rule-governed activities woven into forms of life — asking, joking, commanding, thanking — with meaning given by use, not by naming. See [Wittgenstein (late)](eras/05-20th-century/wittgenstein-late.md).
- **Legal positivism:** the view that what the law *is* depends on social facts — what has been enacted, decided or customarily accepted — not on whether it is morally good, so "what is the law?" is a separate question from "is it good law, and should I obey it?" Hart's version, taken from Bentham and Austin; contrast Fuller's "inner morality" of law. See [H. L. A. Hart](eras/05-20th-century/h-l-a-hart.md).
- **Lifeworld (Lebenswelt):** Husserl's term for the pre-theoretical world of everyday experience that science presupposes and abstracts from; Habermas takes it up as the sphere of shared meanings colonized, he argues, by markets and bureaucracies. See [Frankfurt School](eras/05-20th-century/frankfurt-school.md).
- **Logos:** Greek word spanning speech, reason, account, cosmic order — Heraclitus and the Stoics exploit all its senses.
- **Modal realism:** David Lewis's view that other possible worlds are as real and concrete as ours, each sealed off in space and time from the others; "actual" works like "here," picking out the world we are in. What is possible for me depends on my *counterparts*, people in other worlds who closely resemble me. See [Sellars, Davidson, Putnam and Lewis](eras/05-20th-century/analytic-philosophy-after-quine.md).
- **Monad:** Leibniz's simple, perceiving, windowless substance.
- **Moral sense:** Hutcheson's name for a built-in power to be pleased by benevolent motives and displeased by malicious ones, in ourselves or others, working immediately, before any reasoning about our own advantage. Modeled on Locke's ideas of reflection, not a literal sense organ; Shaftesbury's "sense of right and wrong" came first, and Butler preferred to speak of conscience. See [Shaftesbury, Hutcheson and Butler](eras/03-early-modern/moral-sense-school.md).
- **Myth of the Given:** Sellars's name for the empiricist idea that knowledge rests on items, such as sense impressions, known directly, without inference and without needing other knowledge first. He argued that nothing can do both jobs: to serve as a reason it must be claim-like, and then it is no longer bare and free of concepts. Calling something knowledge places it in the "logical space of reasons." See [Sellars, Davidson, Putnam and Lewis](eras/05-20th-century/analytic-philosophy-after-quine.md).
- **Natural law:** a moral law rooted in nature, not in any state's enactments, and binding on every people whatever their religion. Suárez held that its content comes from nature but its force as law from God's will; Grotius defined it as a dictate of right reason and said it would hold "even if" there were no God, a hypothesis he called wicked. See [Suárez and Grotius](eras/03-early-modern/suarez-and-grotius.md).
- **Necessary Existent:** Avicenna/Aquinas's God as the being whose existence is not caused by another.
- **Narrative identity:** Ricoeur's account of selfhood: a life becomes a self by being emplotted — told as a story that mediates between *idem* (character, what stays the same) and *ipse* (self-constancy, as in keeping a promise). See [Paul Ricoeur](eras/05-20th-century/paul-ricoeur.md).
- **Nominalism / Realism (universals):** universals are only names/concepts / universals are real. *Not* the modern political senses of those words.
- **Non-identity problem:** Parfit's puzzle: choices that determine *which* people will exist (e.g. long-term policies) seem to wrong no one, since the people who result could not have existed otherwise — yet the choices can still seem wrong. See [Derek Parfit](eras/05-20th-century/derek-parfit.md).
- **Normal science:** Kuhn's term for research conducted within an accepted paradigm — puzzle-solving that elaborates the paradigm rather than testing its foundations. See [Thomas Kuhn](eras/05-20th-century/thomas-kuhn.md).
- **Noumenon / Phenomenon:** Kant: thing as it is in itself (unknowable) / thing as it appears under our cognitive forms.
- **Nous:** Greek for intellect/mind — in Aristotle, the faculty that grasps first principles directly, and the "active intellect" of *De Anima* III.5, whose separateness from the body was debated for centuries. See [Aristotle](eras/01-ancient/aristotle.md).
- **Occasionalism:** the view that God is the only true cause and created things are merely the occasions on which God acts: when one billiard ball strikes another, God moves the second. Malebranche built it into a full system; al-Ghazali is standardly read as an occasionalist, though scholars dispute it. Hume kept the negative argument (we never perceive a necessary connection) and dropped God. See [Nicolas Malebranche](eras/03-early-modern/nicolas-malebranche.md).
- **Ontological argument:** Anselm's a priori argument from the concept of God to God's existence.
- **Open question (argument):** Moore's test against defining "good": for any proposed definition (good = pleasure, good = what we desire), it remains an open, substantive question whether things with that property are good — so "good" is not identical to any natural property. This is his case against the "naturalistic fallacy." See [G. E. Moore](eras/05-20th-century/g-e-moore.md).
- **Original position:** Rawls's hypothetical standpoint for choosing principles of justice: parties choose behind the veil of ignorance, not knowing their talents, class, or conception of the good. See [John Rawls](eras/05-20th-century/john-rawls.md).
- **Other, the (autrui):** the other person as irreducibly other. For Levinas, the face of the Other commands "thou shalt not kill" and makes ethics prior to all theory; the term also runs through Sartre and Beauvoir in different senses. See [Emmanuel Levinas](eras/05-20th-century/emmanuel-levinas.md).
- **Paradigm:** Kuhn's term for the constellation of theories, methods, standards and exemplary problems that defines a scientific community's work; a *paradigm shift* is the (rare, contested) replacement of one by another after crisis. See [Thomas Kuhn](eras/05-20th-century/thomas-kuhn.md).
- **Performativity:** Butler's account of gender as constituted by repeated acts — ways of walking, dressing, speaking and desiring — rather than expressed by an inner core. The term comes from Austin's sentences that do what they say ("I promise"). It is not a costume chosen each morning but the forced, repeated citing of norms, which forms the self that might later resist them. See [Irigaray, Kristeva and Butler](eras/05-20th-century/feminist-philosophy-after-beauvoir.md).
- **Personal identity:** what makes a person at one time the *same* person as at another. Locke ties it to continuity of consciousness (memory); Parfit argues the relation that matters can hold by degrees and that identity is "not what matters." See [John Locke](eras/03-early-modern/john-locke.md).
- **Phenomenology:** Husserl's descriptive method for conscious experience; later, a whole continental movement.
- **Physis / Nomos:** nature / convention or law. The Sophists and their critics fought over whether justice, morality and the gods exist by physis or merely by nomos — the first great nature-versus-culture debate. See [Protagoras & the Sophists](eras/01-ancient/protagoras-and-the-sophists.md).
- **Positive freedom:** T. H. Green's account of freedom as a real power to do something worth doing, which a society secures for all its members, rather than merely the absence of restraint. It justified reform laws and gave the New Liberalism its philosophical basis; Isaiah Berlin later warned that talk of "true" freedom can license coercion. See [T. H. Green and F. H. Bradley](eras/04-19th-century/british-idealists.md).
- **Praxis:** action/practice — in Marx, the sensuous, world-changing activity through which humans make themselves and their history; philosophy should not only interpret the world but join in changing it. See [Karl Marx](eras/04-19th-century/karl-marx.md).
- **Presentational symbol:** Langer's term for a symbol — an image, a rite, a work of music — whose meaning lies in the whole simultaneous form rather than in parts translatable one by one, as words are in discursive language. Art is her prime case: it articulates the forms of feeling. See [Susanne Langer](eras/05-20th-century/susanne-langer.md).
- **Principle of charity:** Davidson's rule for radical interpretation: assume the speaker's beliefs are mostly true and consistent by our lights, the only way to break the circle of working out belief and meaning together. Not politeness but a condition of treating the noises as language at all; disagreement only makes sense against a large background of agreement. See [Sellars, Davidson, Putnam and Lewis](eras/05-20th-century/analytic-philosophy-after-quine.md).
- **Principle of tolerance:** Carnap's principle that rival positions in logic and mathematics are not true or false claims about one reality but proposals for languages, judged by how well they serve a purpose. Anyone may build a language; the only demand is to state its rules clearly. See [Rudolf Carnap](eras/05-20th-century/rudolf-carnap.md).
- **Private language argument:** Wittgenstein's argument that a language intelligible in principle to only one person is impossible: without public criteria of correct use, "seeming right" and "being right" collapse into each other. See [Wittgenstein (late)](eras/05-20th-century/wittgenstein-late.md).
- **Procession and return:** Proclus's law that every effect *remains* in its cause, *proceeds* from it, and *returns* (reverts) to it by desiring and imitating its source — as a ray of light stays connected to the sun, travels out from it, and points back to it. Causes give without being diminished, and this outflow and return links every level of being. See [Porphyry, Iamblichus, Proclus and Hypatia](eras/01-ancient/late-neoplatonists.md).
- **Public sphere:** the arena — salons, press, associations — where private persons form public opinion outside state control. Habermas traces its bourgeois rise and its deformation by mass media and commerce. See [Frankfurt School](eras/05-20th-century/frankfurt-school.md).
- **Recognition (Anerkennung):** being acknowledged as a self-conscious agent by another such agent. Hegel's master–slave dialectic argues self-consciousness can only be satisfied by recognition from an equal — a theme running from Fichte to modern recognition theory. See [G. W. F. Hegel](eras/04-19th-century/g-w-f-hegel.md).
- **Re-enactment:** Collingwood's account of how the historian knows the past: by re-thinking past thoughts in one's own mind, as one follows Plato's argument or works through Caesar's situation as he saw it. "Thought" means reasoning that can be re-thought, not feelings, and re-enactment is critical, judging as it re-thinks; it is not telepathy. See [Dilthey and Collingwood](eras/04-19th-century/dilthey-and-collingwood.md).
- **Reflective equilibrium:** Rawls's method of justification: adjust principles and considered judgments back and forth until they cohere, with neither side fixed in advance. Now a standard method across ethics and political philosophy. See [John Rawls](eras/05-20th-century/john-rawls.md).
- **Reification (Verdinglichung):** Lukács's term, taken up by the Frankfurt School: treating human relations and processes as if they were things — fixed, natural, and beyond anyone's control. See [Frankfurt School](eras/05-20th-century/frankfurt-school.md).
- **Rhizome:** Deleuze and Guattari's image of thought: like crabgrass or a ginger root, it has no centre, and any point can connect to any other. Contrast the *tree*, with root, trunk and branches: a hierarchy with one origin. *A Thousand Plateaus* is meant to be a rhizome itself. See [Gilles Deleuze](eras/05-20th-century/gilles-deleuze.md).
- **Rule of recognition:** Hart's secondary rule giving the criteria for which rules belong to a legal system (in the UK, roughly, whatever the Queen in Parliament enacts). It is not valid because a higher rule says so; it exists as a social fact, the rule officials actually use when deciding what counts as law. Contrast Kelsen's "basic norm," which must be presupposed. See [H. L. A. Hart](eras/05-20th-century/h-l-a-hart.md).
- **Semantic externalism:** the view, argued by Putnam with his Twin Earth case, that what is in the head does not fix what words refer to: two molecule-for-molecule twins saying "water" refer to different stuffs (H₂O and XYZ) if their worlds differ. Reference depends on the world's underlying natures and on a division of linguistic labour with experts. See [Sellars, Davidson, Putnam and Lewis](eras/05-20th-century/analytic-philosophy-after-quine.md).
- **Semiotic, the:** Kristeva's name for the rhythm, tone and sound pattern of speech, charged with bodily drives, which comes before the infant's grasp of words and is bound up with the mother's body. Contrast the *symbolic*: language as grammar, logic and reference, tied to social law. Every utterance mixes the two, and both belong to every speaker. See [Irigaray, Kristeva and Butler](eras/05-20th-century/feminist-philosophy-after-beauvoir.md).
- **Social contract:** the device of explaining political authority by an (usually hypothetical) agreement among free individuals — used very differently by Hobbes (security), Locke (rights and consent), and Rousseau (the general will). See [Thomas Hobbes](eras/03-early-modern/thomas-hobbes.md).
- **Substance / attribute / mode:** Spinoza's three-level ontology: substance is what exists in itself (God-or-Nature, unique); an attribute is what intellect perceives as a substance's essence (Thought and Extension are the two we know); a mode is a particular modification of substance — every finite thing and thought. See [Baruch Spinoza](eras/03-early-modern/baruch-spinoza.md).
- **Superstructure (base and):** Marx's architectural metaphor: the economic base (forces and relations of production) conditions the superstructure of law, politics and ideology built on it. How strong the "conditioning" is remains a standing scholarly debate. See [Karl Marx](eras/04-19th-century/karl-marx.md).
- **Supposition:** the medieval theory, given its classic form by Ockham, of how terms stand for things in propositions — the medieval logician's tool for untangling what a sentence actually commits us to. See [William of Ockham](eras/02-medieval/william-of-ockham.md).
- **Synthetic a priori:** Kant's pivotal category: judgments that genuinely extend knowledge (synthetic) yet are knowable independently of experience (a priori) — his examples include arithmetic and the principle of causality. Whether and how they are possible is the *Critique of Pure Reason*'s central question. See [Immanuel Kant](eras/03-early-modern/immanuel-kant.md).
- **Tabula rasa:** "blank slate", the later shorthand for Locke's image of the mind as "white paper" furnished by experience; he denies innate ideas, not innate faculties. See [John Locke](eras/03-early-modern/john-locke.md).
- **Telos:** end/purpose; Aristotle explains things partly by their telos (final cause).
- **Theurgy:** "god-work": ritual actions, prayers, sacred names and objects through which, Iamblichus held, the gods themselves lift the soul toward them. Against Plotinus, he taught that the soul descends completely into the body and cannot think its own way back, so union with the gods comes from the rites, not intellectual effort. He insisted the rites do not force the gods. See [Porphyry, Iamblichus, Proclus and Hypatia](eras/01-ancient/late-neoplatonists.md).
- **Transcendental:** Kantian: concerned with the *conditions of possibility* of experience/knowledge, not with a world "beyond" (avoid the colloquial "transcendent" confusion).
- **Univocity:** Scotus's claim that "being" is said in *one* sense of God and creatures (against Aquinas's analogy): without a shared concept of being, theology could prove nothing about God at all. See [John Duns Scotus](eras/02-medieval/john-duns-scotus.md).
- **Utilitarianism:** right action maximizes overall well-being/happiness (Bentham, Mill, Singer, in different versions).
- **Veil of ignorance:** Rawls's device for choosing principles of justice without knowing one's own social position.
- **Verification principle:** the Vienna Circle's criterion of meaning: a (non-tautological) statement is meaningful only if some possible experience could verify it or count toward its truth. It famously struggles to verify itself — a problem its defenders had to answer. See [Vienna Circle / Logical Positivism](eras/05-20th-century/vienna-circle-logical-positivism.md).
- **Verstehen (understanding):** Dilthey's term for how the human sciences grasp the meaning of human actions and products from within, since the inquirer is the same kind of being as those studied; contrast *Erklären* (explaining), the natural sciences' bringing of events under causal laws from outside. Not mere empathy: for the late Dilthey it works through public expressions and contexts. See [Dilthey and Collingwood](eras/04-19th-century/dilthey-and-collingwood.md).
- **Virtual, the:** Deleuze's term, from Bergson, for what is real but not actual: a field of differential relations and thresholds ("singular points") at which a system changes its behaviour. Things become actual through *actualization*, which never resembles the virtual it comes from; contrast the *possible*, pictured as a ready-made copy of the real. See [Gilles Deleuze](eras/05-20th-century/gilles-deleuze.md).
- **Virtue (aretē):** excellence of character/function, not merely rule-obedience; central to Plato, Aristotle, Stoics, Aquinas.
- **Vision in God:** Malebranche's view that we perceive bodies through ideas that exist in God, who contains the archetypes of everything he made, since every alternative source of ideas fails. We do not see God's essence, only what in God represents creatures. See [Nicolas Malebranche](eras/03-early-modern/nicolas-malebranche.md).
- **Will to power:** Nietzsche's name for the drive he takes to underlie life and value-creation — not mere political domination, though how psychological, biological or metaphysical the doctrine is remains debated. See [Friedrich Nietzsche](eras/04-19th-century/friedrich-nietzsche.md).
