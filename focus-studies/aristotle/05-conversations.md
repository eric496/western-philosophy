# Aristotle in Conversation — Backward, Sideways, Forward

*Focus study: Aristotle · [Dossier home](README.md) · [Library entry](../../eras/01-ancient/aristotle.md)*

Aristotle is the first philosopher who does philosophy by writing its history. His major treatises open by setting out what the predecessors said, their *endoxa* or reputable opinions, on the principle that a good theory should explain why sensible people believed what they did. So his backward conversation is unusually explicit. Sideways, he argued with the Academy's heirs and with Athens' school of rhetoric, and worked for decades beside the colleague who succeeded him. Forward, his logic became the West's textbook, his corpus passed through Arabic into the medieval universities, early modern science made its name by rejecting him, and in the twentieth century his ethics came back. This file traces those conversations; the scholarly controversies and the reception by era are in [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md).

## Backward: the predecessors he lines up

### The Presocratics, as Aristotle tells their story

Much of what we know about the earliest Greek philosophers we know because Aristotle summarized them, and he summarized them with an agenda. *Metaphysics* A (Book I) argues that wisdom is knowledge of first causes, then tests his own four causes (material, formal, efficient and final; see [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md)) against everyone before him. His verdict: each predecessor reached for some of the four, and none grasped all of them clearly.

The story starts with matter. In Ross's translation, "Thales, the founder of this type of philosophy, says the principle is water" (*Met.* A.3, 983b20–21). Aristotle then guesses at the reasoning, that nourishment is moist and seeds are wet, and marks the guess with a "perhaps." [Thales](../../eras/01-ancient/thales-of-miletus.md) left nothing that survives, so this is Thales filtered through a later system. [Empedocles](../../eras/01-ancient/empedocles.md) earns credit twice: he was the first to name four material elements, earth, water, air and fire, which Aristotle kept for the region below the moon, and he split the cause of motion in two, Love and Strife. Aristotle's standing complaint is that he uses the two loosely and never says why the cycle turns when it does.

Then comes a turn Aristotle enjoys. When someone said that mind was present in nature as the cause of all order, "he seemed like a sober man in contrast with the random talk of his predecessors" (*Met.* A.3, 984b15–18, Ross). That someone is Anaxagoras, treated in the library's [Presocratic Pluralists](../../eras/01-ancient/presocratic-pluralists.md) entry. The praise has a sting, the one Socrates gives it in Plato's *Phaedo*: having found Mind, Anaxagoras uses it only when mechanical explanation runs out. The final cause, the "for the sake of which," was glimpsed and dropped. [Democritus](../../eras/01-ancient/democritus-of-abdera.md) and Leucippus get the opposite treatment: "the full and the empty are the elements" (*Met.* A.4, 985b4–5, Ross), atoms and void moving by necessity, with no purpose anywhere. The [Pythagoreans](../../eras/01-ancient/pythagoras.md) made numbers the principles of things and, Aristotle reports, "say that things exist by 'imitation' of numbers" (*Met.* A.6, 987b11–12, Ross), a phrase he will turn against Plato.

*Physics* I runs the same review for a narrower question: how is change possible at all? Here the opponent is Elea. [Parmenides](../../eras/01-ancient/parmenides-of-elea.md) and his follower Melissus held that what is, is one and motionless. Strictly, Aristotle says, arguing with them is not physics, which assumes that things move; he argues anyway, and his answer in *Physics* I.7–8 is one of the decisive moves of ancient philosophy. Change needs three things, not two: a subject that persists, a form it gains, and a lack (*sterēsis*, "privation") it loses. The unmusical man becomes musical; nothing comes from sheer nothing. "The first of those who studied science were misled in their search for truth and the nature of things by their inexperience" (*Phys.* I.8, 191a24–26, Hardie and Gaye), which on his diagnosis was failing to see that "not being" is said in more than one way. The paradoxes of [Zeno of Elea](../../eras/01-ancient/zeno-of-elea.md) get their answer later, in *Physics* VI.

One more predecessor is answered in week 2. In *Physics* II.8 Aristotle states, more fairly than many later critics of teleology, the view that animals' parts arose by chance and only the combinations that happened to fit survived, "as Empedocles says his 'man-faced ox-progeny' did" (198b31–32, Hardie and Gaye). He replies that what happens always or for the most part cannot be chance. Nineteenth-century readers saw Darwin in the rejected view; the argument is in [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md).

A caution belongs with all of this. Aristotle's history has a direction of its own: the predecessors are shown groping toward the four causes. Scholars use his reports as indispensable evidence and read them with that frame in mind. Where a report can be checked against a surviving fragment, the fragment wins.

### Socrates, through Plato

Aristotle was born fifteen years after [Socrates](../../eras/01-ancient/socrates.md) was executed in 399 BCE. He knew him through Plato's dialogues and the Academy's memory, and he gives the most careful ancient statement of how Socrates differed from Plato. Socrates, he says, "was busying himself about ethical matters and neglecting the world of nature as a whole but seeking the universal in these ethical matters, and fixed thought for the first time on definitions" (*Met.* A.6, 987b1–4, Ross). Later, more exactly, "two things may be fairly ascribed to Socrates—inductive arguments and universal definition," but Socrates "did not make the universals or the definitions exist apart" (*Met.* M.4, 1078b27–31, Ross). Plato did. This is the hinge of the "Socratic problem" discussed in the [Plato dossier's reception file](../plato/06-reception-and-debates.md). Aristotle is detached but not neutral: the line he draws is the line along which he attacks the Forms.

The ethics owes Socrates a method, testing definitions against cases and against what people say, and one long engagement. In *NE* VII Aristotle takes up *akrasia*, acting against one's better judgment, which Socrates had denied could happen. He ends by conceding that "the position that Socrates sought to establish actually seems to result" (*NE* VII.3, 1147b14–15, Ross), with a subtler account of how knowledge can be present but not in use.

### Plato and the Academy

The largest conversation of Aristotle's life is with [Plato](../../eras/01-ancient/plato.md), and the library already tells it twice: in [Aristotle's entry](../../eras/01-ancient/aristotle.md) and in the [Plato dossier's conversations file](../plato/05-conversations.md), which traces the Forms critique from the Academy's side. The arguments themselves, the Third Man and the charge that separate Forms explain nothing, are in [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md). Three points are worth carrying through the nine weeks.

First, the tone. Before criticizing Plato's Form of the Good, Aristotle writes that "while both are dear, piety requires us to honour truth above our friends" (*NE* I.6, 1096a16–17, Ross). The critique is a family argument; he often says "we" when he means the Platonists. Second, the charge of empty language: "to say that they are patterns and the other things share in them is to use empty words and poetical metaphors" (*Met.* A.9, 991a20–22, Ross). Third, how much he kept. Form is still the principle of being and of knowledge, contemplation is still the highest life, and the cosmos is still ordered toward the good. What changes is location: form is in things, and has to be found by studying them. That is why the library entry calls the empiricist-versus-rationalist contrast a rough map.

## Sideways: the Academy's heirs, a rival school, a colleague

### Speusippus and Xenocrates

When Plato died in 347 BCE the Academy passed to his nephew Speusippus, and Aristotle left Athens; the reasons are weighed in [01-life-and-times.md](01-life-and-times.md). The philosophical quarrel is in the *Metaphysics* itself. Speusippus gave up the Forms and made mathematical numbers the first realities, with a separate principle for each further level of being. Aristotle's objection is that the world then has no unity: "the observed facts show that nature is not a series of episodes, like a bad tragedy" (*Met.* N.3, 1090b19–20, Ross). A bad tragedy, in the *Poetics*, is one whose scenes follow each other without necessity, so the objection turns his literary theory into a test for metaphysics. Xenocrates, head of the Academy from 339 BCE, went with Aristotle to Assos after Plato's death, on Strabo's later report. He identified the Forms with mathematical numbers, a view *Metaphysics* M–N treats as the worst option on offer.

### Isocrates and the quarrel over rhetoric

Athens' other great school belonged to Isocrates (436–338 BCE), whose fee-paying program in rhetoric and statesmanship called itself "philosophy" and dismissed the Academy's mathematics and dialectic as useless. Cicero (*On the Orator* III.141), writing three centuries later, says Aristotle began teaching rhetoric in the Academy in open rivalry with Isocrates, adapting a line of Euripides: it would be shameful to keep silent and let Isocrates speak. Treat the anecdote as tradition. The certain outcome is the *Rhetoric* (week 9), which treats persuasion as a craft with its own logic, built on argument (*logos*), the speaker's character (*ēthos*) and the hearer's emotions (*pathos*). It answers both Plato's *Gorgias*, which called rhetoric a knack of flattery, and Isocrates, who taught it without a theory.

### Theophrastus

The colleague who mattered most was Theophrastus of Eresos (c. 371–287 BCE). He met Aristotle in the Academy, went with him to Assos, Lesbos and Macedonia, returned with him to Athens, and ran the Lyceum for about thirty-five years after Aristotle left in 323 BCE. Their work on Lesbos was shared: Aristotle's animals have a companion in Theophrastus' *Enquiry into Plants* and *Plant Explanations*, the founding works of botany. Theophrastus' short *Metaphysics* is the first critical reading of Aristotle from inside the school; it asks whether teleology really reaches everything, and how an unmoved mover could cause the heavens' rotation.

## Forward: the school, the commentators, three faiths, the revolt, the revival

### The Peripatetics and Alexander of Aphrodisias

After Theophrastus the Lyceum declined. Its next head, Strato of Lampsacus, made physics more naturalistic and did without the unmoved mover. Theophrastus' will left his books, Aristotle's manuscripts among them, to his pupil Neleus. Strabo and Plutarch tell how Neleus' heirs hid them in a cellar at Scepsis, in Asia Minor, until they were bought back in the first century BCE. The story is late and almost certainly simplified. What is firm is that Andronicus of Rhodes edited the treatises in the first century BCE, and the arrangement we read is largely his, as [02-works-and-texts.md](02-works-and-texts.md) explains.

The greatest ancient commentator, Alexander of Aphrodisias, worked around 200 CE. He dedicated *On Fate* to the emperors Septimius Severus and Caracalla, joint rulers from 198 to 209, in thanks for an endowed chair of philosophy; whether it was one of the Athenian chairs Marcus Aurelius had founded is uncertain. Alexander defended Aristotle as a coherent whole against the Stoics. His most consequential reading concerns week 4: he identified the "active intellect" of *De Anima* III.5 with the divine first cause rather than with a part of each human soul. That reading, flagged as disputed in [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), started a debate that ran through the Arabic and Latin Middle Ages. After him the commentators were Neoplatonists.

### Porphyry and Boethius

[Plotinus](../../eras/01-ancient/plotinus.md) taught Aristotle beside Plato and absorbed much of him. His pupil Porphyry, in [The Late Neoplatonists](../../eras/01-ancient/late-neoplatonists.md), wrote the *Isagoge*, a short introduction to the *Categories* (week 1). It defined genus, species, difference, property and accident, and raised without answering the question whether genera and species exist in things, in the mind, or apart. [Boethius](../../eras/02-medieval/boethius.md) translated the *Categories*, the *De Interpretatione* and the *Isagoge* into Latin around 510–522. For six centuries the Latin West's Aristotle was an Aristotle of logic only, and the medieval problem of universals, argued from [Abelard](../../eras/02-medieval/peter-abelard.md) to [Ockham](../../eras/02-medieval/william-of-ockham.md), grew out of those few pages.

### Avicenna, Averroes, Maimonides

The rest of the corpus reached the medieval world through Arabic. [Avicenna](../../eras/02-medieval/avicenna.md) (980–1037) says he read the *Metaphysics* forty times without grasping its purpose until a treatise by al-Fārābī unlocked it. He rebuilt Aristotle into a system of his own, with Neoplatonic emanation and a sharp line between what a thing is and that it exists. [Averroes](../../eras/02-medieval/averroes.md) (1126–1198) thought Avicenna had buried Aristotle under that system and set out to recover "the real Aristotle" in commentaries on almost the whole corpus, which is why Latin readers called him "the Commentator." His reading of *De Anima* III, that the receptive intellect is one for all humans, became the most contested doctrine of the thirteenth century. [Maimonides](../../eras/02-medieval/maimonides.md) (1138–1204) took on the hardest collision with Scripture, Aristotle's eternal world, and concluded that neither eternity nor creation can be demonstrated. Each of the three faiths had to decide what to do with the god of week 5, a first mover that creates nothing and cares for no one.

### Aquinas and the scholastics

The twelfth- and thirteenth-century Latin translations, from Arabic and then from Greek, gave the universities the whole Aristotle, and they did not take him calmly. Paris banned lectures on his natural philosophy in 1210; by 1255 his works were set texts in its arts faculty. [Thomas Aquinas](../../eras/02-medieval/thomas-aquinas.md) (1225–1274) calls him simply "the Philosopher" and wrote line-by-line commentaries on the *Physics*, *Metaphysics*, *De Anima* and *Nicomachean Ethics*. His synthesis keeps Aristotle's virtues, his hylomorphism (substance as form in matter) and his first mover, and sets them inside a theology of creation, natural law and grace. In 1270 he wrote *On the Unity of the Intellect against the Averroists* to defend an individual intellect for each person. [Duns Scotus](../../eras/02-medieval/john-duns-scotus.md), Ockham and, later, [Suárez](../../eras/03-early-modern/suarez-and-grotius.md) argued inside an Aristotelian framework even when they broke with Thomas. The [Aquinas *Summa* study guide](../../study/aquinas-summa.md) shows the synthesis at work.

### The early-modern revolt

Early modern philosophy defined itself against this school Aristotle, and said so. [Francis Bacon](../../eras/03-early-modern/francis-bacon.md) called his method *Novum Organum* (1620), a "new instrument" to replace the *Organon*, and rejected the syllogism as able to compel assent but not to grip nature. Galileo, who has no library entry, gave the Aristotelian physics to a character called Simplicio in his *Dialogue* (1632) and attacked its account of falling bodies in *Two New Sciences* (1638). [René Descartes](../../eras/03-early-modern/rene-descartes.md) built a physics of matter in motion with no final causes; in a letter to Mersenne of 28 January 1641 he confided that the *Meditations* contained the foundations of his physics, and hoped readers would accept his principles before noticing that they destroyed Aristotle's. [Thomas Hobbes](../../eras/03-early-modern/thomas-hobbes.md) was blunter: "I beleeve that scarce any thing can be more absurdly said in naturall Philosophy, than that which now is called Aristotles Metaphysiques, nor more repugnant to Government, than much of that hee hath said in his Politiques" (*Leviathan*, 1651, ch. 46). The target, for all four, was mostly the Aristotle of the schools.

### Hegel and Heidegger

The rehabilitation began with [G. W. F. Hegel](../../eras/04-19th-century/g-w-f-hegel.md), who lectured on Aristotle as one of the deepest minds philosophy had produced and ended his *Encyclopaedia of the Philosophical Sciences* (1830) with a passage of *Metaphysics* Λ.7, in Greek and without comment, on the divine thought that thinks itself, as if Aristotle had already said the last word of Hegel's own system. [Martin Heidegger](../../eras/05-20th-century/martin-heidegger.md) went further. His Freiburg and Marburg lectures of the early 1920s are largely readings of Aristotle, among them the *Rhetoric* and *Nicomachean Ethics* VI, whose distinction between *phronēsis* (practical wisdom) and *technē* (craft knowledge) he made central to his account of human existence. A 1924 course famously waves away the philosopher's biography: Aristotle was born, worked, and died; what matters is the thinking.

### Arendt, Anscombe, Foot, MacIntyre, Nussbaum

Among Heidegger's Marburg students in 1924–25 was [Hannah Arendt](../../eras/05-20th-century/hannah-arendt.md). *The Human Condition* (1958) builds on Aristotle's distinction between *praxis*, action done for its own sake among equals, and *poiēsis*, making something, and argues that the Latin rendering of his "political animal" as "social animal" lost his meaning: a life lived in public speech and action, not merely in company.

The same year Elizabeth Anscombe published "Modern Moral Philosophy," the manifesto of revived virtue ethics. She argued that the modern moral "ought" survived from a law-based ethics whose lawgiver had been dropped, and pointed philosophers back toward virtue and flourishing. Philippa Foot carried the program to *Natural Goodness* (2001), which treats moral evaluation as a case of evaluating any living thing by the standards of its life form, a recognizably Aristotelian thought about function (week 6). Both are in the [Anscombe & Foot](../../eras/05-20th-century/anscombe-and-foot.md) entry. [Alasdair MacIntyre](../../eras/05-20th-century/alasdair-macintyre.md) made the historical case in *After Virtue* (1981): the Enlightenment tried to justify inherited moral rules without any account of what human beings are for, and had to fail; the way forward was a renewed Aristotelian tradition of practices and virtues. The [*After Virtue* study guide](../../study/macintyre-after-virtue.md) follows the argument. [Martha Nussbaum](../../eras/05-20th-century/martha-nussbaum.md) took two Aristotles. In *The Fragility of Goodness* (1986) he is the thinker who admits that luck can damage even a virtuous life, as with Priam in *NE* I. In her capabilities approach he is the Aristotle of the *Politics*, for whom a city is judged by whether its citizens can live well, with his exclusions of slaves and women dropped, the exclusions week 8 asks readers to criticize.

## The themes trace

The library's [problem index](../../themes.md) places Aristotle in seven of its nine threads. In most of them he gives the first answer to Plato, the position later thinkers build on or define themselves against.

- [§1 Knowledge & Skepticism](../../themes.md#1-knowledge--skepticism--what-can-we-know-and-how-do-we-know-it) — All knowledge starts in perception; the intellect abstracts forms and demonstrates conclusions from self-evident first principles. Week 1, *Posterior Analytics* II.19.
- [§2 Mind & Body](../../themes.md#2-mind--body--what-is-mind-and-how-does-it-relate-to-the-body) — Soul is the form of the living body, not a second thing in it, though the active intellect may be separable. Week 4; Averroes, Aquinas and Descartes answer it directly.
- [§3 God & Religion](../../themes.md#3-god--religion--does-god-exist-and-what-follows-for-faith-and-reason) — The unmoved mover, thought thinking itself, moves all things by being loved. Week 5, and the medieval argument from Avicenna to Aquinas.
- [§4 Ethics & the Good Life](../../themes.md#4-ethics--the-good-life--how-should-i-live-and-what-is-right) — *Eudaimonia* is activity of soul in accordance with virtue; virtue is a trained mean, steered by practical wisdom. Weeks 6 and 7, and the line to Anscombe, Foot, MacIntyre and Nussbaum.
- [§5 Politics & Justice](../../themes.md#5-politics--justice--how-should-we-live-together-and-who-may-rule) — The polis exists by nature, for living well; constitutions are judged by whether rulers aim at the common advantage or their own. Week 8, with Hobbes and Arendt as sharp later replies.
- [§7 Language & Meaning](../../themes.md#7-language--meaning--how-do-words-and-thoughts-hook-onto-the-world) — Spoken words symbolize affections of the soul, which are likenesses of things; the syllogism gives reasoning a machinery of proof. Week 1, and the logic Boethius passed to the medieval schools.
- [§8 Science & Method](../../themes.md#8-science--method--how-should-inquiry-proceed-and-what-does-it-prove) — Science demonstrates effects from their causes; the biology models knowledge built patiently from observed differences. Weeks 2 and 3, and the target of Bacon's *Novum Organum*.

The index does not place him in §6 (Self & Identity) or §9 (History & Progress). The first absence is a filing decision: Aristotle's account of what keeps a thing the same through change (form persisting through changing matter) and of the soul as what a living thing is does the work that thread needs, and the index carries it under §2. The second is closer to a real absence. Aristotle collected histories of constitutions but wrote no philosophy of history, and he thought the arts and sciences had been found and lost many times over (*Met.* Λ.8). A direction for history came later, from Christian and then Enlightenment sources.
