# Life and Times: Stagira, the Academy, Alexander, and the Lyceum
**English** | [简体中文](../../zh/focus-studies/aristotle/01-life-and-times.md)

Aristotle's biography has the same shape as Plato's, turned inside out. With Plato, the polished works survive whole and the life survives in scraps. With Aristotle, the polished works are almost all lost, and what survives is the working library: lecture notes and research files never meant for the public. The life is a little better documented than Plato's, but no contemporary wrote it. Our fullest source is again Diogenes Laertius' *Lives of the Eminent Philosophers*, Book V, written in the third century CE, more than five hundred years after Aristotle died. Diogenes matters because he copies older writers: Hermippus, a biographer of the late third century BCE; Apollodorus, whose *Chronology* supplies the dates; and Aristotle's own will, which he transcribes in full. But he copies the gossip too, some of it from Aristotle's rivals. Later still come the Neoplatonist *Lives* of late antiquity, which turn the philosopher into a saint. Firmer evidence is scarce: Aristotle's own writings, which mention places but almost never his life, and a few inscriptions. This file keeps the dossier's rule: at each step it marks what is recorded, what is reported only in the late *Lives*, and what is legend.

## Stagira, Macedon, and a doctor's son

Aristotle was born in 384 BCE (Apollodorus, via Diogenes, gives the first year of the 99th Olympiad, that is 384/383) at Stagira, a small Greek city on the Chalcidice peninsula in the northern Aegean. Stagira was a Greek colony, not a Macedonian town, but it sat on the edge of the kingdom of Macedon, and his family was tied to the Macedonian court from the start.

His father, Nicomachus, was a physician. Diogenes reports, from Hermippus, that he lived with King Amyntas III of Macedon "in the capacity of physician and friend" (trans. R. D. Hicks). Amyntas was the father of Philip II and the grandfather of Alexander the Great. The family claimed descent from Asclepius, the hero-god of medicine; many Greek medical families made that claim, and it tells us they saw themselves as hereditary doctors, not that the descent is real. His mother was Phaestis, whose family is said to have come from Chalcis on the island of Euboea. That detail returns at the end of the story, when Aristotle fled to a house at Chalcis.

Both parents died while he was young. The late *Lives* say a relative, Proxenus of Atarneus, became his guardian. Aristotle's will supports the outline: it orders a statue of Proxenus and makes Proxenus' son Nicanor the chief guardian of his own children. A doctor's household is an obvious background for the man who would dissect animals, but that link is an inference by later readers, not something the sources report.

## Twenty years in the Academy

In 367 BCE, at seventeen, Aristotle went to Athens and joined Plato's Academy. Diogenes gives both numbers: he became Plato's pupil at seventeen and stayed twenty years. Plato was away in Sicily at the time, on his second voyage to Syracuse, so the young man's first teachers were the school's senior members, among them the mathematician and astronomer Eudoxus of Cnidus. (The Plato dossier's [life file](../plato/01-life-and-times.md) describes the Academy and the Sicilian voyages.)

The Academy was a community of research and conversation, not a university with courses and degrees, and Aristotle stayed two decades, first as a student, then as a teacher and researcher. In those years he wrote his first published works: dialogues in Plato's manner, and an *Exhortation to Philosophy* (the *Protrepticus*). He seems also to have taught rhetoric, in rivalry with the orator Isocrates. Many scholars place the early logical works, the *Categories* and the *Topics*, here too, though that dating is an inference from their content.

How he and Plato got on is a question the late sources answer with stories. Diogenes and the miscellany-writer Aelian (third century CE) report that Plato compared him to a colt kicking its mother, and Aelian tells a long tale of Aristotle humiliating the aged Plato in argument. These are anecdotes from rival schools and belong to legend. The firmest evidence is Aristotle's own. Criticising the theory of Forms in the *Nicomachean Ethics*, he speaks as a member of the family: the Forms were "introduced by friends of our own," and yet

> "while both are dear, piety requires us to honour truth above our friends."

— *Nicomachean Ethics* I.6, 1096a16–17, trans. W. D. Ross

That is the tone of the relationship as the works show it: close, respectful, and openly critical.

## Atarneus, Assos, and Hermias

Plato died in 348/347 BCE, and Aristotle left Athens. Why is debated. The headship of the Academy went to Plato's nephew Speusippus, and he may have been disappointed. Athens was also turning against Macedon: Philip II had just destroyed Olynthus in the Chalcidice (348), and a man with Aristotle's connections had reason to feel unwelcome. And he had an invitation. The sources do not let us choose between these motives.

He went east across the Aegean to Hermias, ruler of Atarneus and Assos, a small principality on the coast of Asia Minor at the edge of the Persian empire. The hostile tradition calls Hermias a eunuch and a former slave of a banker whose power he inherited. The geographer Strabo (*Geography* XIII.1.57, written around the turn of the era) adds that he had studied in Athens and invited both Aristotle and Xenocrates, another senior Academic, to his court. Two Academics from nearby Scepsis, Erastus and Coriscus, belonged to the same circle (the *Sixth Letter* in the Platonic corpus, of disputed authenticity, is addressed to them and Hermias together). Aristotle married Pythias, whom Strabo calls Hermias' niece and Diogenes his daughter or niece. Diogenes says he stayed three years.

The end was violent. Around 341 BCE Hermias was lured to a meeting by a Greek general in Persian service (Strabo names Memnon of Rhodes; other ancient sources his brother Mentor), arrested, sent to the Persian king, and executed. Aristotle wrote a hymn to Virtue in his memory, which Diogenes preserves, and an epigram for his statue at Delphi. Both would be used against him twenty years later.

## Lesbos, Theophrastus, and the biology

In 345/344 BCE (Diogenes gives the archon year), Aristotle crossed to Mytilene on the island of Lesbos. By then he was working with Theophrastus of Eresus, a younger man from Lesbos who would become his closest colleague and his successor.

This is where the biology comes in. The zoological works, above all the *History of Animals*, are full of observations from the eastern Aegean, and they name the lagoon of Pyrrha on Lesbos several times: its sea-urchins, the starfish fishermen call a pest there, the fish that leave the lagoon in winter (*History of Animals* V, VIII and IX). Scholars have used such place names to argue that much of the fieldwork was done on Lesbos and the nearby coast in the 340s; H. D. P. Lee made the case in detail in 1948, and the biologist Armand Marie Leroi's *The Lagoon* (2014) retells it vividly. It is a strong inference, not a record: others argue the biology was written up much later, in the Lyceum, from notes and reports. Either way, the attitude behind it is on record:

> "We therefore must not recoil with childish aversion from the examination of the humbler animals. Every realm of nature is marvellous"

— *Parts of Animals* I.5, 645a15–17, trans. William Ogle

## Tutor to Alexander at Mieza

In 343/342 BCE Philip II called Aristotle to Macedon to teach his son Alexander, then thirteen; Diogenes dates the move to the archonship of Pythodotus. Plutarch, writing his *Life of Alexander* in the early second century CE, adds the place: Philip gave teacher and pupil the sanctuary of the Nymphs near Mieza as "a school-house and dwelling" (trans. George Long), and in Plutarch's day visitors were still shown Aristotle's stone seats and shady walks. Plutarch also says Philip rebuilt Stagira, which he had destroyed, as Aristotle's fee; Diogenes has Aristotle persuade Alexander to restore it later. Some restoration is likely; who ordered it is uncertain.

What Aristotle taught the prince is mostly unknown. The story that Alexander carried Aristotle's edition of the *Iliad*, "the casket copy," on campaign and slept with it under his pillow is told by Plutarch in a passage citing Onesicritus, a companion of Alexander given to embroidery. The letters in which Alexander scolds Aristotle for publishing secret teachings are almost certainly later inventions. The picture of the philosopher shaping the conqueror rests on very little. The tutorship ended around 340, when the sixteen-year-old Alexander was made regent; where Aristotle spent the next five years, in Macedon or at Stagira, is not recorded. One lasting tie began at court: with Antipater, Philip's senior general, who later governed Macedon and Greece while Alexander was in Asia. Antipater is the first name in Aristotle's will.

## The Lyceum and the Peripatos

In 335/334 BCE, the year after Alexander became king, Aristotle returned to Athens and began teaching at the Lyceum. The Lyceum was a public gymnasium, an exercise ground with covered walks, outside the city walls to the east, next to a sanctuary of Apollo Lykeios. As a metic, a resident foreigner, Aristotle could not own land in Athens; the school's garden and buildings were acquired later by Theophrastus. Remains identified as the Lyceum gymnasium were found in central Athens in 1996.

The school's members were called Peripatetics. Diogenes offers two explanations: Aristotle's habit of walking up and down (*peripatein*) as he taught, or walks he took with the convalescent Alexander. Most modern scholars derive the name from the *peripatos*, the covered walkway where the school met.

The next twelve or thirteen years were his most productive. Most of the surviving treatises took their final shape now. The Lyceum also ran collective research on a scale no Greek school had tried: its members compiled the constitutions of 158 Greek states (the *Constitution of the Athenians*, recovered on papyrus in 1890, is the one that survives), records of dramatic festivals, and a list of the victors at the Pythian games. That last project is confirmed by an inscription found at Delphi honouring Aristotle and his great-nephew Callisthenes for compiling it, one of the few documentary records of his life.

Callisthenes also belongs to the darker story. Aristotle had recommended him to Alexander as the expedition's historian. Around 327 BCE Callisthenes refused to prostrate himself before Alexander in the Persian manner, was implicated in a conspiracy, and died in custody. Diogenes says this cost Aristotle the king's favour. The tale that Aristotle supplied the poison that killed Alexander is a hostile invention.

His private life in these years is visible mainly through the will. Pythias died, leaving a daughter, also named Pythias. His later companion was Herpyllis, apparently from Stagira; Diogenes calls her his concubine, and whether they married is unknown. She was the mother of his son Nicomachus, after whom the *Nicomachean Ethics* is probably named.

## Flight to Chalcis and death

Alexander died at Babylon in June 323 BCE. Athens and its allies rose against Macedon in the Lamian War, and anyone close to Macedon was exposed. Diogenes reports that Aristotle was indicted for impiety by Eurymedon, the hierophant (high priest) of the Eleusinian Mysteries, or, according to Favorinus, by one Demophilus, on the ground that the hymn to Hermias honoured a mortal like a god. The charge fits the political moment, but every detail of it comes through late sources.

Aristotle withdrew to Chalcis, where his mother's family had property and a Macedonian garrison offered safety. The most famous line of his life belongs here. Aelian (*Varia Historia* III.36) reports that, asked why he left Athens, he said he would not let the Athenians sin twice against philosophy, remembering Socrates. It is a perfect line, which is a reason for caution: it is reported only in late sources, and the dossier prints it as tradition.

He died at Chalcis in 322 BCE, in his sixty-third year, within a year of leaving Athens. Diogenes, following Apollodorus, says he died a natural death, and rejects the historian Eumelus' story that he drank aconite. Demosthenes, Macedon's great Athenian enemy, died the same year.

## The will

The will is the most personal document we have. Diogenes copies it in full (*Lives* V.11–16), and most scholars accept it as substantially authentic, though some have doubted parts. It opens with a soldier's caution:

> "All will be well; but, in case anything should happen, Aristotle has made these dispositions."

— Diogenes Laertius, *Lives* V.11, trans. R. D. Hicks

Antipater is named general executor. Until Nicanor can arrive, a group of friends, with Theophrastus among them if he is willing, is to look after Herpyllis, the children and the property. Nicanor is to marry Aristotle's daughter when she comes of age and to care for her and the boy Nicomachus "as if he were father and brother." Herpyllis is to receive money, servants, and her choice of a house: the one at Chalcis or his father's house at Stagira. Several slaves are to be freed, and none of the servants who looked after him sold. And one line looks back to his first wife:

> "wherever they bury me, there the bones of Pythias shall be laid, in accordance with her own instructions."

— Diogenes Laertius, *Lives* V.16, trans. R. D. Hicks

The will says nothing about books or the school. According to Strabo, Aristotle left his library to Theophrastus, who led the Peripatos until about 287 BCE.

## How the corpus survived

### The lost exoteric works

Aristotle wrote two kinds of work. The first were finished works for an educated public: dialogues, and the *Protrepticus*. Aristotle himself refers to "the discussions outside our school" (*Nicomachean Ethics* I.13, 1102a26–27, trans. Ross), and "exoteric" (outward-facing) is the ancient name for this kind. These were the works antiquity knew him by: Cicero praised their style as a "golden stream" of eloquence (see the [library entry](../../eras/01-ancient/aristotle.md)), which no one says of the treatises. Diogenes' catalogue lists about 150 titles, among them *On Justice*, the *Eudemus* (a dialogue on the soul), *On Philosophy* and the *Exhortation*, totalling 445,270 lines. Every exoteric work is lost. Fragments quoted by later writers were collected by Valentin Rose in the nineteenth century, and much of the *Protrepticus* can be reconstructed because the Neoplatonist Iamblichus (c. 245–c. 325 CE) copied from it into his own *Protrepticus*, as Ingram Bywater showed in 1869.

The second kind were the school treatises: lecture notes, revised many times, for use inside the Lyceum. These are what survive. Their compressed, repetitive, sometimes abrupt style is the voice of a teacher's files, which is why reading Aristotle feels so different from reading Plato.

### The books in the cellar at Scepsis

How the treatises survived is told in a dramatic ancient story that modern scholars contest. Strabo (*Geography* XIII.1.54) and Plutarch (*Life of Sulla* 26) say that Theophrastus left the library, Aristotle's books included, to Neleus of Scepsis, a pupil of both. Neleus took it home to Scepsis in the Troad, and his heirs, "ordinary people," kept it badly. When the kings of Pergamum went hunting for books for their own library, the heirs "hid their books underground in a kind of trench" (Strabo, trans. H. L. Jones). There the books lay, damaged by damp and moths, until they were sold to the collector Apellicon of Teos around 100 BCE, who recopied them and filled the gaps badly. When Sulla took Athens in 86 BCE he carried Apellicon's library to Rome. There the grammarian Tyrannion worked on it, and, in Plutarch's version, Andronicus of Rhodes got copies from him, published the works, and "drew up the lists now current" (Plutarch, trans. Bernadotte Perrin). Strabo's moral is that the Peripatetics after Theophrastus, lacking the treatises, could only "talk bombast about commonplace propositions."

Scholars accept parts of this and doubt the rest. That one set of books travelled from Theophrastus to Scepsis to Rome may well be true. What is doubted is that it was the only copy and that the treatises vanished for two centuries. Athenaeus, another ancient writer, says that Ptolemy II of Egypt bought Neleus' books for the library at Alexandria; Hellenistic writers seem to know some treatises; and Diogenes' catalogue probably goes back to a Hellenistic list made long before Sulla. A common view, argued for example by Jonathan Barnes, is that the cellar story exaggerates: some treatises were always available, but little read until the first-century edition made them the center of Aristotle studies.

### Andronicus of Rhodes

That edition is the real turning point. Andronicus of Rhodes, a Peripatetic of the first century BCE (his exact date is disputed), collected the treatises, grouped them by subject, and set them in the order we still use, from the logical works to ethics, politics, rhetoric and poetics. Porphyry, in the third century CE, reports that Andronicus divided Aristotle's works into groups of related treatises. Some of our titles and book divisions may be his; the *Metaphysics*, "the things after the physics," may be named for its place in his order. Our Aristotle is partly Andronicus' Aristotle. From his edition came the Greek commentaries, from Alexander of Aphrodisias (c. 200 CE) to the Neoplatonists, and through them the Syriac, Arabic and Latin traditions. The oldest surviving manuscripts are Byzantine, from the ninth and tenth centuries.

### Bekker's Berlin edition, 1831

The modern text begins with Immanuel Bekker's edition for the Prussian Academy of Sciences in Berlin: two volumes of Greek text in 1831, followed by Latin translations (1831), ancient scholia edited by Christian August Brandis (1836), and a final volume (1870) with Rose's fragments and Hermann Bonitz's *Index Aristotelicus*. Its lasting gift is a reference system. A Bekker number gives the page, column and line of this edition: *Nicomachean Ethics* 1106b36 means page 1106, column b, line 36. Serious editions and translations print these numbers in the margin, so a reference works in any language. That is why this dossier cites Aristotle by Bekker number, as the Plato dossier cites Plato by Stephanus page.
