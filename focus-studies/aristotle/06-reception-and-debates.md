# Aristotle Received — "The Philosopher," the Rebellion Against Him, and the Fights Still Open

*Focus study: Aristotle · [Dossier home](README.md) · [Library entry](../../eras/01-ancient/aristotle.md)*

Aristotle's reception has an unusual shape: near-disappearance, then an authority so complete that medieval writers called him simply "the Philosopher," then a rebellion that made his name a byword for what modern science had to overthrow, then a scholarly recovery that has made him, in ethics and philosophy of mind, a living participant again. This file tells that story by era, states seven live controversies at their current state, and closes with the standard misconceptions in full form, the long version of the library entry's "Misreadings to avoid," and the myths about the life.

## Antiquity: the eclipse and the commentators

The Lyceum outlived its founder under Theophrastus and Strato, but in the Hellenistic centuries after 300 BCE it lost ground to the Stoics, Epicureans and skeptics, and the treatises we now read seem to have circulated narrowly. What educated readers knew as "Aristotle" were his published dialogues, the works Cicero admired, now lost.

An ancient story explains the eclipse. Strabo and Plutarch report that the treatises passed to Neleus of Scepsis, whose heirs hid them in a cellar from the book-collecting kings of Pergamum; that Apellicon of Teos later bought them, damaged; and that the Roman general Sulla seized Apellicon's library in 86 BCE and carried it to Rome, where Tyrannio arranged the texts and Andronicus of Rhodes published them. Plutarch says the library held "most of the treatises of Aristotle and Theophrastus, at that time not yet well known to the public" (*Sulla* 26, Perrin's translation). Treat the cellar as a good story, not settled history: scholars doubt the school had no other copies. The secure result is that the Aristotle we have is Andronicus' edition, lecture treatises grouped under editors' titles like *Metaphysics*, "the things after the *Physics*."

That edition made the age of commentary possible. Alexander of Aphrodisias, who held an official chair of Aristotelian philosophy around 200 CE, became the model commentator. The [late Neoplatonists](../../eras/01-ancient/late-neoplatonists.md), heirs of [Plotinus](../../eras/01-ancient/plotinus.md), then built Aristotle into their curriculum as the first step on a ladder whose top was Plato. Porphyry's *Isagoge*, an introduction to the *Categories*, became the West's first logic textbook. John Philoponus, a sixth-century Christian, broke ranks by attacking the eternity of the world and Aristotle's account of thrown objects, arguments that mattered again a thousand years later. The commentators' texts, gathered in the Berlin *Commentaria in Aristotelem Graeca* (1882–1909), are still the first stop for a hard passage.

## The medieval Aristotle: Baghdad, Córdoba, Paris

For centuries the Latin West had only a sliver of him. [Boethius](../../eras/02-medieval/boethius.md), in the early sixth century, translated the *Categories* and *On Interpretation*; with Porphyry they formed the "old logic" (*logica vetus*) on which early medieval schooling, and debates like [Abelard](../../eras/02-medieval/peter-abelard.md)'s on universals, were built.

The Arabic-speaking world received far more, far earlier. The Baghdad translation movement of the ninth and tenth centuries rendered almost the whole corpus into Arabic, often by way of Syriac. Al-Fārābī became "the Second Teacher," after Aristotle the first. [Avicenna](../../eras/02-medieval/avicenna.md) (980–1037) rebuilt Aristotelianism into a system of his own. [Averroes](../../eras/02-medieval/averroes.md) (1126–1198), a judge in Córdoba, went back to the text, and his commentaries tried to recover what Aristotle meant beneath the Neoplatonic overlay; the Latins called him simply "the Commentator." [Maimonides](../../eras/02-medieval/maimonides.md) built Aristotle into Jewish thought, while [al-Ghazālī](../../eras/02-medieval/al-ghazali.md) attacked the philosophers' doctrines, the eternity of the world among them.

In the twelfth and thirteenth centuries the Latin West caught up in a rush, through translations from Greek (James of Venice, later William of Moerbeke, whom [Thomas Aquinas](../../eras/02-medieval/thomas-aquinas.md) used) and from Arabic, including Averroes' commentaries. The new natural philosophy alarmed the authorities: Paris banned lecturing on it in 1210 and 1215, yet by 1255 the Arts Faculty prescribed nearly the whole corpus. The fight moved inside the university. Arts masters around Siger of Brabant, later called "Latin Averroists," taught Aristotle's conclusions where they conflicted with faith; Aquinas answered them in *On the Unity of the Intellect* (1270); and in 1277 the Bishop of Paris condemned 219 propositions, many Aristotelian. Aquinas's synthesis survived to become, centuries later, the official philosophy of Catholic teaching, while [Duns Scotus](../../eras/02-medieval/john-duns-scotus.md) and [Ockham](../../eras/02-medieval/william-of-ockham.md) took the toolkit in new directions. Dante, placing him in Limbo, called him the master of those who know (*Inferno* IV.131).

## The Renaissance and the early-modern rejection

Aristotle did not fall in the Renaissance; he multiplied. The Aldine press in Venice printed the first Greek edition in 1495–98, and Padua produced the most sophisticated Aristotelianism ever written: Pietro Pomponazzi's *On the Immortality of the Soul* (1516) argued that on Aristotle's principles the soul cannot be shown to survive the body. The [humanists](../../eras/03-early-modern/renaissance-humanists.md) objected mainly to scholastic Latin and to Aristotle-as-authority; Petrarch mocked the schools' Aristotle-worship.

The real break came with the new science. Galileo's *Dialogue* (1632) gave the Aristotelian position to a character named Simplicio. [Francis Bacon](../../eras/03-early-modern/francis-bacon.md) titled his program the *New Organon* (1620), a challenge to the "Organon," the name of the logical works. [Descartes](../../eras/03-early-modern/rene-descartes.md) built a mechanical physics with no room for "substantial forms" or final causes, and [Hobbes](../../eras/03-early-modern/thomas-hobbes.md)'s *Leviathan* (1651) dismissed the schools' Aristotle as vain philosophy. Molière's doctor, who explains opium's power to cause sleep by its "dormitive virtue," became the standing caricature of explanation by forms. The target was the scholastic textbook Aristotle more than the Greek text, and the rejection was not complete: [Leibniz](../../eras/03-early-modern/gottfried-wilhelm-leibniz.md) rehabilitated substantial forms in metaphysics, and the ethics, politics, rhetoric and *Poetics* stayed in the curriculum. But in natural philosophy, the field he founded, his name came to mark the wrong turn.

## The nineteenth century: the philological recovery

The modern scholarly Aristotle begins in Berlin. Immanuel Bekker's edition of the Greek text for the Prussian Academy (1831) gave every later reader a common reference system of page, column and line, as in *NE* II.6, 1106b36: the "Bekker numbers" used throughout this dossier. Bonitz's *Index Aristotelicus* (1870) and the commentators' texts followed, and in 1890 an Egyptian papyrus restored the lost *Constitution of the Athenians*. [Hegel](../../eras/04-19th-century/g-w-f-hegel.md)'s lectures treated Aristotle as one of the deepest speculative minds, not a fallen authority, and Adolf Trendelenburg taught him as an alternative to German idealism. [Franz Brentano](../../eras/04-19th-century/franz-brentano.md)'s dissertation *On the Several Senses of Being in Aristotle* (1862) carried that interest forward; the young Heidegger later said the book set his course. The Oxford translation, edited by J. A. Smith and W. D. Ross from 1908, made English the scholarship's second home; Jonathan Barnes's revision of it is this dossier's reference set.

## The twentieth century: analytic and continental revivals

Two revivals ran side by side and rarely cited each other. On the continental side, [Heidegger](../../eras/05-20th-century/martin-heidegger.md)'s lectures of the 1920s read the *Ethics*, *Physics* and *Rhetoric* as sources for his analysis of human existence. [Gadamer](../../eras/05-20th-century/hans-georg-gadamer.md)'s *Truth and Method* (1960) made *phronēsis*, practical wisdom, the model for interpretation itself: understanding as applying the general to this case, not following a rule. [Hannah Arendt](../../eras/05-20th-century/hannah-arendt.md)'s *The Human Condition* (1958) took from Aristotle the distinction between making and acting.

On the analytic side, Oxford in the 1950s and 1960s treated Aristotle as a colleague. [Ryle](../../eras/05-20th-century/gilbert-ryle.md) and [Austin](../../eras/05-20th-century/austin-and-strawson.md) taught him, G. E. L. Owen argued with him line by line, and J. L. Ackrill's *Categories and De Interpretatione* (1963) launched the Clarendon Aristotle series of translations with philosophical commentary. Philosophers of mind read the *De Anima* as anticipating functionalism, the view that mental states are defined by what they do rather than what they are made of. Hilary Putnam and Martha Nussbaum defended that reading and Myles Burnyeat attacked it, both in *Essays on Aristotle's De Anima* (1992). The largest revival, in ethics, gets its own debate below.

## Live debates

**(1) Jaeger's developmental thesis and its critics.** Werner Jaeger's *Aristoteles* (1923) told the first full story of Aristotle's mind: a young Platonist in the Academy, a middle period of critical distance after 347 BCE, and a mature empirical researcher at the Lyceum who had left metaphysics behind for data. On this view the treatises could be dated by how Platonic they sounded. The thesis dominated a generation and then came apart. Its method was circular, since "more Platonic" was both the evidence for an early date and the conclusion drawn from it; François Nuyens applied it to the psychology and got a different order; Ingemar Düring (1957, 1966) undercut its biographical frame; and G. E. L. Owen's "The Platonism of Aristotle" (1965) argued that the movement may have run the other way, from a young critic of Plato to a mature thinker returning to Platonic questions. The current state: almost no one defends Jaeger's grand narrative, but almost everyone accepts that the corpus is layered, revised over years, with real local development in a doctrine here or a book there.

**(2) Eudaimonia: dominant or inclusive?** What does *eudaimonia* (flourishing) consist in? W. F. R. Hardie (1965) named the two options. On a **dominant** reading, eudaimonia is one supreme activity, and Book X says which: contemplation (*theōria*), the study of unchanging truths, which Aristotle calls "the complete happiness of man, if it be allowed a complete term of life" (*NE* X.7, 1177b24–25, Ross). On an **inclusive** reading, eudaimonia is a whole life containing every intrinsic good, including the ethical virtues, friendship and honor. J. L. Ackrill's "Aristotle on Eudaimonia" (1974) made the classic inclusive case from Book I's claim that happiness is "most desirable of all things, without being counted as one good thing among others" (*NE* I.7, 1097b16–17, Ross): if adding any good could improve it, it would not be final, so it must already contain them all. Richard Kraut's *Aristotle on the Human Good* (1989) read that passage more narrowly and gave the strongest modern dominant reading, on which Aristotle ranks two lives: perfect happiness is contemplation, and the ethically virtuous life is happy "in a secondary degree" (*NE* X.8, 1178a9, Ross). John Cooper, David Keyt and Gabriel Richardson Lear hold positions in between, and some scholars think Book X simply sits uneasily with Books I–IX. The current state: most readers accept that contemplation has a special priority while ethical activity is still a real form of happiness; the fight is over how that ranking works. The [study guide](../../study/aristotle-nicomachean-ethics.md) presents the same tension (§2(c), §4.7) and also leaves it open.

**(3) *De Anima* III.5 and the active intellect.** In a chapter of about a dozen lines, Aristotle distinguishes the mind that becomes all things, by taking on the forms it thinks, from "another which is what it is by virtue of making all things: this is a sort of positive state like light" (*DA* III.5, 430a14–15, J. A. Smith). This second mind, he says, is separable and unmixed: "When mind is set free from its present conditions it appears as just what it is and nothing more: this alone is immortal and eternal" (430a22–23). The phrase "active" or "agent intellect" (*nous poiētikos*) is the commentators' label, not his. Every era has fought over it. Alexander of Aphrodisias identified the making mind with God, the unmoved mover. Avicenna made it a separate cosmic intelligence that gives forms to the world below the moon. Averroes argued that even the receptive intellect is one for all humanity, the view the Latins called monopsychism. Aquinas insisted that each person has an agent intellect of their own, a power of the individual soul. Modern scholarship divides along the same lines. Victor Caston's "Aristotle's Two Intellects: A Modest Proposal" (1999) and Myles Burnyeat's *Aristotle's Divine Intellect* (2008) revived the divine reading, while Michael Wedin and others read the making mind as part of the human cognitive system. The current state: no consensus, since the text is too compressed to settle it, which is why [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md) flags its reconstruction as disputed.

**(4) Is form universal or particular?** *Metaphysics* Z argues that the primary substance of a thing is its form (*eidos*), and then seems to box itself in. Z.13 says "it seems impossible that any universal term should be the name of a substance" (1038b8–9, Ross), since a thing's substance is peculiar to it. Yet Z.8 says that Callias and Socrates are "different in virtue of their matter (for that is different), but the same in form; for their form is indivisible" (1034a6–8, Ross). If form is shared, it looks like a universal; if it is a substance, it cannot be. Defenders of **particular forms**, notably Michael Frede and Günther Patzig in their commentary on Book Z (1988), hold that each individual has its own form: Socrates' soul and Callias' are alike in kind but numerically two. Defenders of **species forms**, Michael Loux among them, hold that the form is one for the whole kind, and argue it can be a substance without being a universal in the forbidden sense, for instance because it is predicated only of matter. The current state: open since Rogers Albritton and Wilfrid Sellars framed it in 1957, and a standard test for any reading of Book Z.

**(5) Teleology after Darwin.** Teleology, explanation by the end something serves, seemed finished off by Darwin. The record is more interesting. In *Physics* II.8 Aristotle states, in order to refute it, a theory close to selection: "Wherever then all the parts came about just what they would have been if they had come be for an end, such things survived, being organized spontaneously in a fitting way" (198b29–31, Hardie & Gaye). Darwin later cited the passage in the historical sketch added to the *Origin* as natural selection faintly foreshadowed, and in 1882, after reading William Ogle's translation of *Parts of Animals*, he wrote that Linnaeus and Cuvier "have been my two Gods, though in very different way, but they were mere school-boys to old Aristotle" (letter to Ogle, 22 February 1882). Current scholarship asks three questions. What kind of teleology was it? Not a designer's intention, since nature acts for ends without deliberating; on Allan Gotthelf's influential reading, an irreducible potential for form in living things. How far does it reach? Whether rain falls for the sake of the crops is a long dispute, with David Furley reading Aristotle's rain example as non-teleological and David Sedley arguing for a broader, world-ordering teleology. Does any of it survive? Biologists still say "the heart is for pumping blood," and philosophers analyze such talk either as what a trait was selected for or, with neo-Aristotelians like Michael Thompson and [Philippa Foot](../../eras/05-20th-century/anscombe-and-foot.md), as a description of how a life-form goes on. The current state: the cosmic teleology is dead; the biological teleology is a live model in philosophy of biology.

**(6) Natural slavery and women in the *Politics*.** These are not passing remarks, and this dossier does not soften them. *Politics* I.4–7 argues that some human beings are slaves by nature: "from the hour of their birth, some are marked out for subjection, others for rule" (*Pol.* I.5, 1254a23–24, Jowett). *Politics* I.13 grades the souls of the household: "the slave has no deliberative faculty at all; the woman has, but it is without authority" (1260a12–13, Jowett). The biology adds that the female is in some respects an incomplete or deformed male (*Generation of Animals* II.3). Scholars treat these texts in four main ways. Some **excise** them, holding that the theory of virtue and flourishing does not depend on them. Some **diagnose** them: Malcolm Schofield (1990) read natural slavery as ideology, a theory built to justify a practice, and Susan Moller Okin (1979) argued that defining people by their role in the household makes the exclusion of women structural, not incidental. Some **turn Aristotle against himself**: natural slaves must be identifiable by nature, yet he concedes that "Nature would like to distinguish between the bodies of freemen and slaves" (1254b27–28), that the opposite often happens, and that this is "what nature, though she may intend it, cannot always accomplish" (I.6, 1255b3–4). On his own terms the slavery of his day was largely unjust. And feminist scholars (for instance in *Feminist Interpretations of Aristotle*, ed. Cynthia Freeland, 1998) ask whether his metaphysics of form and matter really requires the gendered roles he gives them. The current state: almost no one defends the doctrines; the live question is how deep they go. [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md) reconstructs the natural-slavery argument and then criticizes it.

**(7) The virtue-ethics revival.** [Elizabeth Anscombe](../../eras/05-20th-century/anscombe-and-foot.md)'s "Modern Moral Philosophy" (1958) argued that the concept of moral obligation made sense only within a divine-law ethics, and pointed back to Aristotle's virtues. Philippa Foot's *Natural Goodness* (2001) treated human goodness as a case of the goodness of any living thing. [Alasdair MacIntyre](../../eras/05-20th-century/alasdair-macintyre.md)'s *After Virtue* (1981) argued that modern moral language is the debris of a lost Aristotelian framework. [Martha Nussbaum](../../eras/05-20th-century/martha-nussbaum.md)'s *The Fragility of Goodness* (1986) took his exposure to luck seriously, and her capabilities approach, developed with Amartya Sen, turned his account of human functioning into a tool of development economics. Rosalind Hursthouse's *On Virtue Ethics* (1999) gave the view its standard form, and [Bernard Williams](../../eras/05-20th-century/bernard-williams.md) asked whether ethics could be grounded in human nature at all. The current state: virtue ethics is one of the three standard normative theories, beside consequentialism and deontology. Its live problems are Aristotelian ones: whether "act as the virtuous person would" gives guidance or only restates the question; whether social psychology's situationist experiments show stable character traits to be rarer than the theory needs; and whether a neo-Aristotelian naturalism can do without the teleology just discussed.

## Misconceptions, in full form

- **"Eudaimonia means feeling happy."** It names a whole life going well, defined as activity, "activity of soul in accordance with virtue" in "a complete life" (*NE* I.7, 1098a16–18, Ross), not as a feeling. A cheerful person can fail to be *eudaimōn*, and a grieving one can be; that is why Priam, a good man ruined in old age, is Aristotle's test case for luck. Pleasure completes unimpeded activity, Book X says, but it is a mark of flourishing, not its definition. Book X's praise of contemplation is a claim about which activity is most complete, not about which feels nicest.

- **"The mean means moderation in everything."** The mean is the right response, felt "at the right times, with reference to the right objects, towards the right people, with the right motive, and in the right way" (*NE* II.6, 1106b21–22, Ross), relative to the person and the situation. Sometimes the right amount of anger is a great deal, and some actions, adultery, theft and murder among them, have no mean at all. Each virtue is a trained disposition to get feeling and action right, and getting it right takes *phronēsis*, practical wisdom.

- **"The unmoved mover is the Christian God."** Aristotle's first mover creates nothing, since the world is eternal; it moves the heavens as an object of desire; and its activity is "a thinking on thinking" (*Met.* Λ.9, 1074b34–35, Ross), with no care for individuals. Christian, Jewish and Islamic theologians used the argument only by adding creation, knowledge of particulars and providence, knowing they were adapting it.

- **"Aristotle was an empiricist, Plato a rationalist, full stop."** Aristotle starts from what appears: observations, dissections, and *endoxa*, the reputable opinions of the many and the wise. But knowledge ends for him in first principles grasped by *nous*, intellect (*Posterior Analytics* II.19), and in forms and ends no sense perceives as such. The pair in Raphael's *School of Athens*, Plato pointing up and Aristotle's hand held level, is a rough map. They disagreed over where forms are, not over whether reason matters.

- **"His books are finished books."** What survives are lecture treatises and research notes, edited centuries later, with titles he did not give; the polished dialogues are lost. Repetitions, abrupt transitions, doublets (*Metaphysics* K repeats earlier material) and apparent contradictions partly reflect that origin. Reading each sentence as his final word flattens the layering the developmental debate is about.

- **"Aristotle blocked science for two thousand years."** He founded formal logic and systematic biology, and his method was to gather data and work through the puzzles, the *aporiai*, before deciding. His physics was wrong on falling bodies and the heavens. But what the seventeenth century fought was a university Aristotelianism that had turned his conclusions into authority, and "everything in nature has a purpose, like rain falling for the crops" is that tradition's caricature: his teleology is mainly biological, and its reach to the weather is disputed.

## Myths about the life

Most of the colorful stories come from Diogenes Laertius, writing perhaps five centuries after Aristotle's death, and from late Neoplatonic *Lives*; treat each as legend unless something earlier supports it.

- **The nicknames "the Mind" and "the Reader,"** repeated in the library entry, come from late sources that have Plato coin them: anecdote, not record.
- **Plato's complaint that "Aristotle spurns me, as colts kick out at the mother who bore them"** is given by Diogenes (V.2, Hicks's translation) only as a remark "attributed" to Plato, in a passage that also claims Aristotle left the Academy while Plato was alive. The standard account has him stay until Plato's death in 347 BCE.
- **The parting line that Athens should not sin twice against philosophy,** said on leaving for Chalcis, is a late attribution: a good line about Socrates' execution, of uncertain authorship.
- **Alexander bankrolled his biology.** Athenaeus' 800 talents and Pliny's thousands of hunters and fishermen gathering specimens are late and implausible; much of the marine work was done earlier, on Lesbos.
- **Death by poison.** Diogenes records Eumelus' claim that Aristotle drank aconite at Chalcis, a report that also puts his age at seventy rather than about sixty-two. Most historians accept death from illness in 322 BCE.
- **Phyllis riding Aristotle.** The medieval tale of the philosopher who scolds Alexander about his mistress and is then saddled and ridden by her is thirteenth-century fiction, a joke about wisdom undone by desire, with no ancient basis.
