# Study Plan — Aristotle in Nine Weeks
**English** | [简体中文](../../zh/focus-studies/aristotle/07-study-plan.md)

## What this plan is, and where it plugs in

The [learning path](../../learning-path.md) meets Aristotle in **Stage 1 (Ancient Foundations)**, as a core figure (●) at row 4, straight after Plato. The path's block is selective by design: *Nicomachean Ethics* I–II, III in selection, VII in selection, VIII and X in selection, then *Politics* I, *Metaphysics* VII and XII in selection (Z and Λ in the Greek-letter numbering this dossier uses), *De Anima* II–III in selection, and *Categories* 1–5 as a summary pass. Its two warnings stand here too: not Ross as a first read, and do not start with the *Physics* or the full *Metaphysics*. This dossier keeps the path's editions and its texts, and expands the block to nine weeks. It reads the corpus in a deliberate order, logic first and the ethics late: the logic and the *Categories* give the vocabulary, the *Physics* gives the four causes and the definition of change, the biology shows those tools at work on real animals, the *De Anima* defines the soul as the form of those animals, and the *Metaphysics* asks what all of it presupposes. Only then do the *Ethics*, the *Politics*, the *Rhetoric* and the *Poetics* apply the system to human life. [02-works-and-texts.md](02-works-and-texts.md), "The reading order," gives the reasons work by work.

Run it as one block in place of Stage 1's Aristotle row, after Plato (row 3) and before Epicurus (row 5). The order is load-bearing: Aristotle's critique of the Forms reads as a reply only if Plato came first, and if you ran the [Plato dossier](../plato/README.md), its last week ends on the Third Man and on Aristotle's objection in seed, which is where Week 5 here picks the thread up.

**Total cost, stated honestly: ≈55 hours all-in across nine weeks — ≈36.5 hours at the desk, ≈10 hours of assigned media, and ≈8 hours of the daily [Anki](https://apps.ankiweb.net) [FSRS](https://docs.ankiweb.net/deck-options.html) queue (about 8 minutes a day for 63 days).** Each week below states its own three numbers. The weekly totals run from about 5 to 6.5 hours, with Week 9 at about 8.5 because the capstone essay is written there. Optional items (the Yale lectures, extra reading marked *optional*) are not in the total.

**The accounting against the path, stated plainly: these hours sit on top of Stage 1's budget, they are not hidden inside it.** The path does not fix a week count for its Aristotle row. At Stage 1's rate (60 desk hours over 13 weeks, about 4.6 a week) its selective block reads as roughly two weeks, about 10 desk hours; that is this dossier's estimate, not the path's figure. Adamson's Aristotle episodes are already in the path's Stage 1 audio spine (eps. 1–51 complete), so they are not new cost. Count a net addition of roughly 40 hours to Stage 1 if you run the dossier as written, again an estimate. The path's 12-month trim (§10), which halves the Stage 1 Adamson selection, is incompatible with this dossier; choose one. If you are also running the Plato dossier, the two together add roughly 65 hours to the stage.

**Reading texts.** Follow the path's rulings and [08-bibliography.md](08-bibliography.md), tier 1. The *NE* in Terence Irwin (Hackett, 3rd ed., 2019) or C. D. C. Reeve (Hackett), pick one and do not alternate — Weeks 6–7. Everything else from the Hackett *Readings in Ancient Greek Philosophy*, ed. Cohen, Curd & Reeve (5th ed., 2016), where it prints the passage, and otherwise from Reeve's New Hackett Aristotle: *Physics* (2018), *Generation of Animals & History of Animals I, Parts of Animals I* (2019), *De Anima* (2017), *Metaphysics* (2016), *Politics* (2017), *Rhetoric* (2018). The *Poetics* in Richard Janko's annotated translation (Hackett, 1987), the dossier's ruling where the path is silent. Barnes's *Complete Works* (Revised Oxford Translation) is the reference set for anything the volumes above omit, such as the later books of the *History of Animals*. [02-works-and-texts.md](02-works-and-texts.md), "Editions and translations," says which volume holds which week's passages. **Not Ross, Jowett or the other old Oxford translations as your reading copy:** the dossier quotes them only because they are public domain and can be checked word for word online.

**Cite by Bekker number, and budget by chapter.** Every edition named above prints Bekker numbers (page, column and line of the 1831 Berlin edition, e.g. *NE* II.6, 1106b36) in the margin; cite by them, never by page. The *Readings* and the Reeve volumes paginate differently, so this plan sets each week's target as books and chapters plus a session length, not as a page count. The Greek-letter names of the *Metaphysics* books are keyed in [02-works-and-texts.md](02-works-and-texts.md), "A note on citation before the works."

**The guide is your companion, by design.** The library's [study guide to the *Nicomachean Ethics*](../../study/aristotle-nicomachean-ethics.md) — book-by-book walkthroughs, three reconstructions, fifteen checkpoints with answers, the misreadings — is not duplicated here. Weeks 6–7 assign its book sections, reconstructions and checkpoints by number; the checkpoints printed in those weeks are the dossier's own additions, aimed at what the guide leaves to [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md) and [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md).

## Week 1 — Logic and categories: *Categories* 1–5, *De Interpretatione* 9, *Posterior Analytics* II.19 (~5.7 hrs: 3.5 desk + 1.3 media + 0.9 cards)

**Primary.** All three passages are in the Hackett *Readings*. *Categories* 1–5: the ten kinds of thing that can be said, the two relations "said of" and "present in," and primary substance, the individual man or horse, as the bedrock everything else depends on. Read chapter 5 slowest. *De Interpretatione* 9: the sea battle, the chapter that asks whether a true prediction makes the future necessary. It is short and has been argued over for two thousand years; read it twice. *Posterior Analytics* II.19: the last chapter of the work on scientific knowledge (*epistēmē*), which asks how we come to grasp the first principles that demonstration starts from, and answers with a climb from perception through memory and experience to *nous*, intellectual insight. *Optional:* *Prior Analytics* I.1, I.2 and I.4, the definition of the syllogism and the first figure, which [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md) singles out as the chapters that repay close reading. The plan does not assign them; Argument 4 reconstructs the system in summary.

**Alongside.** [01-life-and-times.md](01-life-and-times.md) (the life, the Academy years, and how the corpus survived: these are school treatises, not books he published) and [02-works-and-texts.md](02-works-and-texts.md) ("A note on citation before the works," and the Organon entry). Then [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md), "Substance and the ten categories," and [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Arguments 3 (the sea battle) and 4 (the syllogism).

**Media.** *Review:* Peter Adamson, [*History of Philosophy Without Any Gaps*](https://historyofphilosophy.net/podcast/classical-greek/aristotle), ep. 34, "Mr. Know It All: Aristotle's Life and Works" (with the life reading); ep. 35, "The Philosopher's Toolkit: Aristotle's Logical Works"; ep. 36, "A Principled Stand: Aristotle's Epistemology" (after *Posterior Analytics* II.19); and ep. 37, "Hugh Benson on Aristotelian Method."

**Checkpoints (book closed).**
1. *What is primary substance, and what do "said of" and "present in" do in the definition?* Species and genera are said of a subject (Edghill's translation has "predicable of"), as man is said of Socrates; qualities and the like are present in a subject and cannot exist apart from it, as a patch of white is in a body. Primary substance is what is neither said of nor present in a subject: the individual man or horse (*Cat.* 5, 2a11–14). Species and genera are substances only in a secondary sense. Then say how this inverts Plato, for whom the universal was most real. [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md), "Substance and the ten categories."
2. *Name the ten categories and give one predicate of Socrates for each.* Substance, quantity, quality, relation, place, time, position, state (also rendered having), action, affection, meaning being acted on (*Cat.* 4, 1b25–27). For Socrates: man; two cubits long; white; double; in the Lyceum; yesterday; lying down; shod; cutting; being cut. The other nine depend on the first. Same section.
3. *State the fatalist argument of De Interpretatione 9 and the three main readings of Aristotle's escape.* If one of "there will be a sea battle tomorrow" and its denial is already true, the battle looks necessary and deliberation pointless; but deliberation does make a difference, so a premise must go. The non-bivalence reading says such statements are not yet true or false; Anscombe's modal-fallacy reading says the fatalist slides from the necessity of a conditional to the necessity of its consequent; a middle reading (Boethius) says one of the pair is true but not *definitely* true. What all three keep: the truth of a prediction does not fix the future. [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 3.
4. *How, according to Posterior Analytics II.19, do we come to know first principles, and why can they not be demonstrated?* Demonstration proceeds from principles, so the principles themselves cannot all be demonstrated without a regress. We reach them from below: perception, then memory, then experience built up from many memories, then *nous*, the intellectual grasp of the universal. This is why "Aristotle the empiricist, full stop" is a misconception. [02-works-and-texts.md](02-works-and-texts.md), the Organon entry; [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md), "Misconceptions, in full form."

**Reconstructions.** The sea battle (Argument 3), first blank-page reconstruction (200–400 words); it enters the +2 days / +1 week / +3 weeks rhythm below.

**Cards.** First 4 of the deck: the argument cards for Arguments 3 (the sea battle) and 4 (the syllogism) from [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), and the concept cards *ousia* (substance) and *kategoriai* (categories) from [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md), "Key terms." After the week's reading, never before. Deck at 4.

## Week 2 — Nature: *Physics* II whole, III.1, VIII in summary (~5.9 hrs: 4 desk + 1 media + 0.9 cards)

**Primary.** *Physics* II whole (in the *Readings* or Reeve's *Physics*): nature as an inner source of change and rest (II.1), the four causes (II.3, repeated in II.7), chance (II.4–6), and teleology against Empedocles (II.8), with II.9 on necessity in nature. II.3 and II.8 are the chapters to read slowest. Then III.1, the definition of change as the actuality of the potential as such, the short chapter that answers Parmenides. *Physics* VIII in summary only: read the opening chapter's question (has motion always existed?) and the chapters the *Readings* prints (VIII.5–6), and leave the rest; the argument to an unmoved first mover returns in full with *Metaphysics* Λ in Week 5.

**Alongside.** [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md), "Matter and form," "The four causes," "Potentiality and actuality," and "Nature and teleology"; [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Arguments 5 (the four causes), 6 (teleology against Empedocles) and 7 (change). [05-conversations.md](05-conversations.md), "The Presocratics, as Aristotle tells their story," for the predecessors these chapters answer, with links to the library entries on [Empedocles](../../eras/01-ancient/empedocles.md) and [Parmenides](../../eras/01-ancient/parmenides-of-elea.md).

**Media.** *Review:* Adamson ep. 39, "Form and Function: Aristotle's Four Causes" (after II.3 and II.7); ep. 40, "Let's Get Physical: Aristotle's Natural Philosophy" (after III.1); ep. 41, "Richard Sorabji on Time and Eternity in Aristotle" (after the *Physics* VIII summary: it is the bridge to Week 5's eternal motion).

**Checkpoints (book closed).**
1. *Give all four causes of a bronze statue, and say why Aristotle thinks three of them often coincide in natural things.* Material: the bronze; formal: the shape or essence; efficient: the sculptor, or the sculptor's art; final: what it is for. In natural things the last three often coincide (*Phys.* II.7, 198a24–25): the form of a man, his end and the parent who made him are one form in different roles. *Aitia* is closer to "explanation" than to the modern "cause." [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 5.
2. *Run the teleology argument of Physics II.8, then give Darwin's objection and the best reply.* Things happen by chance or for an end; the parts of living things come about always or for the most part in the same way; chance outcomes are not regular; so the parts come about for an end. Darwin: natural selection explains regularity without ends, so the either-or is not exhaustive. Reply: the Empedoclean view Aristotle rejects has no inheritance, so it is not natural selection; and biology still describes parts by what they are for. [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 6; [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md), "Teleology after Darwin."
3. *State the definition of change and say what "in so far as" does in it.* Change is the actuality of what exists potentially, in so far as it exists potentially (*Phys.* III.1, 201a10–11). The qualifier picks out the one aspect the change actualises: not the bronze as bronze but the bronze as potentially a statue. Then name the two readings of *entelecheia* here, actualization (Ross) and actuality (Kosman), and why Kosman's avoids circularity. [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 7.
4. *What is nature, by Physics II.1's definition, and why is a thing's nature above all its form?* A source of being moved and of being at rest in the thing itself (192b21–23). A wooden bed has no inner tendency to be a bed; an acorn has its own principle of growth toward a mature form. [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md), "Nature and teleology."

**Reconstructions.** The four causes with teleology against Empedocles (Arguments 5–6, as one reconstruction); change as actuality (Argument 7). The sea battle gets its +1-week touch.

**Cards.** +6: the argument cards for Arguments 5, 6 and 7, and the concept cards matter and form (*hylē*, *morphē* / *eidos*), potentiality and actuality (*dunamis*, *energeia*, *entelecheia*) and *aitia* (cause, explanation). Deck at 10.

## Week 3 — Biology: *Parts of Animals* I, *History of Animals* and *Generation of Animals* selections (~5.1 hrs: 3 desk + 1.2 media + 0.9 cards)

**Primary.** *Parts of Animals* I, the biology's essay on method (in the *Readings* for I.1 and I.5; the whole book in Reeve's biology volume). I.1 is where the *Physics*' teleology is put to work: how an account of an animal should be ordered, and why the explanation by what a part is for comes first. I.5 is the defence of zoology against those who find animals beneath philosophy, with the story of Heraclitus inviting his visitors into the kitchen because gods were present there too. Then selections from the rest of the same volume: *History of Animals* I, the data-gathering work that collects facts on hundreds of species before explaining any of them, and the opening of *Generation of Animals* I, on reproduction explained by matter and form. Read those two for the method, not the zoology: you are watching the four causes meet real organisms. The plan reads the biology before *De Anima*, the one swap in the traditional order of the corpus, because the soul is about to be defined as the form of exactly these living bodies.

**Alongside.** [01-life-and-times.md](01-life-and-times.md), "Lesbos, Theophrastus, and the biology" (where the marine research was done); [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md), "Nature and teleology" again, now read against the organisms; [05-conversations.md](05-conversations.md), "Theophrastus"; [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md), live debates (5) "Teleology after Darwin" and (6) "Natural slavery and women in the *Politics*," which begins in the *Generation of Animals*.

**Media.** *Review:* Adamson ep. 43, "Classified Information: Aristotle's Biology." BBC Radio 4, *In Our Time*, ["Aristotle's Biology"](https://www.bbc.co.uk/programmes/m0002cfd) (7 Feb. 2019, with Armand Leroi, Myrto Hatzimichali and Sophia Connell), after your own reading; Leroi's *The Lagoon* ([08-bibliography.md](08-bibliography.md), tier 3) is the friendly book if this week catches you.

**Checkpoints (book closed).**
1. *Why does Parts of Animals I.5 tell the student to study even the humblest animals?* Because every realm of nature is marvellous: each animal, studied without distaste, shows something natural and something beautiful (*PA* I.5, 645a15–23), and the Heraclitus story makes the point that the divine is present in the kitchen too. The defence is philosophical, not sentimental: if nature is form working toward ends, the form is as present in a sponge as in a star. [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md), "Nature and teleology"; [02-works-and-texts.md](02-works-and-texts.md), the biology entry.
2. *Parts of Animals I.1 puts the final cause first. What does that mean for how you explain a part, say a tooth?* That you say what the part is for before you say how it came to be: the account of the developmental process is given in the light of the form the animal is on its way to. This is Argument 6's method put to work, and it is the reading (Gotthelf, Cooper) on which a final cause is the form a developing organism is heading toward. [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 6, "Textual basis" and "Reply."
3. *What does the Generation of Animals claim about the contribution of male and female, and how should you treat the claim?* That the female supplies the matter and the male the form. It is an argued position inside the matter-and-form theory, and the dossier names it plainly rather than skipping it: it feeds the *Politics*' ranking of souls, and it has been central to feminist criticism. [02-works-and-texts.md](02-works-and-texts.md), the biology entry; [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md), live debate (6).
4. *Why does the History of Animals come before the explanations, and what does that order tell you about Aristotle's method?* Facts first, then causes: the *History* collects what is the case about kinds of animals, and the *Parts* and *Generation* explain why. It is the *Posterior Analytics*' ideal (Week 1) in practice: knowledge is of the "why," but inquiry starts from what appears. [02-works-and-texts.md](02-works-and-texts.md), the biology entry.

**Reconstructions.** No new argument this week; this is the lighter desk week by design. Session C carries the +2-day and +1-week touches for the four causes, teleology and change instead, now with an organism of your choice as the example.

**Cards.** +2 concept cards: *phusis* (nature) and *telos* (end). Deck at 12.

## Week 4 — Soul: *De Anima* II.1–5, III.4–5 (~5.2 hrs: 4 desk + 0.3 media + 0.9 cards)

**Primary.** *De Anima* in Reeve's translation or the *Readings*. II.1, the definition of soul as the first actuality of a natural organised body, built in steps from the theory of substance: read it twice, the second time with Argument 8 open beside you. II.2–3, the powers of soul (nutrition, perception, thought, movement) and the ladder from plants to humans that the *Ethics*' function argument will reuse in Week 6. II.4, the nutritive soul; II.5, perception as a special kind of being acted on. Then III.4, the mind that is nothing actual before it thinks, and III.5, less than a page, on the mind that makes all things. Read III.5 last and slowly, and expect not to be sure what it says: no one is.

**Alongside.** [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md), "Soul as the form of the living body" and "*Nous*, the intellect"; [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Arguments 8 (soul as first actuality) and 9 (the active intellect, flagged as disputed); [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md), live debate (3) "*De Anima* III.5 and the active intellect."

**Media.** *Review:* Adamson ep. 42, "Soul Power: Aristotle's *De Anima*." This is the week's only assigned episode; ep. 47, on mind and god, waits for Week 5, where it closes the arc from III.5 to *Metaphysics* Λ.

**Checkpoints (book closed).**
1. *Build the definition of soul in De Anima II.1 from its premises.* Matter is potentiality, form actuality; a living body is a compound; the body is what has life, not what life is, so the body is matter and the soul its form; form is actuality; and of the two grades of actuality, like knowledge possessed and knowledge in use, the soul is the first, since a sleeping animal is still alive. So the soul is the first actuality of a natural body that has life potentially (412a27–28), that is, of a natural organised body. [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 8.
2. *What is Ackrill's objection, and what does the proximate/remote matter distinction do with it?* Hylomorphism works for bronze and statue because the bronze can be picked out without the statue; but the matter of a living thing, an organised body, cannot be picked out without its soul, since a dead eye is an eye only in name. The commentators' reply: the remote matter (flesh, elements) can be picked out and outlasts the animal as a corpse, while the proximate matter is alive whenever it exists, which is Aristotle's point that soul and body are not two things. Whether this solves the problem or restates it is still argued. Same section.
3. *What does perception receive, and why does that make thought a problem for the theory?* The form of its object without the matter, as wax takes a signet's shape without its gold. Thought receives forms too, but every form, so it can have no bodily nature of its own: before it thinks, it is nothing actual at all (*DA* III.4, 429a23–24). That is the one place the soul-as-form picture strains. [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md), "*Nous*, the intellect."
4. *What does De Anima III.5 say, and what are the three classic readings of what it is about?* That thinking needs a receptive mind that becomes all things and a productive mind that makes all things, like light making potential colours actual; and that the productive mind is separable, unaffected, deathless (430a10–25). Alexander: it is god. Averroes: one separate intellect shared by the human species. Aquinas: a power of each human soul. Each pays a price; state one. This is disputed, and the dossier presents it as a dispute. [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 9.

**Reconstructions.** Soul as first actuality (Argument 8). The four causes, teleology and change get their +3-week touches in Week 5; the sea battle gets its +3-week touch this week.

**Cards.** +4: the argument cards for Arguments 8 and 9, and the concept cards *psychē* (soul) and *nous* (intellect). Deck at 16.

## Week 5 — First philosophy: *Metaphysics* A.1–2, Γ.3–4, Z.1, 3, 17, Λ.6–10 (~6.4 hrs: 4.5 desk + 1 media + 0.9 cards)

**Primary.** The hardest week, and the reason the path warns against starting here. The *Readings* first, as the path rules, then Reeve's *Metaphysics* for the chapters the anthology omits, among them Γ.4, Λ.6 and Λ.8 ([02-works-and-texts.md](02-works-and-texts.md), "Editions and translations"). A.1–2: all human beings by nature desire to know; experience against craft (*technē*) against wisdom; wisdom as knowledge of first causes; philosophy begun in wonder. Γ.3–4: the principle of non-contradiction and its defence by refutation. Z.1 (being is said in many ways, and the ancient question what being is turns out to be what substance is), Z.3 (why matter cannot be substance), Z.17 (the fresh start: substance is form, the cause that makes the letters a syllable). Λ.6–10: eternal motion, a substance whose essence is actuality, the mover that moves as an object of love, thought thinking itself, and the army image of Λ.10. Read Λ.8, the astronomy of the spheres, quickly. *Optional:* *Metaphysics* A.9, Aristotle's critique of the Forms, which the plan does not assign; Argument 1 reconstructs the Third Man from its sources.

**Alongside.** [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md), "First philosophy and the unmoved mover," then "Known internal tensions," (1) universal or particular form and (2) two accounts of substance; [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Arguments 1 (the Third Man), 2 (non-contradiction), 10 (substance as form) and 11 (the unmoved mover); [05-conversations.md](05-conversations.md), "Plato and the Academy"; [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md), live debate (4) "Is form universal or particular?"

**Media.** *Review:* Adamson ep. 38, "Down to Earth: Aristotle on Substance" (after Z); ep. 47, "God Only Knows: Aristotle on Mind and God" (after Λ: it joins *De Anima* III.5 to Λ.9); ep. 50, "MM McCabe and Raphael Woolf on Aristotle on Plato" (with Argument 1, the critique of the Forms).

**Checkpoints (book closed).**
1. *Metaphysics A.1 ranks experience, craft and wisdom. On what basis?* By grasp of the "why." The merely experienced know that a remedy works; the craftsman knows why it works and can teach it; wisdom is knowledge of the first causes and principles of everything. Hence the drive that opens the book, the desire to know, ends in first philosophy. [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md), "Techne, rhetoric and mimesis" and "First philosophy and the unmoved mover."
2. *Why can the principle of non-contradiction not be proved, and how does Aristotle defend it anyway?* Every proof assumes it, and to demand proof of everything is a regress. So the defence is elenctic, by refutation: ask the opponent only to say something meaningful; a word like "man" means something by excluding its denial, so in speaking he relies on the principle he rejects. The practical rider: why does he walk to Megara and not into a well (1008b12–19)? Then state the begging-the-question objection and the dialetheist challenge. [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 2.
3. *State the Third Man in three sentences, and say what Aristotle's critique actually targets.* If the many men share a Form of Man, and the Form is itself a man, a further Form is needed over the men and the Form, and so without end. Whether Plato held self-predication in that sense is disputed (Cherniss); but the critique's real target is **separation**, the Forms existing apart (*Met.* M.4, 1078b30–32). Then give the rebound: Z's own forms face the question whether they are universal or particular. [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Arguments 1 and 10.
4. *How does the unmoved mover move the heavens without moving, and what is its life?* As an object of love moves the lover: it is a final cause, pure actuality without matter (1071b19–21, 1072b3–4). Its activity is the best there is, thinking, with the best object, itself: thought thinking itself (1074b34–35). It does not manage the world; the world is ordered toward it, as an army is toward its general (Λ.10). It is no creator. [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 11.

**Reconstructions.** Three this week, the plan's heaviest Session C: non-contradiction (Argument 2); the critique of the Forms and substance as form, as one reconstruction (Arguments 1 and 10); the unmoved mover (Argument 11). The four causes and teleology, and change, get their +3-week touches; soul as first actuality gets its +1-week touch.

**Cards.** +5: the argument cards for Arguments 1, 2, 10 and 11, and the concept card *to ti ēn einai* (essence). Deck at 21.

## Week 6 — Ethics I: *Nicomachean Ethics* I–III, with the guide (~6.1 hrs: 4 desk + 1.2 media + 0.9 cards)

**Primary.** *NE* Books I–III in Irwin or Reeve, with [the guide](../../study/aristotle-nicomachean-ethics.md) in its own rhythm: its book section read before each book as orientation and afterwards as a check, your reconstruction written book-closed before you open the guide's version, the objections last. Book I: the highest good, *eudaimonia*, and the function argument (I.7); I.3's warning that precision is not to be sought alike in all discussions. Book II: virtue of character acquired by habit, and the doctrine of the mean (II.6). Book III: voluntary action, choice (*prohairesis*), courage and temperance. This is where the path's Aristotle block starts; the dossier has delayed it eight weeks so that "function," "soul," "actuality" and "form" arrive already understood.

**The guide.** Its §1 sections on Books I, II and III; its reconstructions **(a)** (the function argument) and **(b)** (the doctrine of the mean), written book-closed before checking; its §3 **checkpoints 1–7** as this week's extended set in Session C, with the answers in its §5. Read its §4, "Common misreadings," at the end of the week.

**Alongside.** [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md), "The good life: *eudaimonia*, virtue, *phronesis*"; [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Arguments 12 (the function argument) and 13 (the doctrine of the mean), which follow the guide's reconstructions and add the literature's objections and replies; [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md), live debate (7) "The virtue-ethics revival."

**Media.** *Review:* Adamson ep. 44, "The Goldilocks Theory: Aristotle's Ethics," after Book II. BBC Radio 4, *In Our Time*, ["Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics"](https://www.bbc.co.uk/programmes/m001rylh) (2 Nov. 2023, with Angie Hobbs, Roger Crisp and Sophia Connell), after your reconstructions. *Optional, from the path:* Sadler's *Lectures* podcast on the *NE* as commute audio, not counted.

**Checkpoints (the dossier's own).**
1. *Which premise of the function argument comes from the De Anima, and what does Week 4 add to it?* Premise 3, the ladder of soul-powers: nutrition and growth (plants), perception (animals), and what remains peculiar to humans, the life of the part that has reason (1097b33–1098a4). Week 4 shows the ladder is not a list of talents but the structure of soul as the form of a living body, which is why "function" here means what a thing characteristically does, not a purpose a designer assigned. [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 12, "Textual basis" and "Reply."
2. *The doctrine of the mean is "trivial or empty": state the charge and two replies.* Trivial if "the mean" just means "the right amount"; empty if the only test is what the practically wise person would choose. Urmson's reply: the mean is a settled character that feels what each occasion calls for, sometimes a great deal, so the mean is not moderation. Aristotle's own limits: in its essence virtue is a mean, but with regard to what is best it is an extreme (1107a6–8), and some actions and feelings admit no mean at all (1107a8–9). Hursthouse's sharper objection: many virtues do not lie on one scale of more and less. [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 13.
3. *Why does I.3's warning about precision matter for the mean's circularity worry?* Because Aristotle never promised a decision procedure: ethics, for him, cannot be written as a code, so a definition that leaves room for trained judgment is accurate by his own lights rather than evasive. Whether that satisfies a reader who wants rules is the live question. [02-works-and-texts.md](02-works-and-texts.md), the ethical works entry; [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 13, "Reply."
4. *Virtue of character comes neither by nature nor against nature. What does the phrase mean, and where will you need it again?* We are fitted by nature to receive virtue and completed by habit (*NE* II.1): repeated action builds the settled state (*hexis*). You will need it in Week 8, where it is the literature's reply to the objection that a polis founded by a lawgiver cannot be natural. [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 16, "Objection to the polis argument."

**Reconstructions.** The function argument (guide (a) is the check, not the source); the doctrine of the mean (guide (b)). Non-contradiction, the Forms critique and the unmoved mover get their +1-week touches; soul as first actuality gets its +3-week touch in Week 7.

**Cards.** +6: the argument cards for Arguments 12 and 13, and the concept cards *eudaimonia* (flourishing), *ergon* (function), *aretē* (virtue, excellence) and *hexis* (state, disposition). Deck at 27.

## Week 7 — Ethics II: *Nicomachean Ethics* V–VII and X, with the guide (~6.1 hrs: 4.5 desk + 0.7 media + 0.9 cards)

**Primary.** *NE* Book V (justice; if time is short, V.1–2 and V.5, the core distinction between kinds of justice, as the guide advises), Book VI (the intellectual virtues and *phronēsis*, practical wisdom), Book VII (*akrasia*, weakness of will, and the first discussion of pleasure; VII.1–3 is the core), and Book X (pleasure again, then contemplation in X.7–8 and the turn to politics in X.9). Books V–VII are the "common books," shared with the *Eudemian Ethics*; the guide's note on that overlap is enough for now. *Optional, and worth it if the week allows:* *NE* VIII.1–4, the three kinds of friendship. The path's Aristotle row assigns a selection from VIII; this plan's fixed weeks do not, so take it here with Adamson ep. 45, and the guide's checkpoint 13, if you want the path's full block covered.

**The guide.** Its §1 sections on Books V, VI, VII and X; its reconstruction **(c)** (the contemplative life), written book-closed before checking; its §3 **checkpoints 9–12, 14 and 15** as the extended set, answers in §5 (checkpoint 13 too if you read VIII). Checkpoints 8 (Book IV) and 13 (Books VIII–IX) cover books this plan does not assign; leave them for a second pass.

**Alongside.** [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Arguments 14 (*akrasia*) and 15 (the contemplative life); [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md), "Known internal tensions," (4) contemplation against the practical life; [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md), live debate (2) "*Eudaimonia*: dominant or inclusive?"

**Media.** *Review:* Adamson ep. 45, "The Second Self: Aristotle on Pleasure and Friendship," after Book X (and VIII, if you took it); ep. 46, "Dominic Scott on Aristotle's Ethics," at the end of the week.

**Checkpoints (the dossier's own).**
1. *Aristotle says the facts refute Socrates on akrasia, then concludes that Socrates' position seems, after all, to result (1147b14–15). Is that a contradiction?* No, on either main reading, but the two readings differ. The akratic person has knowledge without using it, or uses the universal premise without the particular one, or has it only as a drunk man recites the verses of Empedocles (1147a13–20, 1147b12). On the ignorance reading, akrasia is a real but qualified ignorance, close to Socrates by design; on the "clear-eyed" reading, the akratic thinks the right thing and is moved by appetite all the same. Either way, Aristotle explains *how* passion blocks knowledge from action, which Socrates never did. [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 14.
2. *Distinguish the akratic from the self-indulgent, and the impetuous from the weak.* The self-indulgent person endorses his appetites and feels no regret; the akratic person judges rightly, fails, and regrets it, and the regret is the evidence. The impetuous akratic acts before deliberating; the weak one deliberates and then does not stand by the result (1150b19–22). Same section, "Textual basis."
3. *Why does Book X's god look familiar, and what does the familiarity tell you?* It is the god of *Metaphysics* Λ (Week 5), whose whole life is thought, and the divine element in us that contemplates is close kin to the active intellect of *De Anima* III.5 (Week 4). The ethics' summit is built on the psychology and the theology: this is why the dossier reads them first. [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 15, "Textual basis."
4. *Inclusive or dominant: state the dispute and the two textual points that constrain every reading.* Inclusive (Ackrill): *eudaimonia* is a whole of several goods, contemplation and virtuous action among them. Dominant (Kraut): it is organised around one supreme good, contemplation. Nagel: the tension is real, and Aristotle wavers between two pictures of what a human being is. The constraints: the contemplative person, as a human living among others, still chooses virtuous acts (1178b5–6); and the life of moral virtue is called happy in a secondary way (1178a9–10), never unhappy. The dossier leaves the dispute open, like the guide. [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 15; [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md), live debate (2).

**Reconstructions.** *Akrasia* (Argument 14); the contemplative life (guide (c) is the check). The function argument and the mean get their +1-week touches; soul as first actuality gets its +3-week touch.

**Cards.** +3: the argument cards for Arguments 14 and 15, and the concept card *phronēsis* (practical wisdom). Deck at 30.

## Week 8 — Politics: *Politics* I and III, VII selections (~5.6 hrs: 3.5 desk + 1.2 media + 0.9 cards)

**Primary.** *Politics* in Reeve's 2017 translation, or the *Readings*, which prints I, III and VII.1–3, 13, 15 ([02-works-and-texts.md](02-works-and-texts.md), "Editions and translations"). Book I whole: the polis by nature (I.2), then natural slavery (I.4–7), then the household's ranking of souls (I.13). Read I.4–7 as an argument to be reconstructed exactly and then criticised, which is what the library entry and the guide ask, not as a passage to skip. Book III: what a citizen is, and the classification of constitutions by who rules (one, few or many) and for whose good. Book VII in the *Readings*' selection: VII.1–3, on which life is best for a city and for a person, which reopens Book X's question in public; VII.13 and VII.15 on the best city's aim and its education.

**Alongside.** [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md), "The polis as natural" and "Known internal tensions," (5) teleology made political; [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 16, both halves, with the criticism of the slavery argument in full; [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md), live debate (6) "Natural slavery and women in the *Politics*"; [05-conversations.md](05-conversations.md), "Arendt, Anscombe, Foot, MacIntyre, Nussbaum," for who took the polis argument forward.

**Media.** *Review:* Adamson ep. 48, "Constitutional Conventions: Aristotle's Political Philosophy." BBC Radio 4, *In Our Time*, ["Aristotle's Politics"](https://www.bbc.co.uk/programmes/b00f8530) (6 Nov. 2008). *Optional, watch-after (the path's line, not counted in the hours):* Yale PLSC 114 (Steven B. Smith), [lectures 7–9](https://oyc.yale.edu/political-science/plsc-114), "The Mixed Regime and the Rule of Law: Aristotle," on *Politics* I, III, IV and VII; Smith reads Aristotle as a political scientist, which is its value as a second voice.

**Checkpoints (book closed).**
1. *Run the polis-by-nature argument, then give Keyt's objection and the reply.* Household, village, polis, each meeting more needs, the polis arising for life and continuing for the good life (1252b29–30); the earlier associations are natural and the polis is their end, and the nature of a thing is its end (1252b32); so the polis is natural and man a political animal (1253a2–3), the animal with speech, which can set forth the just and the unjust. Keyt: a polis is founded, and Aristotle praises the founder (1253a30–31), so it is a product of craft. Reply: the polis is natural as virtue is, neither by nature nor against it (Week 6, checkpoint 4). [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 16.
2. *What premise does natural slavery need, and how does Aristotle's own text undermine it?* A class of humans who share in reason enough to apprehend it but not to have it (1254b22–23): able to follow reasons, unable to deliberate. There is no such class, and Aristotle admits nature often fails to mark free and slave bodies apart (1254b27–32) and that souls' differences cannot be seen, so no actual person could be identified as a natural slave. A slave who understands orders is exercising the very reason the function argument makes the human mark. [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 16, "Criticism of the slavery argument."
3. *How does Book III define the citizen and classify constitutions?* The citizen by sharing in judging and ruling. Constitutions by whether one, few or many rule, and whether for the common good (kingship, aristocracy, and the constitution Aristotle calls polity) or for the rulers' own (tyranny, oligarchy, democracy, the deviant forms). [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md), "The polis as natural."
4. *Politics VII.1–3 asks which life is best. How does the answer bear on NE X?* It puts Book X's question to cities as well as persons, active life or philosophic life, and treats the best life for the city and for the individual as the same. Compare its answer with your Week 7 reconstruction of the contemplative life, and say whether it moves you toward the inclusive or the dominant reading. [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 15; [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md), tension (4).

**Reconstructions.** The polis by nature and natural slavery (Argument 16), the second half reconstructed and then criticised. *Akrasia* and the contemplative life get their +1-week touches; non-contradiction, the Forms critique and the unmoved mover get their +3-week touches.

**Cards.** +2: the argument card for Argument 16, and the concept card *endoxa* (reputable opinions), which this week shows at its most dangerous: in a slave-owning society, the reputable opinion about slaves was the owners'. Deck at 32.

## Week 9 — Rhetoric and Poetics, and the capstone (~8.6 hrs: 5.5 desk incl. the essay + 2.2 media + 0.9 cards)

**Primary.** *Rhetoric* I.1–2 in Reeve: rhetoric as the counterpart of dialectic, the definition of rhetoric as the capacity to see the available means of persuasion in each case, the three proofs (the speaker's character, *ēthos*; the hearer's emotion, *pathos*; the argument, *logos*), and the enthymeme. Then Book II's emotions in selection: II.1 on why emotion matters to judgment, then three of the chapters II.2–11 (anger, fear and pity are the natural set, since pity and fear return in the *Poetics* two days later). Then the *Poetics* 1–14 in Janko: imitation as natural to humans (chapter 4), the definition of tragedy (6), plot as the soul of tragedy and the unity it needs (6–8), poetry against history (9), and the best plot (13–14). About three hours of reading in all, then the capstone essay (about 2.5 hours, Session C and an extra sitting).

**Alongside.** [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md), "Techne, rhetoric and mimesis"; [05-conversations.md](05-conversations.md), "Isocrates and the quarrel over rhetoric," and "The themes trace"; [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md) in full, its "Misconceptions, in full form" and "Myths about the life" as capstone raw material; [08-bibliography.md](08-bibliography.md) for what to read next, and when.

**Media.** *Review:* Adamson ep. 49, "Stage Directions: Aristotle's *Rhetoric* and *Poetics*"; BBC Radio 4, *In Our Time*, ["Aristotle's Poetics"](https://www.bbc.co.uk/programmes/b00xw210) (27 Jan. 2011). *Review, whole-figure:* *Philosophize This!* (Stephen West), [#005, "Aristotle Part 1"](https://www.philosophizethis.org/podcast/aristotle-part-1-8z586) and [#006, "Aristotle Part 2"](https://www.philosophizethis.org/podcast/aristotle-part-2-axker): a popularizer's tour; test its compressions against nine weeks of text, and never cite it as evidence. The plan budgets about an hour for the pair, an estimate. *Optional, after the capstone:* Adamson ep. 51, "The Next Generation: the Followers of Plato and Aristotle," the bridge to Stage 1's Hellenistic rows.

**Checkpoints (book closed).**
1. *How is the Rhetoric's definition a reply to Plato's Gorgias?* The *Gorgias* calls rhetoric a knack of flattery, not a craft. Aristotle makes it a craft: a reasoned capacity, the counterpart of dialectic, to see the available means of persuasion in each case (*Rhet.* I.2), with argument at its centre in the enthymeme, a syllogism from premises that hold for the most part. If you ran the Plato dossier, set this against its Week 5 reconstruction of the flattery argument. [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md), "Techne, rhetoric and mimesis"; [05-conversations.md](05-conversations.md), "Isocrates and the quarrel over rhetoric."
2. *How does Rhetoric II define an emotion? Take one example through the scheme.* Each emotion is defined by its object, its cause, and the pleasure or pain it involves, so the speaker can know what state of mind produces it, toward whom, and on what grounds. Anger, for instance, is a painful desire for revenge for a perceived slight. Book II is moral psychology as much as advice, and it connects back to the *Ethics*' claim that virtue concerns feelings as well as actions. [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md), "Techne, rhetoric and mimesis"; [02-works-and-texts.md](02-works-and-texts.md), the *Rhetoric* entry.
3. *Poetry is more philosophical than history. Why, and what does the claim answer?* Because poetry tends to express the universal, the kind of thing such a person would say or do, while history tells what a particular person did (*Poet.* 9, 1451b5–7). It answers *Republic* X's charge that imitation stands at a third remove from truth: imitation of action can show what is general in it. [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md), "Techne, rhetoric and mimesis."
4. *What makes the best tragic plot, and what is still disputed about katharsis?* A protagonist neither villain nor saint who falls through some error (*hamartia*) rather than vice (*Poet.* 13), with pity and fear coming from the structure of the events rather than from spectacle (14), and the plot turning on reversal and recognition. Whether *katharsis* means purgation, purification or clarification is still open; say which you find best supported and flag it as a reading. [02-works-and-texts.md](02-works-and-texts.md), the *Poetics* entry.

**Reconstructions.** No new numbered argument: the capstone essay is this week's reconstruction. The polis argument gets its +1-week touch; the function argument and the mean get their +3-week touches.

**Cards.** +2 concept cards: *technē* (craft, art) and *mimēsis* (imitation, representation). Deck complete at 34 (16 argument cards, one per argument in [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), and 18 concept cards from [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md)), and permanent in the FSRS queue: the path's deck is cumulative for the whole plan.

## Capstone essay (800–1,200 words)

Write in Week 9, after the checkpoints, book closed for the first draft. **The gate relationship, stated exactly:** Stage 1's gate essay is *"Is virtue knowledge, habit, or neither?"*, Plato/Socrates against Aristotle, with the Stoics or Epicurus as third voice. **Prompt 1 below is built to serve as the Aristotle half of that gate essay**, the counterpart of the Plato dossier's capstone prompt 1 ([../plato/07-study-plan.md](../plato/07-study-plan.md), "Capstone essay"). Write it here at dossier length; when the gate comes due, after the Hellenistic weeks, join the two halves and add the third voice rather than starting cold. The other prompts stand alone as dossier capstones; each needs at least four of the nine weeks.

1. **Virtue is habit — Aristotle's case, at full strength (the gate-essay prompt).** From the function argument (Week 6) through habituation and the mean to practical wisdom (Week 7): reconstruct the claim that virtue of character is a settled state built by practice and completed by *phronēsis*, which in turn needs virtue. Then meet Socrates on his own ground: is Aristotle's account of *akrasia* a refutation of "virtue is knowledge," or a concession to it (Argument 14's two readings)? Close by saying whether "habit" alone is Aristotle's answer, given that he says practical wisdom and character virtue cannot exist apart.
2. **Forms in things: the critique of Plato and the problem it leaves.** Reconstruct the critique of separate Forms (Argument 1) and Aristotle's alternative, primary substance in the *Categories* and substance as form in *Metaphysics* Z (Week 1, Week 5). Then press Z's own difficulty: is form universal or particular, and what does each answer cost (Argument 10; [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md), live debate 4)? Include, as one paragraph, the learning path's Stage 1 exit checkpoint: Plato against Aristotle on forms and universals in five sentences.
3. **Is the soul the form of the body all the way up?** Soul as first actuality (Argument 8) dissolves the question whether soul and body are one. *De Anima* III.5 (Argument 9) and *NE* X.7–8 (Argument 15) then seem to bring back a separable, deathless intellect and make its activity the best human life. Is this a contradiction, an exception announced in advance, or a sign that Aristotle wavers between two pictures of what a human being is (Nagel)? State which reading of III.5 you assume, and flag it as disputed.
4. **Teleology from the teeth to the polis.** Follow one pattern of argument through the corpus: nature acts for an end (*Physics* II.8, Week 2), parts are explained by what they are for (*Parts of Animals* I.1, Week 3), the polis is the end of human association (*Politics* I.2, Week 8), and some humans are by nature fit to be ruled (I.4–7). Say where the pattern holds (biology after Darwin, the polis argument in its modest form) and where it fails (natural slavery), and decide the question the library entry leaves open: can the failure be removed without damage, or does it expose a flaw in the method itself? Use *endoxa* in your answer.
5. **Plato's two charges, answered.** The *Gorgias* says rhetoric is a knack of flattery; *Republic* X says imitation stands at a third remove from truth. Reconstruct Aristotle's replies, the *Rhetoric*'s craft of persuasion with the enthymeme at its centre, and the *Poetics*' claim that poetry is more philosophical than history (Week 9), and use *technē* (Week 5, *Metaphysics* A.1) to say what makes something a craft. Then judge one reply as Plato would: has Aristotle answered the charge, or changed the subject?
