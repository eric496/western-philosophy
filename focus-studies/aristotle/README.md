## The figure in brief

Aristotle was born in 384 BCE in Stagira, a small Greek town on the Chalcidice peninsula within Macedon's reach; his father Nicomachus was physician to the Macedonian king. At seventeen he went to Athens and joined Plato's Academy, and he stayed twenty years, until Plato's death in 347. The next twelve years took him to Assos in the Troad, to Lesbos, where much of the observation behind his zoology was done around the lagoon of Pyrrha, and to the Macedonian court, where from 343/342 he taught the young Alexander. In 335/334 he returned to Athens and taught at the Lyceum, building a school with a library, collections, and a programme of research that ran from the constitutions of Greek cities to the anatomy of the cuttlefish. When Alexander died in 323 and Athens turned on Macedon's friends, he withdrew to Chalcis, where he died the following year.

The Aristotle we read is not the Aristotle antiquity read. The polished dialogues he published, which Cicero praised for their style, are lost but for fragments. What survives is the school's working material: treatises, often lecture notes revised over years, on logic, nature, life, soul, first philosophy, ethics, politics, rhetoric, and poetry. Their compression is the price of their range. Out of them come the ideas this library keeps returning to: substance and the categories, matter and form, the four causes, potentiality and actuality, soul as the form of a living body, an unmoved mover that is pure actuality, happiness as excellent rational activity over a complete life, virtue as a mean fixed by practical wisdom, and the city as a natural community. He also invented formal logic, and for two thousand years most of it was his.

## Why he rewards weeks of study

First, *the method*: Aristotle begins from the appearances and the reputable opinions, sets out the puzzles they generate, and works toward an account that saves as much of them as it can. That procedure is still how most philosophy is done, and the treatises show it working on problem after problem. Second, *the system*: his doctrines constrain one another. The four causes come from the analysis of change, the analysis of change needs potentiality and actuality, those same concepts define the soul, and the definition of the soul is what the function argument in the *Ethics* rests on. You understand any one part properly only after you have read the others, which is why a few weeks with him repay so much more than a single book does. Third, *the reach*: he is "the Philosopher" of the medieval schools, Islamic, Jewish, and Christian; the target of Bacon, Hobbes, and Descartes; and, since the mid-twentieth century, a working partner again for virtue ethics, philosophy of biology, and the metaphysics of substance and essence. Reading him closely is reading the shared background of most of the figures in this library.

## The study plan at a glance

The library's [learning path](../../learning-path.md) meets Aristotle in **Stage 1** (Ancient Foundations), directly after Plato, on a selective route through the *Nicomachean Ethics*, the *Politics*, and short passages from the theoretical works. This dossier keeps the path's translations (Irwin and Reeve, from Hackett) and its rule that the primary text comes before the media, and widens the route to the whole system. [07-study-plan.md](07-study-plan.md) lays out a **9-week plan** at the path's weekly rhythm:

| Week | Reading | Theme |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | *Categories* 1–5, *De Interpretatione* 9, *Posterior Analytics* II.19 | Logic and categories |
| 2 | *Physics* II, III.1, VIII in summary | Nature, cause, change |
| 3 | *Parts of Animals* I; *History of Animals* and *Generation of Animals* selections | Biology |
| 4 | *De Anima* II.1–5, III.4–5 | Soul and intellect |
| 5 | *Metaphysics* A.1–2, Γ.3–4, Z.1, 3, 17, Λ.6–10 | First philosophy |
| 6 | *Nicomachean Ethics* I–III | Happiness and virtue |
| 7 | *Nicomachean Ethics* V–VII, X | Justice, wisdom, *akrasia*, contemplation |
| 8 | *Politics* I, III, VII selections | The city |
| 9 | *Rhetoric* I.1–2, II selections; *Poetics* 1–14; capstone essay | Persuasion, tragedy, and synthesis |

The ethics weeks use the library's book-by-book [study guide to the *Nicomachean Ethics*](../../study/aristotle-nicomachean-ethics.md). The biology week is there because the three main zoological treatises (*History*, *Parts*, and *Generation of Animals*) fill about a fifth of the Bekker pages, more with the shorter biological essays, and zoology is where his teleology and his theory of form do their most concrete work; skip it and the *Physics* and *De Anima* read as more abstract than he meant them.

The honest total is about **55 hours: roughly 36.5 at the desk, 10 of assigned media, and 8 of daily card review**, with weekly loads of about 5 to 6.5 hours and about 8.5 in Week 9, when the capstone is written. These hours sit on top of Stage 1's own budget rather than inside it; the plan file states the net addition to the stage and the trims that are incompatible with it.
