# Aristotle: The Arguments
**English** | [简体中文](../../zh/focus-studies/aristotle/04-arguments.md)

*Focus study · [Dossier home](README.md) · Library entry: [Aristotle](../../eras/01-ancient/aristotle.md) · The architecture these arguments build: [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md) · Study guide: [*Nicomachean Ethics*](../../study/aristotle-nicomachean-ethics.md)*

Each argument below is reconstructed in numbered premises in the house style: what the argument proves, the reconstruction, its textual basis, the strongest standard objection, and the best reply, Aristotle's own where he gives one and the literature's where he does not, labeled as such. Citations are by **Bekker number**. In 1831 the Prussian Academy in Berlin printed the whole corpus in Immanuel Bekker's edition, paginated straight through, two columns to a page, with numbered lines. So *Physics* III.1, 201a10 means page 201, left-hand column (a), line 10. Every serious translation prints these numbers in its margins; [02-works-and-texts.md](02-works-and-texts.md) explains them in full. Quotations come from the old Oxford translations, now in the public domain, so that each can be checked word for word online: Ross (*Metaphysics*), Edghill (*De Interpretatione*), Jenkinson (*Prior Analytics*), Hardie and Gaye (*Physics*), J. A. Smith (*De Anima*). They are for checking, not for a first read.

One framing caution governs the whole file. With Plato, whose arguments are spoken by characters in dramas, the question is whose argument it is. Aristotle speaks in his own voice, but what survives is not finished writing. The treatises are school texts: lecture notes, drafts and research files, revised over years and ordered by later editors. They are compressed, they leave steps out, and they sometimes list difficulties without resolving them. So the question here is **what the argument is**. A premise a reconstruction needs may be missing from the text or present only as a hint, and one chapter can support two incompatible reconstructions. Where the dispute changes the argument, both readings are named.

**Argument 1. The Third Man and the critique of the Forms**

*What it proves:* that Plato's Forms, taken as separate entities sharing the character of the things that share in them, generate an infinite regress; and, more broadly, that a universal (what many things have in common) cannot be a substance, an independent individual thing. This is Aristotle's case against his teacher, and it shapes his own metaphysics.

Plato had already given Parmenides a regress argument against the Forms (Argument 9 of the Plato dossier, [../plato/04-arguments.md](../plato/04-arguments.md)). Aristotle gave it its name. His fullest statement was in a lost work, *On Ideas* (*Peri Ideōn*, Latin *De Ideis*), known only through Alexander of Aphrodisias, a commentator of around 200 CE, who reports it in his commentary on *Metaphysics* A.9. That is a late paraphrase, not Aristotle's words. In Alexander's version the argument runs on "man":

1. Whenever several things are called by the same name, as Socrates and Callias are both called "man," the Platonist posits one Form, Man itself, existing apart from them, by sharing in which they are men.
2. The Form is itself a man, in the same sense as they are. (Modern readers call this **self-predication**.)
3. The Form is a different thing from the particulars. (**Non-identity**.)
4. Then the Form and the particular men are again several things with one name, and by premise 1 there must be a further Form over them all: a "third man."
5. The same reasoning applies again, without end.
Therefore the theory meant to explain the many by one Form yields infinitely many Forms for each kind.

Aristotle's diagnosis, in *Metaphysics* Z.13 and *Sophistical Refutations* 22, is that premise 2 treats a universal as an individual. "Man" said of many things does not name one more thing alongside them: "no common predicate indicates a 'this', but rather a 'such'," and if you deny it, "many difficulties follow and especially the 'third man'" (*Met.* Z.13, 1038b35–1039a2, Ross). So the substance of a thing is in the thing, not apart from it. A.9 adds that the arguments for Forms prove too much, yielding Forms of negations, and that Forms explain no change: "to say that they are patterns and the other things share in them is to use empty words and poetical metaphors" (*Met.* A.9, 991a20–22, Ross).

**Textual basis.** *Metaphysics* A.9, 990b17, where some arguments "introduce the 'third man'" (Ross), repeated in M.4, 1079a13; Z.13, 1038b34–1039a3; *Sophistical Refutations* 22, 178b36–179a10; *On Ideas* via Alexander, studied in Gail Fine, *On Ideas* (1993).

**Objection.** (i) Aristotle may be attacking a position Plato did not hold. Harold Cherniss (*Aristotle's Criticism of Plato and the Academy*, 1944) argued that he systematically misreads Plato, and many readers deny that Plato held self-predication in the regress-making sense: the Form of Man is not a man but what being a man is. (ii) The diagnosis seems to rebound. If substance is form, as Book Z goes on to argue (Argument 10), is a form universal, shared by a species, or particular, one to each individual? If universal, Z.13's rule that no universal is a substance rules it out. If particular, it is hard to see how forms ground knowledge, which Aristotle says is of universals.

**Reply.** The literature's, not Aristotle's. On (i): the critique need not settle what Plato meant, because its real target is **separation**, the Forms' existing apart. Aristotle credits Socrates with seeking universal definitions and says it was others who gave universals a separate existence (*Met.* M.4, 1078b30–32; M.9, 1086b2–5). Fine argues that once a Form is separate it must be treated as another individual, and then the regress threatens whatever one says about self-predication. On (ii) there is no agreed reply; whether Aristotelian forms are universal or particular is among the oldest disputes in the field, taken up with Argument 10. What survives either way is the distinction between a "this" and a "such."

**Argument 2. The principle of non-contradiction and its elenctic defence**

*What it proves:* that the firmest of all principles is that nothing can be and not be the same thing at the same time and in the same respect; that it cannot be proved directly, since every proof assumes it; and that anyone who denies it refutes himself as soon as he says anything definite.

*Metaphysics* Γ.3 states the principle: "the same attribute cannot at the same time belong and not belong to the same subject and in the same respect" (1005b19–20, Ross). Γ.3 also gives a version about belief (no one can believe the same thing to be and not to be, 1005b23–24), and Γ.6 one about statements (contradictories are not true together, 1011b13–14). How these **ontological**, **doxastic** and **logical** versions of the principle of non-contradiction (PNC) relate is debated. Γ.4 gives the defence. It is **elenctic**, from *elenchos*, refutation: a demonstration "negatively" (Ross) that refutes the opponent from his own commitments.

1. Not everything can be proved, on pain of infinite regress; to demand proof of everything shows "want of education" (1006a5–8).
2. So we can ask the opponent only to say something "significant both for himself and for another" (1006a21). If he says nothing, there is no one to argue with: such a man "is from the start no better than a vegetable" (1006a15).
3. Suppose he says "man." A word has one meaning, or a limited number, for "not to have one meaning is to have no meaning" (1006b7). Let "man" mean two-footed animal.
4. Then whatever is a man is, in that respect, a two-footed animal, and cannot also not be one. Otherwise "man" would mean "not-man" too, and the opponent's word would signify nothing determinate.
5. So in speaking significantly the opponent has relied on the principle he rejects: "while disowning reason he listens to reason" (1006a26).
Therefore the PNC, though indemonstrable, cannot be coherently denied by anyone who says anything.

**Textual basis.** *Metaphysics* Γ.3, 1005b11–34; Γ.4, 1005b35–1009a5, which adds further arguments. The best known is practical (1008b12–19): someone who thought everything alike so and not so would have no reason to act. Yet "why does a man walk to Megara and not stay at home" (Ross), and why does he not walk into a well? His conduct shows he thinks some things better than others.

**Objection.** The defence seems to beg the question. Premise 4 assumes that a word meaning two-footed animal cannot also mean its denial, which is close to the principle itself; a determined opponent grants that his words mean something and denies that meaning excludes its opposite. Jan Łukasiewicz (1910) argued that none of Aristotle's arguments establish the principle. More recently Graham Priest and other **dialetheists**, who hold that some contradictions are true (the usual candidate is the liar sentence, which says of itself that it is false), treat the principle as a thesis open to dispute.

**Reply.** Aristotle's own reply is built into the method. In a direct proof one "might be thought to be begging the question," but in an elenctic one "another person is responsible for the assumption" (1006a17–18, Ross). The argument does not derive the principle from anything more basic; premise 1 says that cannot be done. It claims something weaker and harder to escape: that significant speech already uses the principle, since a word means what it means by excluding something. Recent defenders (Alan Code and Michael Wedin among them) read Γ.4 this way, as showing what the principle is presupposed by. Whether dialetheists escape by allowing true contradictions only in special cases, never about men or walking to Megara, is a live question in philosophical logic.

**Argument 3. The sea battle and future contingents**

*What it proves:* on every reading, that fatalism fails: the move from "it was always true that this would happen" to "it was always necessary" is blocked, because deliberation makes a difference and some things can go either way. What Aristotle gives up to block it is the dispute.

*De Interpretatione* 9 asks about **future contingents**, statements about particular future events that may or may not happen. It states an argument for fatalism and rejects its conclusion.

1. Of any pair of contradictory statements, one is true and the other false (the **principle of bivalence**).
2. So of "there will be a sea battle tomorrow" and its denial, one is true already today.
3. Whatever is true, things are as it says.
4. And "if it was always true to say that a thing is or will be, it is not possible that it should not be or not be about to be" (18b11–12, Edghill). So whatever will happen will happen necessarily.
5. Then nothing happens by chance, and "There would be no need to deliberate or to take trouble" (18b31).
6. But deliberation and action do affect the future, and many things have "potentiality in either direction": a coat may be cut in half, or may wear out first (19a7–16).
So the conclusion is false, and some premise must go.

The resolution follows (19a23–b4). First, "that which is must needs be when it is" (19a23): being necessary *given* that it is so is not being necessary without qualification. Then the example: "A sea-fight must either take place to-morrow or not, but it is not necessary that it should take place to-morrow, neither is it necessary that it should not take place" (19a30–32). The disjunction is necessary; neither half is. Of such pairs, one "may indeed be more likely to be true than the other, but it cannot be either actually true or actually false" (19a38–39).

**Textual basis.** *De Interpretatione* 9, 18a28–19b4. The chapter is among the most commented-on in ancient philosophy, from the Stoics and the late commentators Ammonius and Boethius to the present.

**The main readings.** (a) On the **traditional** or **non-bivalence reading**, Aristotle denies premise 1 for future contingents: "there will be a sea battle tomorrow" is today neither true nor false, and becomes one or the other when tomorrow comes. The chapter's last sentence seems to say just this, and Łukasiewicz built a three-valued logic partly on it (1920). (b) On the **modal-fallacy reading**, defended by G. E. M. Anscombe in "Aristotle and the Sea Battle" (1956) among others, Aristotle keeps bivalence and denies premise 4: the fatalist slides from the necessity of a conditional (necessarily, if it is true it will happen) to the necessity of its consequent (if it is true, it will happen necessarily). The distinction at 19a23 then does the work. (c) A middle reading, found in Boethius, keeps that one of the pair is true but denies that it is *definitely* true, since nothing in the world yet settles which.

**Objection.** Against (a): giving up bivalence is costly. If neither half of "either there will be a sea battle tomorrow or there will not" is true, it is hard to see how the whole can be necessary, as Aristotle insists. Against (b): the chapter says these statements are not "actually true or actually false," which is hard to square with keeping bivalence.

**Reply.** The literature's. Reading (a) can be made consistent: a disjunction can count as true because it comes out true however the future goes, though neither part is true yet, an idea later logicians called **supervaluation**. Defenders of (b) answer that "actually true" means "definitely true," which brings them close to (c). No reading is agreed. What a beginner should keep is common to all three: the truth of a prediction does not fix the future, and the reality of deliberation is a datum any account of truth must respect.

**Argument 4. The syllogism and the first figure**

*What it proves:* that a large class of valid inferences can be described by form alone, whatever they are about, and that all of them reduce to two evident patterns. This, in summary, is the first formal logic in the Western tradition.

*Prior Analytics* I.1 defines the **syllogism** (*sullogismos*, "reckoning together"): "A syllogism is discourse in which, certain things being stated, something other than what is stated follows of necessity from their being so" (24b18–20, Jenkinson). In practice Aristotle studies a narrower form: two premises and a conclusion, each saying that one term belongs to all, none, some or not all of another, with a **middle** term shared by the premises and absent from the conclusion. He writes the terms as letters, itself an innovation.

1. Every premise has one of four forms: A belongs to all B, to no B, to some B, or not to some B.
2. Some premises convert: "no B is A" gives "no A is B," "some B is A" gives "some A is B," and "all B is A" gives only "some A is B" (I.2).
3. The middle term can stand in three positions, the three **figures**. In the first it is subject of one premise and predicate of the other: "If A is predicated of all B, and B of all C, A must be predicated of all C" (I.4, 25b37–39). Medieval logicians named this pattern Barbara, and its negative twin, with "no B" in the first premise, Celarent.
4. A syllogism is **perfect** when it "needs nothing other than what has been stated" to make its necessity plain (24b22–24). Only the first figure's are perfect.
5. The second and third figures are made perfect by conversion or by **reductio ad impossibile**, showing that denying the conclusion leads to an impossibility (I.5–6).
6. The particular first-figure syllogisms can themselves be proved through the second figure, so that "It is possible also to reduce all syllogisms to the universal syllogisms in the first figure" (I.7, 29b1–2).
Therefore the whole system rests on Barbara and Celarent plus the conversion rules.

**Textual basis.** *Prior Analytics* I.1–7, 24a10–29b25, in summary. I.1, I.2 and I.4 repay close reading; the rest is a catalogue of premise pairs, the invalid ones refuted by counterexample, terms that make the premises true and the would-be conclusion false.

**What the system is: the readings.** Łukasiewicz (*Aristotle's Syllogistic*, 1951) read each syllogism as one conditional proposition and the system as axiomatic, with the perfect syllogisms as axioms. John Corcoran (1972) and Timothy Smiley (1973) read a syllogism as an inference from premises to conclusion, and the system as **natural deduction**, proof by chains of evident steps. Their reading fits I.1's "follows of necessity" better and is now the more common one. Corcoran also showed that a formal model of the system is sound and complete: it proves exactly the valid inferences of its form.

**Objection.** The syllogistic is narrow. It handles only subject-predicate statements with one-place terms, so it cannot represent relations: Augustus De Morgan's example, that if a horse is an animal, the head of a horse is the head of an animal, is plainly valid and no syllogism captures it. Nor does it treat whole propositions ("if p then q; p; so q"), which the Stoics did soon after. [Frege](../../eras/05-20th-century/gottlob-frege.md)'s logic of 1879 replaced it with a system that handles both.

**Reply.** The literature's: the objection is true and does not lessen the achievement. Within its fragment the system is rigorous, and the fragment served Aristotle's purpose, the theory of scientific demonstration in the *Posterior Analytics*, where knowledge is of what holds of a whole kind. The ideas that make logic a science are already here: validity as form, variables for terms, a few evident rules from which the rest are derived, invalidity shown by counterexample. It remained the core of logic teaching for some two thousand years.

**Argument 5. The four causes**

*What it proves:* that a complete answer to "why?" about a natural thing has four parts, and that an inquirer who names fewer has not yet explained it.

The Greek *aitia*, usually translated "cause," is closer to "explanation," an answer to a "why?" question; Gregory Vlastos ("Reasons and Causes in the *Phaedo*," 1969) is the classic warning against reading the modern sense into it. [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md) defines each kind.

1. Knowledge is the aim of inquiry, and "men do not think they know a thing till they have grasped the 'why'" (*Phys.* II.3, 194b18–19, Hardie and Gaye).
2. "Why?" is answered in four ways: by that out of which a thing comes to be and which persists, the bronze of a statue (the **material cause**); by its form or essence (the **formal cause**); by the primary source of its change, the sculptor or the father (the **efficient cause**); and by that for the sake of which, as "health is the cause of walking about" (194b33) (the **final cause**).
3. The four are not rivals. One thing has several causes, "not merely in virtue of a concomitant attribute": the sculptor's art and the bronze are both causes of the statue as statue (195a4–8).
4. None reduces to another: an account of the bronze does not say what shape it took, and an account of the sculptor's work does not say what it was for.
Therefore a full explanation gives all four: "it is the business of the physicist to know about them all" (II.7, 198a22–23). In natural things "The last three often coincide" (198a24–25), since the form of a man, his end and the parent who made him are one form in different roles, "for man generates man" (198a27). The deep contrast is form against matter.

**Textual basis.** *Physics* II.3, 194b16–195b30 (repeated almost verbatim in *Metaphysics* Δ.2); II.7, 198a14–b9. *Metaphysics* A.3–10 argues that earlier thinkers each grasped only some of the four.

**Objection.** The founders of modern science rejected half the list. [Francis Bacon](../../eras/03-early-modern/francis-bacon.md) compared the search for final causes to a virgin consecrated to God, who bears nothing (*De Augmentis Scientiarum* III.5), and the seventeenth-century mechanical philosophy kept only matter in motion and efficient causes. On that view formal and final causes merely redescribe what is to be explained.

**Reply.** The literature's: the four are kinds of explanation, not four forces, and the objection lands mainly on later scholastic versions that made forms into hidden powers. As types of answer to "why?", all four survive. Chemistry explains by structure, the heir of formal cause; biology still asks what an organ is *for*, and philosophers of biology, from Larry Wright's "Functions" (1973), analyse function talk without a designer. Whether final explanations are basic is the question of Argument 6.

**Argument 6. Teleology against Empedocles**

*What it proves:* that nature acts for the sake of something: the parts and processes of living things exist and happen for ends. It does not claim that anyone designed them.

*Physics* II.8 first states the rival view with real force. Why should nature not work "just as the sky rains, not in order to make the corn grow, but of necessity" (198b18–19, Hardie and Gaye)? Perhaps teeth too come up sharp in front and broad behind by necessity, and the animals whose parts happened to be useful survived, "whereas those which grew otherwise perished and continue to perish, as Empedocles says his 'man-faced ox-progeny' did" (198b29–32; see [Empedocles](../../eras/01-ancient/empedocles.md)). Aristotle answers that "it is impossible that this should be the true view" (198b34):

1. Things happen either by chance or for an end.
2. Teeth and the other parts of living things come about "either invariably or normally" in the same way.
3. Nothing that happens by chance happens always or for the most part: rain in winter is not chance, rain in the summer heat is (198b36–199a3).
4. So the parts of living things do not come about by chance.
Therefore they come about for an end.

Supporting arguments follow. In art each step is for the sake of the next, and "as in intelligent action, so in nature" (199a9–10). Animals and plants plainly act for ends without deliberating: the swallow builds its nest, roots grow down for nourishment (199a20–30). Nature makes mistakes, which only what aims at an end can do: "monstrosities will be failures in the purposive effort" (199b4). And purpose does not need deliberation, since art does not deliberate either: "The best illustration is a doctor doctoring himself" (199b30–31). Nature is a skill working from inside the thing it shapes.

**Textual basis.** *Physics* II.8, 198b10–199b33. The method is put to work in the biology, *Parts of Animals* I.1.

**Objection.** Darwin answered the argument, and Aristotle had stated the winning reply himself and turned it down. Natural selection explains regularity *without* ends: teeth come out right for the most part because inheritance copies what survived, so premise 3 fails, and premise 1's either-or is not exhaustive. Quoting this passage in the "Historical Sketch" of later editions of the *Origin*, Darwin wrote: "We here see the principle of natural selection shadowed forth, but how little Aristotle fully comprehended the principle, is shown by his remarks on the formation of the teeth" (*On the Origin of Species*, 6th ed., 1872).

**Reply.** The literature's, in two parts. First, the Empedoclean view is not natural selection. It has no inheritance and no gradual accumulation; useful combinations arise at once by luck, and against *that* premise 3 is fair, since regularity would itself be a fluke. Second, Aristotle's teleology survives better than his premises. On the reading developed by Allan Gotthelf (1976) and John Cooper (1982), a final cause is the form a developing organism is on its way to, and to say that a part is for seeing or eating is to say that it cannot be explained without naming what it does. Darwin explains how such parts came to exist; biology still describes them by what they are for. How far the teleology reaches beyond organisms is disputed: David Sedley ("Is Aristotle's Teleology Anthropocentric?", 1991) reads the rain passage as allowing that rain falls for the crops, while most readers take it as Aristotle's own case of necessity without an end (see [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md)).

**Argument 7. Change as the actuality of the potential as such**

*What it proves:* that change is real and definable without contradiction, against [Parmenides](../../eras/01-ancient/parmenides-of-elea.md), who argued that what comes to be must come from what is, already there, or from what is not, which is nothing. The definition uses potentiality (*dunamis*, a capacity to be otherwise) and actuality (*energeia* or *entelecheia*, being at work or fulfilled), both explained in [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md). Aristotle's word for change, *kinēsis* (Hardie and Gaye: "motion"), covers change of substance, quality, quantity and place.

1. Some things are potentially what they are not yet actually: bricks are potentially a house, the cold potentially hot.
2. A thing can be actually one thing and potentially another, in different respects: bronze is actually bronze and potentially a statue.
3. Change is not the bare potentiality (bricks in the yard are not changing) nor the finished actuality (once the house stands, the building is over).
4. Nor is it the actuality of the thing as what it already is: "Bronze is potentially a statue. But it is not the fulfilment of bronze as bronze which is motion" (201a29–31).
5. What remains is the actuality of the thing as potential, the capacity at work while still a capacity: "the buildable, in so far as it is just that," is being built (201a16–17).
Therefore: "The fulfilment of what exists potentially, in so far as it exists potentially, is motion" (*Phys.* III.1, 201a10–11, Hardie and Gaye).

The qualifier "in so far as" (Greek *hēi*, "as such") carries the definition: it picks out the one aspect of the bronze that the change actualises. Aristotle admits that change is "a sort of actuality, but incomplete," and so "hard to grasp, but not incapable of existing" (III.2, 201b31–202a3).

**Textual basis.** *Physics* III.1, 200b12–201b15; III.2–3. Compare *Metaphysics* Θ.6, where actuality is explained by examples rather than defined.

**The two readings.** Does *entelecheia* here mean **actualization**, the process of becoming actual, or **actuality**, the state of being actual? On the first, W. D. Ross's in his edition of the *Physics* (1936), change is the potential being made actual, which is natural but threatens circularity, since actualization is itself a change. On the second, argued by L. A. Kosman ("Aristotle's Definition of Motion," 1969), change is the potential's being actual *as a potential*: the buildable fully at work as buildable just is the building. Kosman's reading avoids the circle and has wide support, though the dispute over the word continues.

**Objection.** The definition explains nothing. [John Locke](../../eras/03-early-modern/john-locke.md) held up its scholastic Latin form, "the act of a being in power, as far forth as in power," as a model of empty definition: "What more exquisite jargon could the wit of man invent" (*Essay* III.iv.8). Motion, for Locke, is a simple idea, known from experience and indefinable; no one who lacked it would learn it from the formula.

**Reply.** The literature's: the definition was never meant to teach the idea of change. It answers Parmenides, by showing that coming to be need not be coming from nothing, and it gives the *Physics* one structure for every kind of change. Read Kosman's way it is not circular, since it defines change by potentiality and actuality, which Aristotle treats as basic and explains by analogy (*Met.* Θ.6). And it does later work: the argument for the unmoved mover depends on it (Argument 11).

**Argument 8. Soul as the first actuality of a natural body that has life potentially**

*What it proves:* that the soul (*psychē*) is not a thing lodged in the body but the form of a living body; and so that the question whether soul and body are one is badly posed.

*De Anima* II.1 builds the definition in steps from the theory of substance and actuality.

1. Substance is said in three ways: as matter, which is not by itself "a this"; as form, in virtue of which a thing is "a this"; and as the compound. "Now matter is potentiality, form actuality" (*DA* II.1, 412a9–10, J. A. Smith).
2. Living bodies are substances in the third sense, compounds; "life" means at least self-nourishment, growth and decay.
3. The body cannot be the soul, since the body is the subject that has life, not what life is. So the body is the matter of the compound and the soul its form.
4. Form is actuality, so the soul is the actuality of such a body.
5. Actuality has two grades, like knowledge possessed and knowledge in use. The soul is the first, the possessed capacity, because a sleeping animal is still alive.
6. A body that has life potentially is one equipped with organs, tools for living.
Therefore the soul is "the first grade of actuality of a natural body having life potentially in it" (412a27–28), or, more briefly, of "a natural organized body" (412b5–6).

The first consequence: "the soul is inseparable from its body, or at any rate that certain parts of it are" (413a3–5). Aristotle leaves open a part, the intellect, that may be the actuality of no body, and a question he does not answer: whether the soul may be "the actuality of its body in the sense in which the sailor is the actuality of the ship" (413a8–9). Both stay open for *De Anima* III.5 (Argument 9).

**Textual basis.** *De Anima* II.1, 412a3–413a10, with the axe and the eye at 412b10–413a3; the powers of soul in II.2–3.

**Objection.** J. L. Ackrill ("Aristotle's Definitions of *psuchē*," 1972–73) raised the standard difficulty. Hylomorphism, the analysis of things into matter (*hulē*) and form (*morphē*), works for bronze and statue because the bronze exists before and after the statue and can be picked out without it. But the body that has life potentially is an organized body, and a body without soul, like a dead eye, is a body only "in name." So the matter of a living thing cannot be identified without its form, and the model does not fit the case it was most needed for. (Whether the definition anticipates modern **functionalism**, the view that mental states are defined by what they do, is a separate debate, between Putnam and Nussbaum, who say yes, and Burnyeat, who says no, both in 1992.)

**Reply.** Aristotle supplies part of an answer and the literature the rest. What is "potentially capable of living" is not what has lost its soul but what still has it, and "seeds and fruits are bodies which possess the qualification" (412b25–27): there is a stage at which matter is on its way to being alive. Commentators add a distinction between **proximate** matter, the organized body, which is alive whenever it exists, and **remote** matter, the flesh and elements, which can be picked out without the soul and outlast it as a corpse. At the remote level the bronze-and-statue model holds. At the proximate level Aristotle's point is precisely that soul and body are not two things, which is why it is idle to ask "whether the wax and the shape given to it by the stamp are one" (412b6–7). Whether this solves Ackrill's problem or restates it is still argued.

**Argument 9. The active intellect: a mind that "makes all things" (*De Anima* III.5)**

*What it proves:* that thinking needs two factors, not one: a receptive mind that becomes whatever it thinks, and an active cause that makes things actually thinkable; and that this active factor is separable from the body, unmixed and deathless. What that factor *is* is the most disputed question in Aristotle's psychology, so the reconstruction states only what the chapter says, and the readings follow.

1. In every class of things in nature there are two factors: a matter that is potentially all the members of the class, and a productive cause that makes them all, as a craft stands to its material (430a10–13).
2. The soul is no exception: these two factors must be found in it too (430a13–14).
3. The mind described in *De Anima* III.4, which is nothing actual until it thinks and then becomes its objects by taking on their forms, is the matter-like factor (430a14–15).
4. So there is a second mind, the one that makes all things. It works like light: "this is a sort of positive state like light; for in a sense light makes potential colours into actual colours" (430a15–17, J. A. Smith). As light lets a colour be actually seen, this mind lets what is potentially thinkable be actually thought.
5. What acts is superior to what is acted on, so this mind "is separable, impassible, unmixed, since it is in its essential nature activity" (430a17–18, J. A. Smith). "Impassible" means it cannot be acted on or changed.
Therefore, this productive mind, once set free, "alone is immortal and eternal" (430a23, J. A. Smith); "we do not, however, remember its former activity because, while mind in this sense is impassible, mind as passive is destructible" (430a23–25); and "without it nothing thinks" (430a25).

**Textual basis.** *De Anima* III.5, 430a10–25: the whole chapter, shorter than a page. It follows III.4's argument that thought has no bodily organ (429a24–27), and it cashes a promise made in II.1, where Aristotle says nothing prevents some parts of the soul from being separable, since they are the actuality of no body (413a6–7). Even the last clause is ambiguous: it can mean that nothing thinks without the active mind, or that the active mind thinks nothing without the passive one.

**The dispute.** The readings disagree about the chapter's subject, not just its details. **Alexander of Aphrodisias** (c. 200 CE), the greatest ancient commentator, identified the productive mind with the first cause, Aristotle's god (Argument 11): human thinking is lit from outside. [Averroes](../../eras/02-medieval/averroes.md) (12th century), in his *Long Commentary* on the *De Anima*, made both the productive and the receptive intellect single, separate substances shared by the whole human species; individuals supply only their images, and no individual mind survives death. [Thomas Aquinas](../../eras/02-medieval/thomas-aquinas.md), in *On the Unity of the Intellect* (1270), answered that the productive intellect is a power of each human soul, or else it would be false that *this* man thinks. Modern scholars still divide along the same lines: Victor Caston ("Aristotle's Two Intellects: A Modest Proposal," 1999) revived a divine reading, while others, Michael Wedin (*Mind and Imagination in Aristotle*, 1988) among them, read the active intellect as a functional aspect of each human mind.

**Objection.** Each reading seems to break something else in Aristotle. If the productive mind is each person's, a deathless part of the individual brings back the Platonic dualism that soul-as-form (Argument 8) was built to remove. If it is god, or one intellect for all, then III.5 is no longer about the human soul, though Aristotle says the two factors are found "within the soul" (430a13–14).

**Reply.** The literature's, since Aristotle gives none. The exception was announced in advance: II.1 and III.4 mark thought off as the one power that is not the actuality of a bodily organ, so hylomorphism was never claimed for every part of the soul. That does not settle which reading is right; it shows the price each pays. The divine reading must explain "within the soul", the Thomist why only this part is immortal and remembers nothing, the Averroist how an individual thinks at all.

**Argument 10. Substance as form: the syllable is more than its letters (*Metaphysics* Z.17)**

*What it proves:* that the substance of a thing, what makes it the thing it is, is its form, understood as the cause that makes its matter one definite thing. In *Metaphysics* Book Z this is a "fresh start" after the other candidates for substance have failed (see [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md)).

1. Substance is a principle and a cause, so we should look for it by asking a "why" question (1041a9–10).
2. A real "why" question always asks why one thing belongs to another. Asking "why he who is a man is a man" is empty; asking "why are these things, i.e. bricks and stones, a house?" is not (1041a10–b9, Ross). The answer is: "Because that which was the essence of a house is present" (1041b5–6, Ross).
3. So "what we seek is the cause, i.e. the form, by reason of which the matter is some definite thing; and this is the substance of the thing" (1041b7–9, Ross).
4. A regress argument confirms it. A syllable is not just its letters: BA is not the same as B and A, and flesh is not just fire and earth, for when they are separated the syllable and the flesh are gone while the letters and the elements remain (1041b11–19).
5. That extra "something" cannot be one more element, or the same question would arise about the new collection, and so on to infinity. Nor can it be made of elements, for the same reason (1041b19–25).
6. So "this 'other' is something, and not an element, and ... it is the cause which makes this thing flesh and that a syllable" (1041b25–27, Ross).
Therefore, form "is the substance of each thing (for this is the primary cause of its being)" (1041b27–28, Ross). In living things it is their nature: a principle, not an ingredient (1041b28–31).

**Textual basis.** *Metaphysics* Z.17, 1041a6–b33, with Z.3 on why matter cannot be substance (1029a27–28) and Z.13 on why no universal can be substance (1038b8–15). Book H.6 returns to the unity of definition with the same answer.

**Objection.** There are two, and the second is the deep one. First, "form" may look like an empty label, the name of whatever-it-is that makes a heap into a whole, like explaining sleep by a "sleep-making power." Second, Z cannot seem to decide whether form is universal or particular. Z.13 argues that a thing's substance "is that which is peculiar to it, which does not belong to anything else; but the universal is common" (1038b9–11, Ross). Yet definition and knowledge are of universals, and individuals cannot be defined (Z.15, 1039b27–1040a7). If form is universal, Z.13 rules it out as substance. If form is particular, Socrates' form distinct from Callias', then what we know is not substance.

**Reply.** The literature's. Form is not empty, because in a living thing it is the soul, an organisation specified by what it does (Argument 8). The second objection divides the scholars. Michael Frede and Günther Patzig (1988) argue that forms are particular: each person has their own. Michael Woods, Frank Lewis and Alan Code, among others, defend a form that is general without being a "universal" of the kind Z.13 attacks, which they take to be a universal like a genus, predicated of many things as their substance. Z.8, where Callias and Socrates differ by their matter but are the same in form (1034a5–8), favours generality, and Aristotle returns to the knowledge puzzle in *Metaphysics* M.10 without closing it. Treat the question as open.

**Argument 11. The unmoved mover: eternal motion needs a cause that is pure actuality (*Metaphysics* Λ.6–7, 9)**

*What it proves:* that there is an eternal, immaterial substance, wholly actual, which moves the cosmos as an object of love moves a lover, and whose own life is thought thinking itself. *Physics* VIII gives the physical version; *Metaphysics* Λ draws the theology.

1. Substances are the first of the things that exist, so if all substances were destructible, everything would be (1071b5–6).
2. But change and time can neither have begun nor end, since any "before" already needs time, and time goes with change. The only change that can be continuous and eternal is circular motion (1071b6–11).
3. Something that could cause motion but is not actually doing so guarantees nothing, for "that which is potentially may possibly not be" (1071b19). So "there must, then, be such a principle, whose very essence is actuality" (1071b19–20, Ross), and it must be without matter, since matter is the seat of potential (1071b20–21).
4. The first heaven turns forever. What moves it cannot itself be moved by something else, or the question repeats, so there is "something which moves without being moved, being eternal, substance, and actuality" (1072a25–26, Ross).
5. There is one way to move without being moved: the way an object of desire or of thought moves the one who desires or thinks of it (1072a26–27). The final cause "produces motion as being loved, but all other things move by being moved" (1072b3–4, Ross).
6. Its activity must be the best there is, which is thinking, and its object the best there is, which is itself. Thinking of anything lesser would make it depend on its object and change for the worse. So "its thinking is a thinking on thinking" (1074b34–35, Ross).
Therefore, a first mover exists of necessity, and "on such a principle, then, depend the heavens and the world of nature" (1072b13–14, Ross): a living god whose whole life is self-thinking thought.

**Textual basis.** *Metaphysics* Λ.6–7 (1071b3–1073a13) and Λ.9 (1074b15–1075a10), with Λ.8 and Λ.10; *Physics* VIII for the physical argument the [library entry](../../eras/01-ancient/aristotle.md) reconstructs.

**Objection.** The library entry's two objections stand: why must the chain of movers stop, rather than run back forever, and why only *one* unmoved mover? Λ.8 itself counts forty-seven or fifty-five spheres, each needing its own mover (1073b38–1074a14). A third objection comes from Λ.9: a god who thinks only itself cannot know or care about the world. So how does it explain anything that happens in the world, beyond the turning of the outermost heaven?

**Reply.** To the first two, the entry's reply: the later scholastic defense says the series is ordered *per se*. Each member moves only insofar as it is being moved here and now, so an infinite regress would leave no member with power of its own, and the prime mover is first in order and dignity, with subordinate movers below it. Whether that is Aristotle's thought or later metaphysics read into him remains disputed. To the third, the reply is Aristotle's own image in Λ.10: the universe holds its good "as an army does; for its good is found both in its order and in its leader, and more in the latter; for he does not depend on the order but it depends on him" (1075a13–15, Ross). The god does not manage the world; the world is ordered *toward* it. On the standard reading, which fills in what Λ leaves terse, the heavens imitate its unchanging activity by unending circular motion. It is no creator, as the library entry stresses, which is why Aquinas's First Way could borrow the argument's structure but not its god.

**Argument 12. The function argument: the human good is excellent rational activity (*Nicomachean Ethics* I.7)**

*What it proves:* the content of *eudaimonia* (flourishing, living well and doing well), the highest human good that Book I has already shown to be final and self-sufficient. This reconstruction follows the study guide's [reconstruction (a)](../../study/aristotle-nicomachean-ethics.md) and the library entry, and agrees with both.

1. For anything with a function (*ergon*, its characteristic work), its good lies in performing that function well. A flute-player's good is playing well; an eye's good is seeing well (1097b25–28).
2. If the parts of a person have functions, the whole person presumably has one too: "Have the carpenter, then, and the tanner certain functions or activities, and has man none? Is he born without a function?" (1097b28–30, Ross).
3. The human function cannot be the life of nutrition and growth, which plants share, or the life of perception, which "the horse, the ox, and every animal" share (1097b33–1098a2, Ross).
4. What remains, peculiar to humans, is "an active life of the element that has a rational principle" (1098a3–4, Ross), both obeying reason and exercising it in thought. The phrase "rational principle" is Ross's rendering of *logos*, reason.
5. A thing performs its function well when it performs it in accordance with the relevant excellence, or virtue (*aretē*): a good lyre-player plays well, not merely plays (1098a7–15).
Therefore, "human good turns out to be activity of soul in accordance with virtue, and if there are more than one virtue, in accordance with the best and most complete" (1098a16–18, Ross), and over a whole life, since "one swallow does not make a summer, nor does one day" (1098a18–19, Ross).

**Textual basis.** *NE* I.7, 1097b22–1098a20, after the criteria for the highest good (1097a15–b21). Premise 3's ladder of soul-powers is the *De Anima*'s (Argument 8); "the best and most complete" virtue points ahead to Book X (Argument 15).

**Objection.** As the guide and the entry put it, humans do not obviously have a function at all. Tools and organs have functions because they are made or used for something, but no maker assigns a job to a whole human life. And even if reason is distinctive of us, distinctiveness is not goodness: a capacity can be peculiar to humans, such as elaborate cruelty, without its exercise being what makes a life go well.

**Reply.** This is the Aristotelian reply, in the study guide's form. Aristotle's function is not a purpose imposed by a designer. It is what a thing characteristically does when it is working well, the activity that explains why it has the structure it has: seeing explains the eye. Reason in the same way structures distinctively human life. Premise 5 also blunts the cruelty case: the good is rational activity done *well*, in accordance with virtue. What remains is a real disagreement about premises, not a flaw in form: the argument stands or falls with Aristotle's broader teleology, and a modern reader may reasonably part company with him there.

**Argument 13. The doctrine of the mean: virtue is a settled state aiming at what is intermediate (*Nicomachean Ethics* II.6)**

*What it proves:* what kind of thing a virtue of character is, its genus and its differentia: a state (*hexis*, a settled disposition) concerned with choice, lying in a mean relative to us. This follows the guide's [reconstruction (b)](../../study/aristotle-nicomachean-ethics.md).

1. Virtue of character is concerned with feelings and actions, and in these there can be excess, deficiency and the intermediate. Fear, confidence, appetite, anger and pity can all be felt too much and too little, "but to feel them at the right times, with reference to the right objects, towards the right people, with the right motive, and in the right way, is what is both intermediate and best" (1106b18–23, Ross).
2. The intermediate is not an arithmetic midpoint but a mean *relative to us*, relative to the agent, the occasion, the object and the reason. If ten pounds of food is a lot and two a little, the trainer will not prescribe six for everyone: six is too little for the wrestler Milo and too much for a beginner (1106a26–b7).
3. Excess and deficiency are failures, while the intermediate is praised and is a kind of success. It is easy to miss the mark and hard to hit it, since there are many ways to go wrong and only one to go right (1106b24–35).
4. Virtue is the settled state that disposes us to choose the intermediate, as reason and the practically wise person would determine it.
Therefore, "Virtue, then, is a state of character concerned with choice, lying in a mean, i.e. the mean relative to us, this being determined by a rational principle, and by that principle by which the man of practical wisdom would determine it" (1106b36–1107a2, Ross). Each virtue is a mean between two vices: courage between cowardice and recklessness.

**Textual basis.** *NE* II.6, 1106a26–1107a27; the list of virtues and their paired vices in II.7 (1107a28–1108b10); the practical advice for hitting the mean in II.9 (1109a24–b26). Two limits come in the same chapter. In its essence virtue is a mean, but "with regard to what is best and right an extreme" (1107a6–8, Ross). And "not every action nor every passion admits of a mean" (1107a8–9, Ross): spite, envy, adultery, theft and murder are bad in themselves, not excesses of something good.

**Objection.** The guide's objection: the doctrine is either trivial or empty. It is trivial if "the mean" just means "the right amount," because then it tells us nothing we did not know. It is empty if the only way to find the mean is to ask what the practically wise person would do, because then we need an independent account of practical wisdom (*phronēsis*) to avoid going in a circle. A sharper modern form, pressed by Rosalind Hursthouse ("A False Doctrine of the Mean," 1981), is that many virtues do not lie on a single scale of more and less at all. Truthfulness, for example, does not mean telling a moderate number of truths.

**Reply.** The Aristotelian reply, as the guide gives it. The doctrine is not a decision procedure but a structural claim about what virtue *is*. It denies that more of a good trait is always better, explains why virtues come flanked by two vices, and places virtue in feeling as well as action. J. O. Urmson ("Aristotle's Doctrine of the Mean," 1973) read it as a doctrine about a settled character that feels what each occasion calls for, sometimes a great deal: the mean is not moderation. The content comes from the virtues of Books III–V and practical wisdom in Book VI. The circularity worry is real but not fatal: ethics, for Aristotle, cannot be written as a code, so a theory that leaves room for trained judgment is accurate by his lights. Whether that satisfies a reader who wants rules is a live debate.

**Argument 14. *Akrasia*: how a person can know better and still do worse (*Nicomachean Ethics* VII.3)**

*What it proves:* that *akrasia*, acting against one's own better judgment, is possible, against [Socrates](../../eras/01-ancient/socrates.md), who denied it; and what kind of knowing the akratic person has and lacks. Ross translates *akrasia* as "incontinence," meaning lack of self-control, and the self-controlled person as "continent."

1. Socrates held that "there is no such thing as incontinence; no one, he said, when he judges acts against what he judges best-people act so only by reason of ignorance" (1145b25–27, Ross). For it would be strange if knowledge could be mastered and dragged about like a slave (1145b23–24).
2. But "this view plainly contradicts the observed facts" (1145b27–28, Ross). We must ask what kind of ignorance the akratic person has.
3. "Know" has two senses: having knowledge without using it, and using it. Acting against knowledge one is using would be strange; acting against knowledge one has but is not using is not (1146b31–35).
4. Action comes from two kinds of premise, a universal one ("dry food is good for every man") and a particular one ("this food is such and such"). A person can use the universal premise while not using the particular one, and it is particular acts that have to be done (1146b35–1147a10).
5. Passion puts a person in a third condition, of having knowledge in a sense and not having it, "as in the instance of a man asleep, mad, or drunk" (1147a13–14, Ross). Anger and sexual appetite actually alter the body. Saying the right words proves nothing: such people can recite proofs "and verses of Empedocles" (1147a20, Ross), as actors speak their lines.
6. In the akratic case two universals are present: one forbids tasting, and another says everything sweet is pleasant. With "this is sweet" active and appetite present, appetite moves the body. What is contrary to the right rule is the appetite, not the opinion (1147a24–b3).
Therefore, the akratic person acts against knowledge only in a qualified sense. The last premise, about a perceptible particular, they either lack in the moment or have only "as a drunken man may utter the verses of Empedocles" (1147b12, Ross). So "the position that Socrates sought to establish actually seems to result" (1147b14–15, Ross): knowledge in the full sense is not what passion drags about.

**Textual basis.** *NE* VII.1–3, 1145a15–1147b19, especially VII.3 (1146b31–1147b19). VII.7–8 add two distinctions the guide stresses. The akratic person differs from the self-indulgent one, who endorses his appetites and feels no regret. And the akratic person may be *impetuous*, acting before deliberating, or *weak*, deliberating and then not standing by the result (1150b19–22).

**Objection.** Aristotle seems to save the phenomenon by giving up what made it a puzzle. Premise 2 says the facts refute Socrates. Yet the conclusion grants Socrates that full knowledge is never overcome. The person who acts against what they clearly see, here and now, to be worse, the case Donald Davidson made central in "How Is Weakness of the Will Possible?" (1970), seems to be defined away as a kind of drunkenness.

**Reply.** The reply is the literature's, and it divides on what VII.3 even says. On one reading, Aristotle is close to Socrates by design: the akratic person lacks the active grasp of the particular premise, so akrasia is a real, but qualified, ignorance. On another, the "clear-eyed" reading, premise 6 shows the akratic person thinks the right thing and is moved by appetite all the same, so the conflict is real. On both readings, as the guide says, Aristotle keeps both halves of the phenomenon: the akratic person genuinely judges the better course, so is not simply ignorant, and genuinely fails to act on it, so is not a hypocrite. Their regret, which the self-indulgent person lacks, is the evidence. VII.3 also explains *how* passion blocks knowledge from bearing on action, which Socrates never did. Whether that is enough is still debated.

**Argument 15. The contemplative life is happiest (*Nicomachean Ethics* X.7–8)**

*What it proves:* that the most complete *eudaimonia* is contemplation (*theōria*), the activity of intellect (*nous*) studying truths that cannot be otherwise, and that the life of the other virtues is happy "in a secondary degree." This follows the guide's [reconstruction (c)](../../study/aristotle-nicomachean-ethics.md).

1. "If happiness is activity in accordance with virtue, it is reasonable that it should be in accordance with the highest virtue; and this will be that of the best thing in us" (1177a12–14, Ross). The best thing in us is intellect.
2. Contemplation is the activity of intellect, directed at the best objects that can be known (1177a19–21).
3. It is the most continuous activity, "since we can contemplate truth more continuously than we can do anything" (1177a21–22, Ross). It is the pleasantest, the most self-sufficient (the just person needs others to be just toward, while the philosopher "even when by himself, can contemplate truth," 1177a32–34, Ross), the most leisured, and alone "loved for its own sake" (1177b1, Ross).
4. The gods are supremely happy, yet it would be absurd to picture them making contracts or facing dangers. Take away action and production from a living being, and "what is left but contemplation?" (1178b20–21, Ross). So the human activity most akin to the gods' is most of the nature of happiness (1178b21–23).
5. The life of the other virtues is happy too, but in a human way: just and brave acts are done toward others, and the virtues of character are bound up with the body and the passions. "But in a secondary degree the life in accordance with the other kind of virtue is happy" (1178a9–10, Ross).
Therefore, the contemplative life is the happiest. Such a life "would be too high for man; for it is not in so far as he is man that he will live so, but in so far as something divine is present in him" (1177b26–28, Ross). Yet we must, "so far as we can, make ourselves immortal" (1177b33, Ross).

**Textual basis.** *NE* X.7–8, 1177a12–1179a32, with the first hint in I.7's clause about "the best and most complete" virtue (Argument 12) and the definitions of *nous* and theoretical wisdom (*sophia*) in VI.6–7. Premise 4's god is the god of *Metaphysics* Λ (Argument 11), whose whole life is this activity, and "something divine ... in him" is close kin to the active intellect of Argument 9.

**Objection.** The guide's objection: this seems to contradict Books I–IX, which present flourishing as an active, social life of friendship, justice and practical wisdom. If contemplation is happiest, the moral virtues look demoted to a consolation prize, and the practically wise person of Book VI looks like someone who has settled for second best.

**The dispute, and the replies.** The labels come from W. F. R. Hardie ("The Final Good in Aristotle's Ethics," 1965). On an **inclusive** reading, *eudaimonia* is a whole made of several goods, contemplation and virtuous action among them. J. L. Ackrill ("Aristotle on Eudaimonia," 1974) argued that Book I already means this. On a **dominant** (or intellectualist) reading, *eudaimonia* is organised around one supreme good, contemplation, with other goods valued partly for its sake. Richard Kraut (*Aristotle on the Human Good*, 1989) is its fullest modern defense. A third line, put by Thomas Nagel ("Aristotle on Eudaimonia," 1972), holds that the tension is real and that Aristotle wavers between two conceptions of what a human being is. Two textual points constrain every reading. Aristotle says of the contemplative person that "in so far as he is a man and lives with a number of people, he chooses to do virtuous acts" (1178b5–6, Ross), so contemplation does not cancel ethics. And Aristotle never calls the life of moral virtue unhappy, only happy in a secondary degree. Like the study guide, this dossier leaves the dispute open.

**Argument 16. The polis by nature, and natural slavery (*Politics* I.2, I.4–7)**

*What it proves:* that the city-state (*polis*) exists by nature and is prior to the individual; and then, by the same pattern of reasoning, that some human beings are slaves by nature. The first is still discussed with respect. The second is reconstructed faithfully so that its failure can be seen exactly; as the library entry and the study guide insist, it must be read critically, not set aside.

*The polis by nature (I.2).*

1. Human associations grow in stages: the household meets daily needs, the village meets more than daily needs, and when villages unite into a community nearly or quite self-sufficient, the polis comes into existence, "originating in the bare needs of life, and continuing in existence for the sake of a good life" (1252b29–30, Jowett).
2. The earlier associations are natural, and the polis is their end, "and the nature of a thing is its end" (1252b32, Jowett): what a thing is when fully developed.
3. So the polis is a creation of nature, and "man is by nature a political animal" (1253a2–3, Jowett).
4. "Nature, as we often say, makes nothing in vain, and man is the only animal whom she has endowed with the gift of speech" (1253a9–10, Jowett). Voice signals pleasure and pain; speech sets forth the just and the unjust, and sharing in these makes a household and a polis (1253a10–18).
5. The polis is prior by nature to the individual, as the whole is to the part. A hand severed from the body is a hand only in name, like a stone hand. Whoever cannot live in a polis, or needs none, "must be either a beast or a god" (1253a29, Jowett).

*Natural slavery (I.4–7).*

6. A slave is a living possession, an instrument for action, one who "being a human being, is also a possession" (I.4, 1254a14–16).
7. Ruling and being ruled is natural and beneficial throughout nature: "from the hour of their birth, some are marked out for subjection, others for rule" (1254a23–24, Jowett). Soul rules body, intellect rules appetite, humans rule tame animals, and, Aristotle adds, male rules female (1254a34–b16). That last claim, too, is rejected today.
8. Where human beings differ as soul differs from body, the lower are slaves by nature, and it is better for them to be ruled (1254b16–20).
9. The natural slave is one "who participates in rational principle enough to apprehend, but not to have, such a principle" (1254b22–23, Jowett): able to follow reasons, unable to deliberate. Later Aristotle says flatly that "the slave has no deliberative faculty at all" (I.13, 1260a12, Jowett).
Therefore, "some men are by nature free, and others slaves, and that for these latter slavery is both expedient and right" (1255a1–2, Jowett). I.6 adds that slavery by mere convention, such as enslaving prisoners of war, is often unjust, and I.7 that mastery is no great science.

**Textual basis.** *Politics* I.2 (1252a24–1253a39); I.4–7 (1253b23–1255b40); I.13 (1259b18–1260b24). The function argument (Argument 12) and the soul's ruling and ruled parts (*NE* I.13) supply the psychology.

**Objection to the polis argument.** "Nature" seems to shift its meaning. Households and villages grow, but a polis is founded and given laws. Aristotle himself says that "he who first founded the state was the greatest of benefactors" (1253a30–31, Jowett). David Keyt ("Three Basic Theorems in Aristotle's *Politics*," 1991) pressed this: a thing made by a founder is a product of craft, not nature, in Aristotle's own sense. **Reply** (the literature's): the polis is natural as virtue is, which arises neither by nature nor against it; we are fitted by nature to receive it and completed by habit (*NE* II.1). The founder perfects what speech and the sense of justice make possible. The argument survives in a modest form: human goods are realised only in a shared civic life.

**Criticism of the slavery argument.** Here no reply rescues the conclusion, and the main lines of criticism can be stated in order.

- *The key factual premise is false.* Premise 9 needs a class of human beings who can follow reasons but cannot deliberate. There is no such class. Aristotle's own text gives the game away. "Nature would like to distinguish between the bodies of freemen and slaves," he writes, "But the opposite often happens" (1254b27–32, Jowett), and the soul's beauty, unlike the body's, "is not seen" (1254b39–1255a1). So by his own account no one could ever pick out a natural slave. The theory could never justify any actual enslavement. He even concedes that "it must be admitted that some are slaves everywhere, others nowhere" (I.6, 1255a31–32, Jowett), and then notes that Greeks call only non-Greeks slaves by nature: the "natural" category tracks Greek prejudice.
- *It is inconsistent with the rest of the theory.* A slave who must understand orders, and who needs enough virtue to do the work well (I.13), is exercising the very reason that the function argument makes the mark of a human being. In *NE* VIII.11 Aristotle admits there can be friendship with a slave *as a human being*, though not as a slave (1161b5–8): a concession that the category does not fit the person.
- *The method failed.* Aristotle starts from reputable opinions (*endoxa*), and in a slave-owning society the reputable opinion about slaves was the owners'. The teleology did not create the prejudice; it gave it the look of a finding about nature.

Scholars differ on what follows. Bernard Williams (*Shame and Necessity*, 1993) argued that Aristotle's theory stands nearly alone in antiquity as an attempt to show slavery just, and that its failure shows how the Greeks lived with an institution they could not defend. Malcolm Schofield ("Ideology and Philosophy in Aristotle's Theory of Slavery," 1990) argued that it is real philosophy, not mere ideology, which makes its failure more instructive, not less. The question the library entry records remains open: can these claims be removed without damage, or do they expose a flaw in the method itself? Many readers answer that the theory of flourishing survives the removal, and that its own premise, that reason is the distinctively human capacity, is the best argument against natural slavery.
