# Aristotle: The System and Its Concepts

*Focus study · [Dossier home](README.md) · Library entry: [Aristotle](../../eras/01-ancient/aristotle.md) · Arguments in full: [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md) · Study guide: [*Nicomachean Ethics*](../../study/aristotle-nicomachean-ethics.md)*

## A system, but a school's system: the honest starting point

Plato left dialogues; Aristotle left something much closer to a system. His surviving treatises run from logic through physics, biology, psychology and metaphysics to ethics, politics, rhetoric and poetics, and they share one vocabulary: substance, matter and form, the four causes, potentiality and actuality, nature and end. A concept made in one treatise is put to work in the next. That interlocking is real, and this file maps it.

Two cautions come with the map. First, the treatises are not finished books. As the library entry notes, they are lecture texts and research notes, edited by later hands, above all Andronicus of Rhodes in the first century BCE, so a chapter may repeat another or contradict a sister treatise. Second, Aristotle reasons through puzzles. An inquiry starts from the reputable opinions (*endoxa*) of the many and the wise, sets out the puzzles (*aporiai*) they produce, and saves as much of the opinion as survives. A passage that reads like doctrine may be one side of a puzzle. Where scholars divide over which is which, this file names the division and does not settle it.

## The central questions

Three questions drive the corpus. What is most fundamentally real? Aristotle's answer is substance, and his long struggle is over what substance turns out to be. What is it to explain something? To know a thing, he says, is to know its causes, and there are four kinds. How should a human being live? The answer is *eudaimonia*, the flourishing life of excellent rational activity, lived in a political community.

Beneath all three sits one habit of mind, which he states again and again: an important word is said in many senses. Plato tended to look for the one Form behind each word. Aristotle asks in how many senses the word is used and how the senses hang together. He does this with "being," "cause," "nature," "one," "good" and "prior." It is his deepest methodological break with his teacher, and it shapes every section below.

## Substance and the ten categories

The *Categories*, the short work that opens the logical corpus, sorts what can be said of things. A word on its own picks out one of ten kinds: "Expressions which are in no way composite signify substance, quantity, quality, relation, place, time, position, state, action, or affection" (*Cat.* 4, 1b25–27, Edghill). "Man" names a substance, "two cubits long" a quantity, "white" a quality, "double" a relation, "in the Lyceum" a place, "yesterday" a time, "is lying down" a position, "has shoes on" a state (also rendered "having"), "cuts" an action, and "is cut" an affection, meaning being acted upon. These are the **categories**, from *katēgoria*, "predication": the highest kinds of thing that can be said.

The other nine depend on the first. A colour is always some substance's colour. The *Categories* makes the point with two relations. Some things are "said of" a subject, as the species "man" is said of Socrates. Others are "present in" a subject, as a patch of white is in a body and cannot exist apart from it. Primary substance passes neither test: "Substance, in the truest and primary and most definite sense of the word, is that which is neither predicable of a subject nor present in a subject; for instance, the individual man or horse" (*Cat.* 5, 2a11–14, Edghill). Species and genera, "man" and "animal," are substances only in a secondary sense.

This inverts Plato. For Plato the universal Form was most real and particulars were its copies. Here the individual is bedrock, and the universal is real as said of individuals. The *Metaphysics* gives the priority a wider form: "There are many senses in which a thing may be said to 'be', but all that 'is' is related to one central point, one definite kind of thing" (*Met.* Γ.2, 1003a33–34, Ross). Aristotle's comparison is "healthy." A diet is healthy because it preserves health and a complexion because it shows health, and every use points back to the health of a body. Being works the same way, and its central case is substance. G. E. L. Owen called this structure **focal meaning** (1960). It is what makes a single science of being possible, and it explains the opening of *Metaphysics* Z: the ancient question "what being is, is just the question, what is substance?" (*Met.* Z.1, 1028b4, Ross).

## Matter and form

The *Categories* says individuals are primary but not what an individual is. The *Physics* and *Metaphysics* answer with **hylomorphism**, from *hylē*, "matter," and *morphē*, "shape" or "form." An ordinary substance is a compound. Matter is what it is made of; form (also *eidos*) is the organisation that makes that matter a thing of a kind. Bronze is a statue's matter and the shape of Hermes its form. Bricks and timber are a house's matter, and the arrangement that makes them a shelter is its form.

Matter is relative to a level. Bricks are the house's matter, but bricks are clay given a form, and clay is earth and water given a form. Whether the ladder ends in a **prime matter** with no form at all is disputed: the tradition said yes, and some modern scholars deny Aristotle held it. Form, not matter, does the explaining. The same bricks could make a wall or a kiln, so what makes this heap a house is its arrangement. Hence Aristotle can say the form is more the thing than its matter is. This is how he answers Plato without abandoning him. Plato was right that a thing's form fixes what it is, and wrong to put the form in a separate realm. Form is in things and is studied by studying things.

## The four causes

The Greek *aitia* is often better rendered "explanation": an answer to a "why?" question. *Physics* II.3 sorts the answers into four. The **material cause** is that out of which a thing comes to be and which persists in it: the bronze. The **formal cause** is the form or essence: the shape of Hermes. The **efficient cause** is the primary source of the change: the sculptor, or more exactly the sculptor's art. The **final cause**, the *telos* or "that for the sake of which," is the end: honouring the god, or, for a living thing, the life it is organised to lead.

The four are not rival theories but four questions a complete explanation answers together. In natural things three of them often coincide (*Phys.* II.7): the form of a man, the end of his growth and the parent who produced him share one form. Only matter stands apart. And the final cause is not a wish in anyone's head. It is the activity a structure is for: an eye is explained by seeing because seeing makes sense of its parts. The four causes are Argument 5 in [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md).

## Potentiality and actuality

Parmenides had argued that change is impossible: what comes to be must come from what is, which is already there, or from what is not, and nothing comes from nothing. Aristotle answers with a pair of concepts. **Potentiality**, *dunamis*, is a capacity to be otherwise or to act. **Actuality**, *energeia* (literally "being at work"), is its exercise or realisation. He also uses *entelecheia*, probably his own coinage, which suggests "having one's end within." The two are often interchangeable, *entelecheia* leaning toward the achieved state and *energeia* toward the activity. An acorn is actually an acorn and potentially an oak. Change is not being from nothing. It is something that already is, becoming actually what it already was potentially.

*Physics* III.1 defines change on this footing: "The fulfilment of what exists potentially, in so far as it exists potentially, is motion" (*Phys.* III.1, 201a10–11, Hardie and Gaye). While a house is being built, the bricks are being actualised as buildable. Once the house stands, the change is over (Argument 7 in [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md)).

Two refinements recur everywhere. One is a distinction of levels. A person who has learned Greek but is asleep has a *first* actuality, an acquired capacity; reading Greek is a *second* actuality, its exercise. The definition of soul depends on it. The other is the thesis that "actuality is prior to potency" (*Met.* Θ.8, 1049b5, Ross). A capacity can only be defined by what it is a capacity for, and what is eternal is fully actual. That thesis points straight at the unmoved mover.

## Nature and teleology

*Physics* II.1 defines a natural thing against an artefact: nature is "a source or cause of being moved and of being at rest in that to which it belongs primarily" (*Phys.* II.1, 192b21–23, Hardie and Gaye). A wooden bed has no inner tendency to be a bed. An acorn carries its own principle of growth toward a mature form, so a thing's nature is, above all, its form.

From here Aristotle argues that nature acts for the sake of something. In *Physics* II.8 he faces the view he links to Empedocles: animals whose teeth happened to come out sharp in front and flat behind survived by luck, and the rest perished. His reply is that chance outcomes are rare, while teeth come out right always or for the most part. Then the key clarification: "It is absurd to suppose that purpose is not present because we do not observe the agent deliberating. Art does not deliberate" (*Phys.* II.8, 199b26–28, Hardie and Gaye). A practised builder does not deliberate over each brick. "If, therefore, purpose is present in art, it is present also in nature" (199b29–30). Nature, he adds, is like a doctor treating himself. Teleology here is not a designer outside nature; it is the end-directedness built into how living things work (Argument 6 in [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md)).

How far it reaches is disputed. A modest reading confines final causes to organisms and their parts. David Sedley ("Is Aristotle's Teleology Anthropocentric?", 1991) defends a stronger, cosmic order, drawing on *Politics* I.8, where plants exist for animals and animals for humans. The biology is where the doctrine earns its keep. *Parts of Animals* I.5 tells the student not to "recoil with childish aversion from the examination of the humbler animals," because "Every realm of nature is marvellous" (*PA* I.5, 645a15–17, Ogle), and repeats the story of Heraclitus inviting visitors into his kitchen, since gods were present there too.

## Soul as the form of the living body

Soul (*psychē*) is where hylomorphism, actuality and teleology meet. It is not a ghost added to a body; it is what makes a living thing alive, so plants have one. *De Anima* II.1 defines it as "the first grade of actuality of a natural body having life potentially in it" (*DA* II.1, 412a27–28, J. A. Smith). "Actuality" makes soul the form of which body is the matter. "First" uses the distinction between having a capacity and exercising it: a sleeping animal is still alive. Smith's next sentence adds that such a body is "organized," equipped with organs, tools for the life the soul is.

The old question of how soul and body are related simply dissolves: "it is as meaningless as to ask whether the wax and the shape given to it by the stamp are one" (*DA* II.1, 412b6–7, J. A. Smith). His illustration follows. If the eye were an animal, "sight would have been its soul" (412b18–19), and an eye that cannot see, like a statue's, is an eye in name only (Argument 8 in [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md)).

Souls differ by their powers (*dunameis*), which are nested. The **nutritive** power, for feeding, growth and reproduction, belongs to everything alive. The **perceptive** power, bringing desire and movement with it, belongs to animals; touch is the sense no animal lacks. The **rational** power belongs to humans. Each higher power presupposes the lower ones, as a square contains a triangle (*DA* II.3). The *Ethics*' function argument climbs exactly this ladder.

### Nous, the intellect

Thinking strains the picture. Perception receives the form of its object without the matter, as wax takes a seal's shape without its gold. Thought receives forms too, but it can receive every form, so it can have no bodily nature of its own. The mind, "by mind I mean that whereby the soul thinks and judges," is "before it thinks, not actually any real thing" (*DA* III.4, 429a23–24, J. A. Smith).

*De Anima* III.5 then distinguishes a mind like matter, which becomes all things, from a mind like a cause, which makes all things, as light makes potential colours actual. This second mind "is separable, impassible, unmixed, since it is in its essential nature activity" (*DA* III.5, 430a17–18, J. A. Smith), and it alone is immortal. The chapter is less than a page long and has divided readers ever since. Alexander of Aphrodisias identified this **active intellect** with God; Averroes made it one separate intellect shared by all humans; Aquinas, against Averroes, made it a power of each human soul. The dossier flags it as disputed (Argument 9 in [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md)).

## First philosophy and the unmoved mover

The *Metaphysics* opens: "All men by nature desire to know" (*Met.* A.1, 980a21, Ross). The desire climbs from sensation through experience and craft to wisdom, knowledge of first causes, pursued by what Aristotle calls **first philosophy**. ("Metaphysics," the library entry notes, is an editor's label.) He describes this science in two ways. In Γ it studies being as being, and so substance as the focal case. In E and Λ it is theology, the study of unchangeable substance.

Book Z follows the first path. It tests four candidates for a thing's substance: the essence, the universal, the genus and the underlying subject (*hypokeimenon*). Z.3 shows that if substance were the underlying subject, stripping away every attribute would leave bare matter. But matter lacks the two marks of substance, which Ross renders "separability and 'thisness'" (*Met.* Z.3, 1029a27–28): it cannot exist on its own, and it is no definite individual. Z.13 argues that "it seems impossible that any universal term should be the name of a substance" (*Met.* Z.13, 1038b8–9, Ross), because a thing's substance is peculiar to it while a universal is common. Z.17 makes a fresh start. Substance is what makes these elements flesh and those letters a syllable: "the primary cause of its being" (*Met.* Z.17, 1041b27–28, Ross). The winner is form, the essence, in Aristotle's phrase *to ti ēn einai*, "the what-it-was-to-be" (Argument 10 in [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md)).

Book Λ follows the second path. Change and time are eternal; eternal change needs an eternal source; a source with unrealised potential might fail to act. So there is a substance whose being is pure actuality, without matter. It cannot move the heavens by pushing, since pushing would change it. It moves them as the loved moves the lover: the final cause "produces motion as being loved, but all other things move by being moved" (*Met.* Λ.7, 1072b3–4, Ross). Its activity must be the best there is, thought, and the best object of thought is itself: "its thinking is a thinking on thinking" (*Met.* Λ.9, 1074b34–35, Ross). Λ.8 adds forty-seven or fifty-five further movers for the planetary spheres. As the library entry stresses, this god creates nothing, knows no particulars and cares for no one (Argument 11 in [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md)).

## The good life: eudaimonia, virtue, phronesis

Ethics is, for Aristotle, part of political science, and it asks what final good human life aims at. *Eudaimonia* means flourishing, not a feeling. The function argument of *NE* I.7 gives it content: a thing's good lies in doing its characteristic work (*ergon*) well, the human work is rational activity, so "human good turns out to be activity of soul in accordance with virtue," and over a complete life, since "one swallow does not make a summer" (*NE* I.7, 1098a16–18, Ross). The ladder of soul-powers from *De Anima* runs inside the argument, and *eudaimonia* is an *energeia*, an activity, not a possession (Argument 12 in [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md); the guide's [reconstruction (a)](../../study/aristotle-nicomachean-ethics.md)).

**Virtue** (*aretē*), excellence, comes in two kinds matching two sides of the soul: the part that has reason and the part of desire and feeling that can listen to it. Virtues of intellect come mainly by teaching, virtues of character by habit. Here the theory of nature sets a precise limit: "Neither by nature, then, nor contrary to nature do the virtues arise in us; rather we are adapted by nature to receive them, and are made perfect by habit" (*NE* II.1, 1103a23–26, Ross). A stone cannot be trained to fall upward; a human can be trained to be brave, because the capacity is natural and its completion is not. "We become just by doing just acts" (1103a34–b1), as builders become builders by building.

The definition of character virtue follows: "Virtue, then, is a state of character concerned with choice, lying in a mean, i.e. the mean relative to us, this being determined by a rational principle, and by that principle by which the man of practical wisdom would determine it" (*NE* II.6, 1106b36–1107a2, Ross). The **mean** is not a midpoint or moderation. It is the right feeling and action, at the right time, toward the right people, for the right reason, and some acts, such as murder and adultery, have no mean at all (Argument 13; the guide's [reconstruction (b)](../../study/aristotle-nicomachean-ethics.md)).

The definition hands the last word to the practically wise person, and Book VI says what that person has. **Practical wisdom** (*phronēsis*) is "a true and reasoned state of capacity to act with regard to the things that are good or bad for man" (*NE* VI.5, 1140b4–6, Ross). It differs from scientific knowledge (*epistēmē*), which concerns what cannot be otherwise, and from craft (*technē*), which aims at a product beyond itself; good action is its own end. Practical wisdom and character virtue need each other: without virtue, practical wisdom is mere cleverness; without practical wisdom, virtue stays the untutored "natural virtue" of a bold child. Book VII's *akrasia*, acting against one's better judgement, tests the joint (Argument 14). Book X then ranks contemplation (*theōria*), the activity of *nous*, above the practical life: "If reason is divine, then, in comparison with man, the life according to it is divine in comparison with human life" (*NE* X.7, 1177b30–31, Ross). The ethics here reaches up to the god of Λ, whose whole life is such thinking (Argument 15).

## The polis as natural

The *Ethics* ends by turning to legislation, and the *Politics* opens by tying the city to the physics. Households form villages, villages form the *polis*, the self-sufficient community, "originating in the bare needs of life, and continuing in existence for the sake of a good life" (*Pol.* I.2, 1252b29–30, Jowett). The argument applies the definition of nature directly: the city is the end of the earlier associations, "and the nature of a thing is its end" (1252b32). "Hence it is evident that the state is a creation of nature, and that man is by nature a political animal" (1253a2–3, Jowett). Humans are political in a fuller sense than bees because they have speech (*logos*), which can show the just and the unjust, not merely signal pain. A being without a city is either a beast or a god.

The city is also prior by nature to the individual, as the body is to the hand: a severed hand is a hand in name only, like the statue's eye. The rest of the *Politics* classifies constitutions by whether one, few or many rule, and whether for the common good; defines the citizen by sharing in judging and ruling (Book III); and sketches the best city's education (Books VII–VIII). The same naturalism yields natural slavery (*Pol.* I.4–7): some humans, Aristotle claims, share in reason enough to grasp it but not to have it, and are better off ruled. Argument 16 in [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md) reconstructs and criticises it. It shows what a teleology of natural kinds can be made to justify when its premises about who has which capacity are false.

## Techne, rhetoric and mimesis

**Craft** (*technē*) is a reasoned capacity to make, and nature was explained by analogy with it. It is also the first form of knowledge that grasps the "why": unlike the merely experienced, the craftsman can say why a remedy works and can teach it (*Met.* A.1). Two treatises apply the concept to arts of speech.

The *Rhetoric* defends persuasion as a craft, against Plato's charge in the *Gorgias* that it is a knack: "Rhetoric may be defined as the faculty of observing in any given case the available means of persuasion" (*Rhet.* I.2, 1355b25–26, Rhys Roberts). Its proofs are of three kinds: the speaker's character (*ēthos*), the hearer's emotion (*pathos*) and the argument (*logos*). Its typical argument is the **enthymeme**, a syllogism from premises that hold for the most part. Book II's account of the emotions, each defined by its object, cause and pleasure or pain, is moral psychology as much as advice.

The *Poetics* starts from nature: humans are the most imitative of animals, learn first by imitating, and enjoy imitations (*Poet.* 4). **Imitation** (*mimēsis*) represents human action in words, rhythm and song, and tragedy is its highest form: "an imitation of an action that is serious, complete, and of a certain magnitude... through pity and fear effecting the proper purgation of these emotions" (*Poet.* 6, 1449b24–28, Butcher). Plot is its soul. The best plots turn on reversal and recognition, and on a protagonist neither villain nor saint who falls through some error (*hamartia*, *Poet.* 13). Whether *katharsis* means purgation, purification or clarification is still open. Against Plato's charge that imitation stands at third remove from truth, chapter 9 replies: "Poetry, therefore, is a more philosophical and a higher thing than history: for poetry tends to express the universal, history the particular" (*Poet.* 9, 1451b5–7, Butcher).

## How the pieces interlock

Read in order, the concepts form a chain. The categories make substance the focal case of being. Hylomorphism analyses a substance into matter and form; the four causes say what a full explanation of it names; potentiality and actuality say how it can change. Nature is form as an inner source of change toward an end. Soul is the form of a living body, with nested powers, and its highest power, *nous*, links human thought to the pure actuality of the unmoved mover. The human good is the excellent activity of the soul's distinctive power; virtue is a natural capacity completed by habit; practical wisdom is reason applied to action; the polis is the natural end in which that activity is complete; and craft, rhetoric and poetry are reason applied to making. Three priorities recur at every link: form over matter, actuality over potentiality, end over process. A good self-test is to name, at any link, which earlier concept it borrows.

## Key terms

In the format of the repo [glossary](../../glossary.md), restricted to Aristotle's own usage, with misleading textbook senses flagged where they exist.

- **Ousia (substance):** what is most fundamentally real; in the *Categories* the individual, neither said of nor present in a subject (*Cat.* 5); in *Metaphysics* Z the form that makes a thing what it is. Not "stuff," as in a chemical substance: closer to "being-ness."
- **Kategoriai (categories):** the ten highest kinds of predicate: substance, quantity, quality, relation, place, time, position, state, action, affection (*Cat.* 4). Not Kant's categories, which are forms of judgement in the mind.
- **Hypokeimenon (subject, substrate):** "what lies under": the subject of attributes and the matter that persists through change. *Metaphysics* Z.3 shows it cannot alone be substance.
- **Hylē (matter):** what a thing is made of, relative to a level: bricks for a house, clay for bricks. A role in an explanation, that which is potentially this, not "physical stuff" in the modern sense.
- **Morphē / eidos (form):** the organisation or essence that makes matter a thing of a kind. *Eidos* is Plato's word for Form, deliberately kept, but Aristotle's form is in the thing, not apart from it.
- **To ti ēn einai (essence):** "the what-it-was-to-be" a thing, stated by its definition; in *Metaphysics* Z the leading candidate for substance.
- **Aitia (cause, explanation):** an answer to a "why?" question, of four kinds: material, formal, efficient, final (*Phys.* II.3). Only the efficient cause resembles the modern "cause" as a prior event.
- **Telos (end):** that for the sake of which; the final cause. In natural things, the mature form and activity that growth runs toward, not a goal anyone intends.
- **Dunamis (potentiality, capacity):** a power to change or be changed, or to be otherwise. The root of "dynamic," but it names a capacity *not* being exercised.
- **Energeia (actuality, activity):** "being at work"; the exercise or realisation of a capacity, prior to potentiality (*Met.* Θ.8). Not "energy" in the physicist's sense.
- **Entelecheia (actuality, fulfilment):** probably Aristotle's coinage, "having one's end within"; close to *energeia* but leaning toward the completed state. The soul is a body's first *entelecheia* (*DA* II.1).
- **Phusis (nature):** an inner source of change and rest in a thing (*Phys.* II.1), above all its form. Not nature as the environment: each natural thing has its own nature.
- **Psychē (soul):** the form and first actuality of a natural organised body, so plants have souls. Not a soul imprisoned in a body: it is to the body as the stamp's shape is to the wax.
- **Nous (intellect):** the power by which the soul thinks; "not actually any real thing" before it thinks (*DA* III.4); in III.5, the disputed "separable" active intellect; in *NE* VI and the *Posterior Analytics*, the grasp of first principles. Narrower than the modern "mind": perception and imagination are separate powers.
- **Endoxa (reputable opinions):** the views of the many or the wise that inquiry starts from and tries to save. Not majority vote: the wise count more, and every opinion is tested.
- **Eudaimonia (flourishing):** activity of soul in accordance with virtue over a complete life (*NE* I.7). An activity judged over a lifetime, not a feeling of happiness.
- **Ergon (function, characteristic work):** what a thing characteristically does when doing well, as seeing is the eye's work. Not a job assigned by a designer.
- **Aretē (virtue, excellence):** what makes a thing do its work well; a knife has its *aretē*. In humans, of character or of intellect; broader than the moral English "virtue."
- **Hexis (state, disposition):** a settled condition built by repeated action; virtue of character is a *hexis* concerned with choice (*NE* II.6). Not a mechanical habit: it includes knowing and choosing.
- **Mesotēs (the mean):** the intermediate relative to us between excess and deficiency, set as the practically wise person would set it (*NE* II.6). Not moderation, and not a midpoint.
- **Phronēsis (practical wisdom):** a "true and reasoned state of capacity to act" about human goods (*NE* VI.5); it needs experience. Not prudence in the modern sense of caution.
- **Technē (craft, art):** a reasoned capacity to make, aimed at a product beyond itself, able to say why and to teach. Covers carpentry, medicine and rhetoric, not just fine art.
- **Mimēsis (imitation, representation):** the representation of human action, natural to humans and a way of learning (*Poet.* 4). Unlike Plato's copy at third remove, it can express the universal (*Poet.* 9).

## Known internal tensions

The interlock is real but not seamless, and the strains drive the scholarship ([06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md) gives their history).

**(1) Is form universal or particular?** *Metaphysics* Z seems to want two things at once. Substance is what definition and knowledge are of, and those are of universals. Yet Z.13 says no universal can be substance, since a thing's substance is peculiar to it. If form is substance and universal, Z.13 is broken. If form is particular, Socrates' form distinct from Callias', then knowledge seems to be of something other than substance. Michael Frede and Günther Patzig (1988), among others, argue for particular forms. Michael Woods, Frank Lewis and Alan Code, among others, defend a form that is general without being a "universal" of the kind Z.13 attacks. Others think Aristotle never fully resolved the puzzle, which he raises again in *Metaphysics* M.10 (Argument 10).

**(2) Two accounts of substance, and two sciences?** The *Categories* makes the individual compound primary; Z makes form primary. Developmental readers see a revision; others see two works answering different questions. Similarly, first philosophy is both the study of being as being (Γ) and theology (E, Λ). Aristotle's bridge is that the study of the first substance is universal "in this way, because it is first" (*Met.* E.1, 1026a30–31, Ross). Whether that unifies the science or covers a seam is still argued.

**(3) The intellect against hylomorphism.** If soul is the body's form, it should perish with the body, and Aristotle says so of most of it. Yet *DA* III.4–5 makes thought organless and the active intellect deathless. Either *nous* is the one exception, or III.5 is about something other than the individual mind. A modern version asks whether Aristotle's psychology anticipates functionalism, as Martha Nussbaum and Hilary Putnam argued, or whether, as Myles Burnyeat argued in "Is an Aristotelian Philosophy of Mind Still Credible?" (1992), its physics of perception is too alien to be revived.

**(4) Contemplation against the practical life.** *NE* I–IX present flourishing as social and practical; X.7–8 put contemplation first and the life of the other virtues happy "in a secondary degree" (X.8, 1178a9, Ross). **Inclusive** readings take *eudaimonia* as a whole made of several goods; **dominant** readings organise it around contemplation. Both find support in the text, and the study guide, like this dossier, leaves the dispute open (Argument 15).

**(5) Teleology made political.** The argument from nature that grounds the city also grounds natural slavery and the subordination of women (*Pol.* I). Critics ask whether those claims can be removed without damage, or whether they expose a method that starts from the reputable opinions of a slave-holding society. The library entry records both answers, and Argument 16 tests them. For where these positions sit among the repo's nine problems, see [themes.md](../../themes.md).
