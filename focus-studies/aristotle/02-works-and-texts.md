# Works and Texts: The Corpus in Reading Order

Aristotle's bibliography has the opposite shape to Plato's. Plato's polished dialogues survive entire; Aristotle's polished works are the ones that are lost. He wrote dialogues for the public in his Academy years, works Cicero praised for their style, and little of them remains beyond fragments quoted by later authors. What survives are the **treatises**: lecture texts, drafts and research files written for the school's inner circle, revised over years and assembled into their present books by later editors. The Stanford Encyclopedia counts thirty-one surviving works judged authentic. Their prose is compressed and technical, and the library entry's warning stands at the head of this file: [these are not finished books](../../eras/01-ancient/aristotle.md). Repetitions, abrupt turns and apparent contradictions are partly marks of how the texts were made.

How they reached us is part of the story. In the century after Aristotle's death the treatises seem to have dropped out of circulation, and they reappear in the first century BCE. Strabo and Plutarch tell how: manuscripts inherited by Theophrastus' heir Neleus, hidden in a cellar at Scepsis, sold to the collector Apellicon, and taken to Rome by Sulla. That is a late anecdote, weighed in [01-life-and-times.md](01-life-and-times.md). What is firm is that our corpus descends from a first-century BCE edition attributed to **Andronicus of Rhodes**, who arranged the works in an order of study: logic, then natural science, then the *Metaphysics*, then ethics and politics, with the *Rhetoric* and *Poetics* at the end. It is a teaching order, not a chronology. This dossier follows it, with one swap explained below, because each subject in it builds on the one before.

## A note on citation before the works

Aristotle is cited by **Bekker numbers**. In 1831 the Prussian Academy at Berlin published the Greek text of the whole corpus, edited by Immanuel Bekker, in two volumes paginated continuously from page 1 (the opening of the *Categories*) to page 1462 (the end of the *Poetics*). Each page has two columns, **a** and **b**, and each column numbers its lines. So **1106b36** means page 1106, right-hand column, line 36: the *Nicomachean Ethics*' definition of virtue as a mean. Every serious modern translation prints these numbers in the margin, as Plato's translations print Stephanus numbers. The lines are lines of Greek, so in English a cited line sits within a line or two of its marginal mark.

The dossier's form is *work + book and chapter + Bekker number*: *NE* II.6, 1106b36. The book and chapter divisions are later than Aristotle but universal. The *Metaphysics* names its fourteen books by Greek letters, while some editions and the library entry use numbers, so keep the key: A (I), α (II, "little alpha"), B (III), Γ (IV), Δ (V), E (VI), Z (VII), H (VIII), Θ (IX), I (X), K (XI), Λ (XII), M (XIII), N (XIV). *Metaphysics* Z is the library entry's Book VII; Λ is its Book XII. The *Poetics*, a single book, is cited by chapter (*Poet.* 6, 1449b24). Abbreviations: *Cat.*, *De Int.*, *APr.* and *APo.* (*Prior* and *Posterior Analytics*), *Phys.*, *DA* (*De Anima*), *PN* (*Parva Naturalia*), *HA*, *PA*, *GA* (*History*, *Parts*, *Generation of Animals*), *Met.*, *NE*, *EE*, *MM*, *Pol.*, *Rhet.*, *Poet.*

## Dating the treatises, and what is not Aristotle's

Dating Aristotle is harder than dating Plato. The treatises were reworked across decades, so one book can hold layers of different dates, and cross-references between works point both ways. Three frames help. The first is the life: the Academy (c. 367–347 BCE), Assos and Lesbos (c. 347–343), Macedon (from 343), the Lyceum (335–323). The second is Werner Jaeger's developmental thesis (1923), which read the corpus as a journey from early Platonist to late empirical researcher; it shaped a generation of scholarship and has been heavily criticised since. The third is local evidence: H. D. P. Lee's study of place names (1948) tied many biological observations to the region around Lesbos, which supports placing much of the animal research between Aristotle's two Athenian periods. Dates below are scholarly estimates, marked "probably" or "c.".

The manuscripts also carry works that are not Aristotle's: the *Problems*, *On the Cosmos*, *On Colours*, the *Mechanics*, *On Virtues and Vices* and the *Rhetoric to Alexander* are generally assigned to followers or later writers. The *Constitution of the Athenians*, recovered on papyrus in 1890 and published in 1891, is attributed to his school. Of the lost early works, the *Protrepticus*, the dialogue *Eudemus*, *On Philosophy* and *On the Ideas* survive in fragments and reports. *On the Ideas* carried his detailed objections to Plato's Forms, the Third Man among them, known largely through Alexander of Aphrodisias' commentary on the *Metaphysics* (c. 200 CE). The disputed *Magna Moralia* is treated with the ethics. Nothing on the weekly reading list is on the doubtful shelf.

## The works in the dossier's reading order

### 1. The Organon: logic and categories (Week 1)

*Organon* means "instrument". The title is not Aristotle's: later Peripatetics gave it to the six logical works, on the view that logic is the tool every science uses. The six are the *Categories*, *De Interpretatione*, *Prior* and *Posterior Analytics*, *Topics* and *Sophistical Refutations*. Their dates are uncertain; the *Categories* and *Topics* are often thought early.

The **Categories** sorts what we talk about into ten kinds: substance, quantity, quality, relation, place, time, position, having, doing and being affected. Individual things, this man or this horse, are the primary beings on which everything else depends. In Edghill's translation, "Substance, in the truest and primary and most definite sense of the word, is that which is neither predicable of a subject nor present in a subject" (*Cat.* 5, 2a11–13). That is the first great anti-Platonic move: the individual, not the universal Form, is most real. **Difficulty:** low to moderate on the page, large in consequence. The authenticity of the second half (chapters 10–15) has been questioned; the dossier reads 1–5.

**De Interpretatione** studies the statement: names, verbs, affirmation and denial, and how contradictories pair off. Chapter 9 is the **sea battle**: if it is true today that there will be a sea battle tomorrow, is tomorrow already fixed? Aristotle tries to keep the future open without giving up logic, and what exactly his answer is remains disputed. **Difficulty:** moderate; chapter 9 is among the hardest short texts in ancient philosophy.

The **Prior Analytics** invents the **syllogism**, a deduction whose conclusion follows necessarily from two premises, studied with letters for terms. It founds formal logic. It is not weekly reading here; [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md) reconstructs the syllogism. The **Posterior Analytics** asks what scientific knowledge (*epistēmē*) is: demonstration from true, primary, explanatory first principles. Its last chapter, II.19, asks how we grasp those principles, and climbs from perception to memory to experience to *nous*, intellectual insight. **Difficulty:** high. The **Topics** and **Sophistical Refutations**, not on this plan, cover dialectic, arguing from *endoxa* (reputable opinions), and fallacies.

**Place:** the tools come first; every later week uses them.

### 2. Natural philosophy: the Physics (Week 2)

The **Physics** (eight books) is the general theory of nature (*physis*): whatever has in itself a source of change and rest. Book I finds the principles of change in matter, form and privation. Book II defines nature, sets out the **four causes** (material, formal, efficient, final), treats chance, and in II.8 defends teleology against Empedocles, who held that useful organs arose by accident. III.1 defines **change** as the actualization of the potential as such. IV–VI treat place, void, time, continuity and Zeno; VII is short and perhaps earlier; VIII argues that motion is eternal and needs a first unmoved mover. The work is probably a lecture course revised over time. **Difficulty:** Book II moderate and the best entry; III.1 very compressed; VIII heavy, so the plan takes it in summary. *On the Heavens*, *On Coming-to-Be and Passing Away* and the *Meteorology* apply the theory to cosmos and elements and are not on the plan.

**Place:** the four causes and the potential/actual pair return in every later week.

### 3. The biology (Week 3)

The biological works are a large share of what survives. The **History of Animals** (ten books, the last generally doubted) collects data on hundreds of species. The **Parts of Animals** (four books) explains why animals have the parts they have, mostly by final causes; its first book is an essay on method, including a defence of zoology against those who find animals beneath philosophy. We should study every animal without distaste, in Ogle's translation, "for each and all will reveal to us something natural and something beautiful" (*PA* I.5, 645a21–23). The **Generation of Animals** (five books) explains reproduction and inheritance by matter and form; its claim that the female supplies the matter and the male the form is an argued position, named here plainly. **Date:** the research probably belongs largely to the Assos and Lesbos years; the writing-up may be later. **Difficulty:** *PA* I moderate and philosophical; *HA* easy but long; *GA* hard.

**Place:** the four causes at work on real organisms. The plan reads the biology before *De Anima*, the one swap in Andronicus' order, because the soul is defined as the form of the living bodies the biology studies.

### 4. Soul: De Anima and the Parva Naturalia (Week 4)

**De Anima** (*On the Soul*, three books) treats the soul (*psychē*) as what makes a living thing alive, not as a ghost in the body. Book I surveys earlier views. In J. A. Smith's translation of II.1, the soul is "the first grade of actuality of a natural body having life potentially in it" (*DA* II.1, 412a27–28): the body's organised capacity to live, as sight is the eye's capacity to see. Book II ranks the capacities (nutrition, perception, thought) and treats perception as receiving form without matter. Book III covers imagination (*phantasia*) and thought; III.4–5 distinguishes an intellect that becomes all things from one that makes all things. III.5, a few lines long, is the most disputed text in the corpus: Alexander of Aphrodisias identified the "making" intellect with god, Averroes held it was one intellect shared by all humans, Aquinas made it a power of each soul. The dossier flags it as disputed. **Difficulty:** Book II moderate; III.4–5 very high.

The **Parva Naturalia** ("short natural treatises") cover capacities soul and body share: sense, memory and recollection, sleep, dreams, length of life, respiration. They are not weekly reading but are the natural next read. **Date** of both: uncertain.

**Place:** the bridge from biology to mind, and the source of the view of the person Aquinas built on.

### 5. First philosophy: the Metaphysics (Week 5)

The **Metaphysics** is a composite of fourteen books. Its title, "after the physics", is a later editors' label; Aristotle calls the subject first philosophy, wisdom or theology. Book A opens with the claim, in Ross's translation, "ALL men by nature desire to know" (*Met.* A.1, 980a21), traces wisdom from experience, surveys earlier thinkers' causes, and ends with the critique of Plato's Forms in A.9, reconstructed in [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md). B is a book of puzzles. Γ defines the science of being as being and defends the **principle of non-contradiction** (Γ.3–4). Δ is a philosophical dictionary. The central books Z, H and Θ ask what substance (*ousia*) is, answer on most readings with form, and develop actuality and potentiality. Λ argues for an eternal unmoved mover whose activity is thought thinking itself (Λ.6–10). M and N return to mathematics and the Forms; K partly duplicates other books and is partly doubted. Jaeger read Λ as an early free-standing treatise, which remains debated. **Difficulty:** the highest in the corpus, Z above all. The learning path's rule holds: never start with the full *Metaphysics*. Here it comes in Week 5, in selection.

**Place:** the summit and the centre of the quarrel with Plato. Whether its subject is being in general or divine substance is a live debate.

### 6. The ethical works (Weeks 6–7)

The **Nicomachean Ethics** (ten books) is Aristotle's most read work and the subject of the library's [study guide](../../study/aristotle-nicomachean-ethics.md), which this file follows rather than restates. Its arc: the human good is *eudaimonia*, flourishing, defined by the function argument (I.7); virtue of character is a habit-formed disposition lying in a mean (II); voluntary action and the particular virtues (III–IV); justice (V); intellectual virtue and *phronēsis*, practical wisdom (VI); *akrasia*, weakness of will, and pleasure (VII); friendship (VIII–IX); pleasure and the contemplative life (X). It says early what kind of book it is: in Ross's translation, "precision is not to be sought for alike in all discussions" (*NE* I.3, 1094b12–13). The title is probably from Aristotle's son Nicomachus. **Date:** usually the Lyceum years. **Difficulty:** moderate; V and VI the most technical.

The **Eudemian Ethics**, perhaps named for the student Eudemus of Rhodes, covers much the same ground. Its Books IV–VI are identical with *NE* V–VII, the **common books**. Which treatise they first belonged to, and which treatise came first, are open. The Stanford Encyclopedia reports the widespread view that the *Nicomachean* is the later, improved version; Anthony Kenny (1978) argued the reverse and claimed the common books for the *Eudemian*. The dossier reads the *Nicomachean* as primary, as the study guide does.

The **Magna Moralia** ("Great Ethics", named for its two unusually long rolls) is the disputed third treatise, covering the same topics more briefly. Some scholars hold it is an early Aristotelian course, perhaps a student's record; others, a post-Aristotelian compilation. Cicero and Diogenes Laertius, who might be expected to know it, do not mention it. The dossier rests nothing on it.

**Place:** the natural philosophy's teleology applied to human life, leading into the *Politics*, which the *NE*'s last chapter announces.

### 7. Politics (Week 8)

The **Politics** (eight books) studies the *polis*, the self-governing city. Book I argues that the city exists by nature: in Jowett's translation, "the state is a creation of nature, and that man is by nature a political animal" (*Pol.* I.2, 1253a2–3). The same book defends natural slavery and the rule of husbands over wives, positions [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md) reconstructs and then criticises. Book II reviews ideal constitutions, Plato's among them; III defines the citizen and classifies constitutions by who rules and for whose good; IV–VI study actual regimes, drawing on the school's collection of 158 constitutions; VII–VIII sketch the best city and its education. The order of the books is disputed, and some editors move VII–VIII after III. **Date:** probably composed and revised over the Lyceum years. **Difficulty:** moderate; III the most demanding of the plan's selections.

**Place:** the completion of the ethics, and the long reply to Plato's *Republic*.

### 8. Rhetoric (Week 9)

The **Rhetoric** (three books) treats persuasion as an art, the counterpart of dialectic. In Rhys Roberts' translation, "Rhetoric may be defined as the faculty of observing in any given case the available means of persuasion" (*Rhet.* I.2, 1355b26–27). I.2 names three kinds of proof: the speaker's character (*ēthos*), the hearer's emotions (*pathos*) and the argument (*logos*), whose rhetorical form is the **enthymeme**, a compressed syllogism. II.2–11 analyses the emotions one by one: anger, calm, friendliness, fear, shame, pity, indignation, envy and others. Book III treats style and arrangement. **Date:** the core is often thought to go back to Academy teaching, revised later. **Difficulty:** low to moderate.

**Place:** the reply to Plato's *Gorgias*, which called rhetoric flattery, and the bridge from the ethics' account of emotion to the *Poetics*.

### 9. Poetics (Week 9)

The **Poetics** survives as one book of twenty-six chapters on tragedy and epic; the promised book on comedy is lost. Chapter 6 defines tragedy, in Butcher's translation, as "an imitation of an action that is serious, complete, and of a certain magnitude" (*Poet.* 6, 1449b24–25), achieving through pity and fear the *katharsis* of such emotions, a word still argued over: purging, purification or clarification. Chapters 6–9 put plot first among tragedy's six parts, require unity, and claim poetry is more philosophical than history because it speaks of universals. Chapters 13–14 describe the best plot: a person neither wholly good nor bad who falls through *hamartia*, an error. **Date:** probably the Lyceum years. **Difficulty:** low to moderate, though compressed and corrupt in places.

**Place:** the reply to Plato's banishment of poetry in *Republic* X, and the dossier's last primary text.

## Editions and translations

The rule is the [learning path's §4](../../learning-path.md): buy by translator, prefer Hackett, and do not take Ross as a first read. As with Plato, stay with one translation through each work, because Aristotle's technical terms vary between translators.

**The reference set** is **Jonathan Barnes (ed.), *The Complete Works of Aristotle: The Revised Oxford Translation*** (Princeton, 1984, two volumes), with every genuine and doubtful work and Bekker numbers throughout. It is for checking and for works the course texts lack: its translations are revised versions of the early twentieth-century Oxford translations, without notes. A newer set, **Reeve and Kontos (eds.), *Aristotle: Complete Works*** (Hackett, two volumes, December 2025), aims at consistent terms across the corpus; the dossier notes it and keeps Barnes as its named reference.

**The course texts** are the learning path's Hackett *Readings*, **Cohen, Curd and Reeve (eds.), *Readings in Ancient Greek Philosophy*** (fifth edition, 2016), and C. D. C. Reeve's **New Hackett Aristotle** translations, each with introduction and extensive notes.

- **Week 1:** the *Readings* has *Categories* 1–5, *De Interpretatione* 9 and *Posterior Analytics* II.19. Going further: J. L. Ackrill, *Categories and De Interpretatione* (Clarendon, 1963).
- **Week 2:** the *Readings* has *Physics* II whole, III.1–3 and VIII.5–6; Reeve's *Physics* (2018) supplies the rest of VIII.
- **Week 3:** the *Readings* has *Parts of Animals* I.1 and I.5; Reeve's volume *Generation of Animals & History of Animals I, Parts of Animals I* covers the other selections; later *HA* books are in Barnes.
- **Week 4:** Reeve's *De Anima* (2017), or the *Readings* (II.1–6, III.3–7); the *Parva Naturalia* in Barnes.
- **Week 5:** the *Readings* first, as the path rules, then Reeve's *Metaphysics* (2016) for chapters the anthology omits, among them Γ.4, Λ.6 and Λ.8.
- **Weeks 6–7:** the *NE* in **Terence Irwin** (third edition, 2019) or **Reeve**, both Hackett, as the path and study guide rule. *Eudemian Ethics*: Reeve (Hackett, 2021), Kenny (Oxford World's Classics, 2011, with the common books) or Inwood and Woolf (Cambridge Texts, 2013). The *Magna Moralia* is in Barnes's second volume.
- **Week 8:** Reeve's *Politics* (2017), or the *Readings* (I, III, VII.1–3, 13, 15).
- **Week 9:** Reeve's *Rhetoric* (2018); the *Poetics* in Richard Janko's annotated translation (Hackett, 1987). The path is silent on both, so these are the dossier's rulings; Malcolm Heath's *Poetics* (Penguin Classics, 1996) is a good modern alternative.

**Avoid, as a first read,** the old Oxford translations on their own: Ross for the *Ethics* and *Metaphysics*, as the path rules, and, by this dossier's extension, Jowett's *Politics* and its companions. They are accurate but terse, dated in idiom and unannotated. Avoid any cheap reprint that drops the Bekker numbers, without which no citation here can be found. And keep the path's other warning: do not start with the *Physics* or the full *Metaphysics*.

One apparent conflict needs a word. The dossier's **quotations** come from exactly those public-domain Oxford translations (Ross, Jowett, J. A. Smith, Hardie and Gaye, Edghill, Mure, Ogle, Rhys Roberts, Butcher), always with the translator named. That rule is about checking, not reading: their full texts are free online, so every quotation can be verified word for word, while the books for study are the Hackett ones above.

## The reading order

The path's own Aristotle block (Stage 1, row 4) opens with the *Ethics*. This dossier deepens that block in the school order instead: tools, nature, life and soul, first philosophy, then action, the city and the arts of speech.

1. **Week 1, logic and categories:** *Categories* 1–5; *De Interpretatione* 9; *Posterior Analytics* II.19.
2. **Week 2, nature:** *Physics* II whole; III.1; VIII in summary.
3. **Week 3, biology:** *Parts of Animals* I (especially I.1 and I.5); *History of Animals* selections; *Generation of Animals* selections.
4. **Week 4, soul:** *De Anima* II.1–5; III.4–5.
5. **Week 5, first philosophy:** *Metaphysics* A.1–2; Γ.3–4; Z selections (Z.1, Z.3, Z.17); Λ.6–10.
6. **Week 6, ethics I:** *Nicomachean Ethics* I–III, with the study guide.
7. **Week 7, ethics II:** *Nicomachean Ethics* V–VII and X, with the study guide.
8. **Week 8, politics:** *Politics* I and III; VII selections.
9. **Week 9, rhetoric and poetics, and the capstone:** *Rhetoric* I.1–2 and II selections (the emotions); *Poetics* 1–14 (especially 6–9 and 13–14).
