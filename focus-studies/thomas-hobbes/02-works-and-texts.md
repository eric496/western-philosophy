# Works and Texts: The Corpus in Reading Order

*Focus study: Thomas Hobbes · [Dossier home](README.md) · Library entry: [Thomas Hobbes](../../eras/03-early-modern/thomas-hobbes.md) · Study guide: [*Leviathan* chs. 13–17+](../../study/hobbes-leviathan.md) · Arguments in full: [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md)*

Hobbes published for half a century, from a translation of Thucydides in 1629 to the verse autobiography of his last year, and much of what he wrote reached print in a form he did not choose. Several of his books were printed without his consent, some only after his death, and two of the most important exist in an English and a Latin version that are not the same book. The SEP sorts the philosophical output into three systematic statements that share one plan: *The Elements of Law*, the three-part *Elements of Philosophy* (*De Corpore*, *De Homine* and *De Cive*), and *Leviathan*. Each starts from mind and language and works toward politics. Around them stand the translations (Thucydides, and in the 1670s Homer), the controversies (with Descartes, with Bishop John Bramhall, with the mathematicians John Wallis and Seth Ward, with Robert Boyle), and the late histories and dialogues written when he was barred from publishing on politics and religion in England.

Two facts shape how the corpus is read. First, Hobbes stated his political philosophy three times, in 1640, 1642 and 1651, and then revised the last statement in Latin in 1668. The IEP says that for the essentials one can rely on *De Cive* and *Leviathan*, and that *De Cive* gives the more straightforward account. Johann Sommerville, introducing the new critical edition of *The Elements of Law* (Oxford University Press, 2024), argues that the later books mostly add to the 1640 argument or adapt it to new controversies rather than abandon it. So the useful habit is the one the Aquinas dossier recommends for parallel treatments: when a passage in *Leviathan* is hard, find its counterpart in the *Elements* or *De Cive* before deciding what Hobbes meant. Where the versions really differ (on authorization, on religion, on the state of nature's history), the difference is itself evidence, and [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md) gives the scholars who make most of it. Second, the text you quote has a history. The *Leviathan* of 1651 survives in three printings that all claim to be the first, the English *De Cive* of 1651 may not be Hobbes's own translation, and the *Elements* was split by its first printer into two books with new titles. The sections below say which text the dossier uses and why.

Quotations in this file keep the spelling of the saved public-domain texts: the 1651 spelling for *Leviathan*, and Molesworth's nineteenth-century spelling for the other English works. Nothing inside quotation marks is modernized.

## The forms Hobbes wrote in

**The treatise in numbered sections.** The *Elements of Law* and *De Cive* are divided into chapters and short numbered sections, in the manner of a geometry book. Hobbes's printer advertised the plan in exactly those terms in 1650: the three parts of the system follow one another "as the later Books of Euclid upon the former" (*Human Nature*, "To the Reader"). *Leviathan* keeps the chapters but drops the section numbers, which is why modern editions number its paragraphs for him.

**The paired English and Latin text.** Hobbes wrote the *Elements* and *Leviathan* in English and *De Cive*, *De Corpore* and *De Homine* in Latin, and then saw most of them into the other language, by his own hand or someone else's. A Latin reader on the Continent and an English reader at home met different books under the same name. The list of which version is authorial is part of the scholarship, not a footnote to it.

**The answer by numbers.** Hobbes's controversies were conducted by quoting the opponent's text section by section and replying to each. The Bramhall exchange is the clearest case: the 1656 *Questions* prints Bramhall's numbered paragraphs in full with Hobbes's "Animadversions" after each. The format makes these works long but easy to navigate, and it means that much of what we know of Bramhall's position comes to us through Hobbes's book.

**The dialogue.** In old age Hobbes turned to dialogue: *Behemoth* is four dialogues between an older and a younger speaker, the *Dialogue ... of the Common Laws* sets a Philosopher against a Student of the Common Laws of England, and the Latin *Leviathan* of 1668 ends with an Appendix in dialogue between speakers labelled A and B. Mark Murphy's review of the Clarendon *Dialogue* (Notre Dame Philosophical Reviews) passes on John Wallis's jibe that A and B should have been named Thomas and Hobbes. The dialogues restate doctrine more than they argue it, and the dossier reads them for application and context.

## The early works (1629–1641)

### The Thucydides translation (1629)

Hobbes's first published book was an English translation of Thucydides' *History of the Peloponnesian War*, made from the Greek. The SEP dates it 1629; the IEP gives 1628, which may reflect when the work was finished or entered rather than the title-page date (the 1891 *Dictionary of National Biography* also gives 1629, "after long delay"). The IEP reads the translation as a political act. Thucydides shows a democracy undone by demagogues, and Hobbes, writing as the quarrel between Charles I and Parliament sharpened in the late 1620s, offered him to English readers as a warning. The IEP also notes Hobbes's own later statement that the history teaches lessons useful for the defense of the king. **Difficulty:** none as philosophy; it is history in an excellent English. **Place:** the political education that preceded the system, and the source of the IEP's suggestion that Hobbes's picture of human nature came as much from reading history as from any science. Not set reading; the Clarendon Edition plans it as volume 23.

### *The Elements of Law, Natural and Politic* (1640; printed 1650)

The *Elements* is Hobbes's first statement of the whole system and the only one written in his own English throughout. He dated the dedication to William Cavendish, Earl of Newcastle, 9 May 1640, during the Short Parliament (Sommerville, OUP 2024, giving the date in both calendars as 9/19 May). Years later Hobbes recalled that the little treatise, "though not Printed", circulated in many copies and would have endangered his life had the king not dissolved Parliament (quoted by Sommerville from *Mr Hobbes considered*, 1662). Sommerville adds that Hobbes's 1662 account was written to stress his loyalty after the Restoration and may not be wholly accurate. Copies circulated in manuscript; Sommerville counts eleven early manuscripts known today, against the six known to Ferdinand Tönnies when he made the first proper edition in 1889.

The dedication states the method and the ambition. Learning divides into the mathematical, which is free of dispute, and the dogmatical, where "as oft as reason is against a man, so oft will a man be against reason"; Hobbes proposes to put the doctrine of justice and policy on principles passion cannot unsettle, and builds it accordingly. He apologizes for the plain style in a sentence that fits the whole corpus: "whilst I was writing, I consulted more with logic than with rhetoric" (EL, Epistle Dedicatory). Part I (19 chapters) runs from sense, imagination and speech through the passions to the state of nature and the laws of nature; Part II (10 chapters) builds the body politic, its kinds, and the sovereign's duties and rights, with chapters on religion.

**The 1650 split.** The *Elements* was first printed in 1650, without Hobbes's direction, as two separate books: *Human Nature, or the Fundamental Elements of Policy* and *De Corpore Politico, or the Elements of Law, Moral and Politic* (SEP, entry on Hobbes's moral and political philosophy; the 1891 DNB dates both 1650). The split does not follow Hobbes's division into two Parts. *Human Nature* stops at Part I chapter 13; *De Corpore Politico* begins with Part I chapter 14, on the state of nature, and continues through Part II. Molesworth reprinted the 1650 division in volume IV of the *English Works* (1840), and the saved text shows it: *De Corpore Politico* opens "In a former treatise of human nature already" and then turns to the natural condition of mankind. The printer's preface to *Human Nature*, signed "F. B.", presented the book as the middle part of Hobbes's system, the *De Homine*: of the three parts, "The last of these he hath already published in Latin beyond the seas; the second is this now presented" (*Human Nature*, "To the Reader"). It says the book came out "by a friend, with leave from him" (same). Whether Hobbes really gave leave, and whether he thought of the *Elements* as part of his three-part plan at all, are disputed. Tönnies held that it was written without regard to the plan, and J. C. A. Gaskin finds no hint that Hobbes saw its two parts as pieces of a larger structure. Richard Tuck and Deborah Baumgold hold that he had the plan by 1640. Noel Malcolm calls the *Elements* a piece written for an occasion (all as reported by Sommerville, OUP 2024). In 1684 the two halves were reprinted with *Of Liberty and Necessity* as *Hobbes's Tripos*, which is the order Molesworth followed.

The split matters for citation. The dossier cites the *Elements* in Tönnies's and Gaskin's numbering, part, chapter and section: EL I.14.11 is Part I, chapter 14, section 11. In Molesworth IV the same passage is *De Corpore Politico* Part I, chapter 1. Gaskin's Oxford World's Classics edition keeps the 1650 titles as names for the two Parts (its catalogue subtitle is "part I, Human nature, part II, De corpore politico"), so in Gaskin "Human Nature" covers all nineteen chapters of Part I, not the thirteen of the 1650 book. **Difficulty:** low to moderate; the prose is clear and the sections short. **Place:** Week 1 reads the Epistle Dedicatory and chapter 1. The *Elements* is the best check on *Leviathan* chapters 1–17: it argues the same chain, more briefly and without the theology.

### The Third Objections to Descartes's *Meditations* (1641)

In 1641 Descartes published the *Meditations* with six sets of objections and his replies; the third set is Hobbes's (SEP). Mersenne had already passed Hobbes's comments on Descartes's *Discourse* and *Optics* to Descartes in 1640, and the SEP describes the relation between the two as a mixture of respect and dismissal. The Third Objections are short and pointed: sixteen objections, each followed by a short reply, running from the First Objection on Meditation I to a Final Objection (Haldane and Ross, vol. II, 1912, print them under the running head "Objections III with Replies"). The most important for this dossier is the second, against the inference from "I think" to a thinking substance, and the objections on ideas, which show Hobbes's view that we have no idea of God or of the soul, only images and names. Haldane and Ross note that the French version of 1647 describes the objections as "urged by a Celebrated English Philosopher" (H&R II, Third Objections, note to the First Objection). **Difficulty:** low for Hobbes's side, moderate for Descartes's replies. **Place:** Week 2, with *Leviathan* chapters 1–6 (Arguments 1 and 3). Read Descartes's replies too: they are often curt, and the curtness is part of the record. The Clarendon Edition will print the Objections with Hobbes's unpublished critique of Thomas White's *De Mundo* (volumes 14–15).


## Paris: *De Cive* (1642, 1647; English 1651)

*De Cive* is the third part of the *Elements of Philosophy*, written and printed first because the politics could not wait. It came out in Paris in 1642 and was reissued at Amsterdam in 1647 as *Elementa Philosophiae de Cive*, with further Latin printings in 1650, 1660 and 1669 (DNB 1891, works list no. 4). The SEP notes that its three sections are titled "Liberty", "Empire" and "Religion", and that it focuses more narrowly on politics than the *Elements of Law*. The 1647 edition is the one modern translators follow. It added the Preface to the Reader and notes answering early critics (Tuck and Silverthorne's edition prints both; the details of what 1647 added are taken from memory and logged unchecked). The library entry calls it the compact statement of the theory, often clearer than *Leviathan* on the covenant, and the IEP agrees that it is the more straightforward account. It is also the source of the tag *bellum omnium contra omnes*, as the library entry says.

**The 1651 English and its translator.** In 1651 an English version appeared as *Philosophical Rudiments concerning Government and Society*. The DNB of 1891 says that Hobbes "published" it, and Molesworth printed it as Hobbes's in volume II of the *English Works*; the dossier quotes it from that volume. Its Preface sets out the plan of the book in one sentence: "In this book thou shalt find briefly described the duties of men" (*De Cive*, Preface), first as men, then as subjects, then as Christians. Whether the English is Hobbes's own is a real dispute. Howard Warrender's Clarendon edition printed it as a separate volume, *De Cive: The English Version*, and treated it as Hobbes's (from memory; logged unchecked). Noel Malcolm argued in 1998 that the translator was the poet Charles Cotton ("Charles Cotton, Translator of Hobbes's *De cive*", *Huntington Library Quarterly* 61, reprinted in *Aspects of Hobbes*, 2002; JSTOR record). Sommerville's 2024 Clarendon *Elements* accepts Cotton, and Cambridge's page for the Tuck and Silverthorne *On the Citizen* (1998) calls the 1651 English a rendering long thought, in error, to be at least sanctioned by Hobbes. The dossier's ruling follows from this: quote the 1651 English for its words, because it is public domain and of the period, but treat its wording as a contemporary translation, and check any argument that turns on a word against Tuck and Silverthorne. **Difficulty:** low; this is the clearest of the three political statements. **Place:** Week 3 reads the Preface to the Reader. Chapters 1–3 are the library entry's suggested shorter restatement of *Leviathan* 13–15.

## *Leviathan* (1651; Latin 1668)

Hobbes wrote *Leviathan* in Paris late in the 1640s, and it was printed in London by Andrew Crooke while he was still abroad. Columbia's Rare Book and Manuscript Library notes that Crooke's office sent him proofs but did not always wait for them, so copies of the first edition vary; it calls the large-paper copies, run last, the most authoritative. When Charles II reached Paris in 1651, Hobbes gave him a manuscript copy on vellum, now British Library Egerton MS 1910 (DNB 1891). The Introduction announces four parts: the matter and maker of the commonwealth, which is man; how it is made by covenants; a Christian commonwealth; and the kingdom of darkness (Lev. Intro.3–6). It also states the method, a reading of oneself, under the old maxim "Nosce Teipsum, Read Thy Self" (Lev. Intro.7). The last paragraph of the Review and Conclusion names the aim: "to set before mens eyes the mutuall Relation between Protection and Obedience" (Lev. R&C.17). **Difficulty:** moderate in Parts I–II, where the argument is continuous; high in Parts III–IV, where it is scriptural exegesis and polemic and the reader needs the background in [01-life-and-times.md](01-life-and-times.md). The SEP's advice to newcomers is to read Parts III and IV too, and the dossier does so in Week 6.

**Head, Bear and Ornaments.** Three printings carry the imprint "London, 1651", told apart by the ornament on the title page. The Head is the true first edition. Columbia's exhibit, following older opinion, describes the Bear as a Dutch piracy of a year or two later. Noel Malcolm's work toward his critical edition changed the picture, as reported from his 2009 Oxford seminar on the Bodleian's book-history blog. From dated ownership inscriptions and prices, he found the Head on the market through the 1650s, the Bear in the late 1670s and early 1680s, and the Ornaments in the early eighteenth century. The Bear began in John Redmayne's London shop, which the Stationers' Company raided in September 1670, and was finished in the Netherlands with an ornament belonging to the Amsterdam printer Christoffel Cunradus. The Ornaments is set in a type of John Darby's not used before the late 1690s, so it dates from no later than 1702, long after Hobbes's death. Malcolm's reading of the variants is that Hobbes oversaw the Head, passed a few significant changes through Crooke that surface in the Bear, and had nothing to do with the Ornaments. All modern editions are therefore Head-based. Wikisource transcribes the Head, first issue, and says the later two are page-for-page reprints with mostly minor changes. The dossier quotes Project Gutenberg #3207, whose header does not name its copy-text; where a quotation matters, check it against Wikisource's Head transcription (see Doubts in the log).

**The Latin *Leviathan* (1668).** Barred from publishing on religion at home, Hobbes had his Latin works collected by Blaeu at Amsterdam in 1668, and the collection included a Latin *Leviathan* (DNB 1891). It is not a translation in the modern sense. The SEP notes significant changes and additions on controversial topics such as the Trinity and the nature of God, and the DNB describes the version as partly modified, with three apologetic dialogues in place of the Review and Conclusion. The saved 1668 text shows the Appendix opening with a chapter on the Nicene Creed, "De Symbolo Niceno" (Lev. Lat., Appendix, cap. I). How to weigh the two versions is disputed. Some scholars treat the Latin as Hobbes's last word on the questions it changes; others read it as a defensive revision for a different audience and keep the English as the text of record. Malcolm's 2012 Clarendon edition, the first to print the two side by side, is where the evidence is now set out, and [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md) names the positions. **Place:** the dossier's arguments are built on the English. Curley's notes and Malcolm's facing text are used to flag where the Latin differs, chiefly in Weeks 6 and 7.

## The system completed: *De Corpore* (1655) and *De Homine* (1658)

Back in London, Hobbes finished the *Elements of Philosophy*. ***De Corpore*** (1655) is the first part. The SEP describes it as covering logic, language, method, metaphysics, mathematics and physics, and as Hobbes's main statement on method and on how language works; its opening part on logic gives the fullest form of the account of reasoning as computation that Argument 2 reconstructs from *Leviathan* (the SEP cites DCo 1.2–1.3 for it). The DNB of 1891 records that publication was held up for a year while Hobbes struggled with friends' objections to his geometrical solutions, and that an English translation, *Concerning Body*, corrected by Hobbes, followed in 1656 with the *Six Lessons* against the Oxford professors Seth Ward and John Wallis. The claimed squaring of the circle in the book began the longest of his quarrels (SEP, citing Jesseph 1999). **Difficulty:** moderate in the logic, high in the physics and geometry. **Place:** background for Argument 2. The SEP cites *De Corpore* by chapter and section (e.g. 1.2, 2.9), and the dossier does the same, as DCo 2.9.

***De Homine*** (1658) completed the plan. The SEP says it deals mainly with physiology and optics. The DNB calls it a "mere makeshift": most of it is a Latin version of optical chapters from an unpublished English treatise of 1646, with less psychology than the *Elements* or *Leviathan* already gave. Its late chapters on speech, the passions and religion are the ones a student of the politics might read. **Place:** not set reading. The Clarendon Edition files it under the writings on optics (volume 19), which says something about how its editors see it.

## The debate with Bramhall (1645–1682)

The free-will controversy began as a conversation. In Paris, in the presence of William Cavendish, then Marquis of Newcastle, Hobbes and John Bramhall, an exiled Anglican bishop, argued about free will, and each then wrote out his position (the DNB dates the discussion 1646; other accounts give 1645). Hobbes's reply was not meant for print. A young Englishman made a copy while translating it for a French friend and published it in 1654, without Hobbes's knowledge, as ***Of Liberty and Necessity*** (DNB 1891). Bramhall, blaming Hobbes, printed the whole exchange with a long rejoinder in 1655. Hobbes answered in 1656 with ***The Questions concerning Liberty, Necessity, and Chance***, which reprints Bramhall's numbered sections with Hobbes's "Animadversions" after each (Molesworth V). Bramhall's *Castigations of Hobbes's Animadversions* (1658) carried an appendix attacking *Leviathan*, *The Catching of Leviathan*. Hobbes's *Answer* to that appendix was written about 1668 and printed only in 1682, after both men were dead (DNB). The DNB's judgment that "The argument upon necessity shows Hobbes at his best" is still common.

*Of Liberty and Necessity* is short and is the best single text on Hobbes's compatibilism. Its definition is the one Argument 16 reconstructs: "Liberty is the absence of all the impediments to action that are not contained in the nature and intrinsical quality of the agent" (LN, "My Opinion about Liberty and Necessity", fifth point; EW IV, 273). The dossier cites LN by Molesworth page and the *Questions* by Bramhall's section number, which both Molesworth and Chappell keep. **Difficulty:** low for LN; the *Questions* are long and repetitive. **Place:** Week 7, with *Leviathan* 21.1–4.

## The late works: *Behemoth* and the *Dialogue* (c. 1668–1681)

After the Restoration Hobbes was under suspicion of heresy, and in 1666 the House of Commons took up a bill against atheism and profanity that named *Leviathan* (from memory; logged unchecked). He stopped publishing on politics and religion in England and wrote instead in forms that could circulate privately.

***Behemoth*** is a history of the causes of the civil wars in four dialogues between an older speaker, A, and a younger, B. The DNB says it was finished about 1668, suppressed at the king's wish, printed surreptitiously in 1679 and properly in 1681. The SEP dates its composition to the late 1660s and its publication as posthumous. Ferdinand Tönnies edited it in 1889 from Hobbes's manuscript at St John's College, Oxford, under the title *Behemoth, or the Long Parliament*. Paul Seaward's Clarendon edition is, in OUP's words, the first fully scholarly edition and the first new text since 1889. The IEP reads *Behemoth* as Hobbes's diagnosis of the war: a dispute about politics and religion, spread by clergy, lawyers and the universities, cost Charles I a power he held by right. **Difficulty:** low; the dialogue is plain and often sardonic. **Place:** Week 7 in selection, as the theory's application to the events it was written in. No Molesworth VI or Tönnies text is saved in the dossier's sources, so the dossier paraphrases *Behemoth* and cites it by dialogue.

***A Dialogue between a Philosopher and a Student of the Common Laws of England*** is Hobbes's attack on the common lawyers' claim that law is the artificial reason of judges rather than the command of the sovereign; Sir Edward Coke is the target (from memory; logged unchecked). Mark Murphy's review of the Clarendon volume reports Alan Cromartie's dating: not finished before 1661, probably not until about 1670. It circulated unofficially and was printed by William Crooke in 1681. The Clarendon volume (2005) adds Quentin Skinner's edition of Hobbes's 1679 answer on hereditary right, written during the Exclusion crisis. Murphy notes how many of *Leviathan*'s theses reappear in the short *Dialogue*, and Cromartie reads it as Hobbes's defense against the charge of heresy. **Place:** optional, with Week 5 and Argument 10.

## The Clarendon Edition

Oxford University Press is publishing the Clarendon Edition of the Works of Thomas Hobbes, under an editorial board of Noel Malcolm, Quentin Skinner and Keith Thomas. The SEP and the NDPR review describe it as a projected 26 volumes. The scheme printed in the 2024 *Elements of Law* lists 27, the last an index: 1 *Elements of Law*; 2 *De Cive*; 3–5 *Leviathan*; 6–7 Correspondence; 10 *Behemoth*; 11 the common-law writings; 13 the liberty and necessity writings; 14–15 the critique of White with the Objections to Descartes; 16–17 *De Corpore*; 19 optics with *De Homine*; 23 Thucydides; 24–25 Homer. Published so far, in the dossier's sources: Warrender's *De Cive*, Malcolm's *Correspondence* (2 vols., 1994), Cromartie and Skinner's common-law writings (2005), Seaward's *Behemoth*, Malcolm's *Leviathan* (3 vols., 2012) and Sommerville's *Elements of Law* (2024). For the rest, Molesworth's *English Works* (11 vols.) and *Opera Latina* (5 vols., 1839–45) remain the complete text (SEP).

## A note on citation and editions

***Leviathan*** is cited by chapter and paragraph, as (Lev. 13.9), with Intro and R&C for the Introduction and the Review and Conclusion. The 1651 text has no paragraph numbers; the dossier counts paragraphs from the chapter title in the saved Gutenberg text. Curley's Hackett edition numbers paragraphs on Molesworth's divisions, which are almost identical to the Head's (as a University of Chicago Press volume that adopts Curley's system notes), so (Lev. 13.9) almost always finds the same place there. Tuck prints the 1651 page numbers in the margin, which lets you match a page reference from older scholarship. Where the dossier is unsure of a count, it cites the chapter alone. The Latin is cited as Lev. Lat. with chapter.

**Other works:** the *Elements* as EL with part, chapter and section (EL I.14.11); *De Cive* by chapter and section, the same in Latin and in English (DCv 1.12), or by Preface; *De Corpore* as DCo with chapter and section; LN by Molesworth page (EW IV, 273); the *Questions* by Bramhall's section number; the Third Objections as Obj. III with the objection's number (Obj. III, Obj. 2); *Behemoth* by dialogue.

**Spelling.** Quotations keep the spelling of the saved text: the 1651 spelling for *Leviathan* ("Warre", "Common-wealth"), Molesworth's for the other English works. Molesworth regularized spelling, which is why *De Cive* and LN look more modern than *Leviathan* in this dossier. The difference comes from the nineteenth-century editor, not from Hobbes.

**Reading editions (the dossier's rulings).** The learning path and the study guide leave the choice open; the dossier rules as follows.

- ***Leviathan*:** Richard Tuck (ed.), *Leviathan*, revised student edition (Cambridge Texts in the History of Political Thought, 1996), which Cambridge describes as the most accurate and authoritative text since its first appearance in 1991. It keeps Hobbes's spelling and punctuation. Or Edwin Curley (ed.), *Leviathan, with selected variants from the Latin edition of 1668* (Hackett, 1994), which modernizes spelling and punctuation and gives the significant Latin variants in its notes (Hackett's description). Curley is the easier read and the better choice for Weeks 6–7; Tuck is closer to the page Hobbes saw. For checking a reading, Malcolm's Clarendon *Leviathan* (3 vols., 2012), the first critical edition and the first with the English and Latin side by side, is the reference; it is a library book, not a reading copy.
- ***Elements of Law*:** J. C. A. Gaskin (ed.), *The Elements of Law Natural and Politic* (Oxford World's Classics, 1994), which keeps Tönnies's numbering under the Part titles *Human Nature* and *De Corpore Politico*. For a disputed reading, Sommerville's Clarendon edition (2024).
- ***De Cive*:** Richard Tuck and Michael Silverthorne (eds.), *On the Citizen* (Cambridge, 1998), translated from the 1647 Latin.
- **Bramhall debate:** Vere Chappell (ed.), *Hobbes and Bramhall on Liberty and Necessity* (Cambridge, 1999), which prints the exchange of 1645–46 and selections from the *Questions*.
- **Third Objections:** any modern translation of the *Meditations* that includes the objections and replies; the dossier quotes Haldane & Ross, vol. II (1911–12; corrected reprint 1934).
- ***Behemoth*:** Seaward's Clarendon text where available; otherwise Stephen Holmes's University of Chicago reprint of Tönnies (from memory; logged unchecked).

**Avoid** abridged *Leviathan*s that drop Parts III and IV without saying so, any "1651" reprint whose copy-text is the Ornaments, and editions that renumber chapters, as the study guide warns. Avoid citing *De Corpore Politico* by its 1650 chapter numbers without the EL equivalent.

## The works in the dossier's reading order

The [learning path](../../learning-path.md) sets *Leviathan* chs. 13–17 in excerpt in Stage 3, row 6, with the rest at map level. The dossier keeps that core and widens it to seven weeks, about 38 hours all-in; [07-study-plan.md](07-study-plan.md) gives the split.

1. **Week 1, life, the Civil War, the project:** *Leviathan* Introduction and Review and Conclusion; EL Epistle Dedicatory and I.1. Read the two prefaces side by side for the method and the occasion.
2. **Week 2, mind and language:** *Leviathan* chs. 1–6; the Third Objections with Descartes's replies. Arguments 1–3.
3. **Week 3, toward war:** chs. 10–13; the *De Cive* Preface to the Reader. Arguments 4–6. Check ch. 13 against EL I.14 and DCv 1.
4. **Week 4, laws of nature and covenant:** chs. 14–16. Arguments 7–11. EL I.15–17 is the shorter parallel.
5. **Week 5, the sovereign:** chs. 17–21, 29–30. Arguments 11–14. The *Dialogue* is optional reading here.
6. **Week 6, religion and the church:** ch. 12; chs. 32–33, 42, 46–47. Argument 15. Use Curley's notes for the Latin variants.
7. **Week 7, free will; capstone:** *Of Liberty and Necessity*; ch. 21 opening; *Behemoth* in selection. Argument 16, then the capstone essay.

The figures Hobbes answered, and those who answered him, have library entries: [René Descartes](../../eras/03-early-modern/rene-descartes.md), [Francis Bacon](../../eras/03-early-modern/francis-bacon.md), [Niccolò Machiavelli](../../eras/03-early-modern/niccolo-machiavelli.md), [Suárez and Grotius](../../eras/03-early-modern/suarez-and-grotius.md), [Baruch Spinoza](../../eras/03-early-modern/baruch-spinoza.md), [John Locke](../../eras/03-early-modern/john-locke.md) and the [moral sense school](../../eras/03-early-modern/moral-sense-school.md).
