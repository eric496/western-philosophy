# Life and Times: Westport, Oxford, Chatsworth, Paris, London, Hardwick
**English** | [简体中文](../../zh/focus-studies/thomas-hobbes/01-life-and-times.md)


Thomas Hobbes lived for ninety-one years, from the year of the Spanish Armada to the last years of Charles II, and through almost every crisis of seventeenth-century England: the quarrels between the early Stuart kings and their Parliaments, the civil wars of the 1640s, the execution of Charles I in 1649, the republic and Cromwell's Protectorate, the Restoration of 1660, and the panic over the Popish Plot at the end of the 1670s. Unlike Aquinas, he wrote about himself, and more than once. Late in life he composed two autobiographies in Latin, one in prose and one in elegiac verse, the verse life written at eighty-four (1672) and printed in 1679, with an English translation in 1680. In 1662, answering the mathematician John Wallis, he wrote a defence of his own conduct in the third person, *Considerations upon the Reputation, Loyalty, Manners, and Religion of Thomas Hobbes*. Those three texts are where most of the dates come from, and they are not disinterested: they were written by an old man defending his reputation against charges of disloyalty and atheism.

The other main source is John Aubrey (1626–1697), the Wiltshire antiquary, who first met Hobbes as a schoolboy in 1634 and gathered notes for a life from the 1660s until after Hobbes's death. Aubrey's *Brief Lives* is a collection of notes rather than a book, never finished by its author and first edited in full by Andrew Clark in 1898; Hobbes's life in Clark's first volume runs to some eighty pages, with letters from Hobbes and from his secretary James Wheldon. Aubrey had the stories from Hobbes himself, from his brother Edmund, and from people who knew him, and he is the source of nearly every vivid anecdote: the Euclid story, the friendship with Galileo, the pen and inkhorn in the head of Hobbes's walking stick, the burning of papers in the 1660s. The SEP article "Thomas Hobbes" relies on Aubrey for these and notes that Hobbes's own autobiographical writings do not mention Galileo. The standard modern life is A. P. Martinich's *Hobbes: A Biography* (1999), which the SEP cites; Noel Malcolm's edition of *The Correspondence of Thomas Hobbes* (Clarendon Edition) and his essays in *Aspects of Hobbes* (2002) supply the documentary record. Leslie Stephen's article in the old *Dictionary of National Biography* (1891), which is public domain, is used here as a check on dates, and is cited as the DNB.

Quotations from Hobbes keep the spelling of the saved texts: the 1651 spelling for *Leviathan*, the 1680 spelling for the English verse life, and the spelling of Molesworth's edition (1839–45) for the other English works. Aubrey is quoted from Clark's 1898 edition in Aubrey's own spelling.

## Westport and Malmesbury, 1588–1603

Hobbes was born on 5 April 1588 in Westport, then a parish just outside the walls of Malmesbury in north Wiltshire and now part of the town; he called himself "of Malmesbury" all his life, and the verse autobiography says he was baptized there. Aubrey, who visited the house in April 1659 with Hobbes's brother Edmund, places it precisely: it was "the farthest howse on the left hand as you goe to Tedbury, leaving the church on your right", and Hobbes was born in the inner of its two upstairs chambers. Aubrey adds that the day was a Friday and that year Good Friday.

The famous birth story is Hobbes's own. It appears in the opening lines of the verse autobiography, which Aubrey copied into his notes in the Latin, ending *Ut pareret geminos meque metumque simul* ("so that she bore twins, me and fear together"). The English translation of 1680 runs:

> For Fame had rumour'd, that a Fleet at Sea,
> Wou'd cause our Nations Catastrophe;
> And hereupon it was my Mother Dear
> Did bring forth Twins at once, both Me, and Fear.

— *The Life of Mr. Thomas Hobbes of Malmesbury* (1680), English translation of the Latin verse life

Aubrey gives the same story in prose: "His mother fell in labour with him upon the fright of the invasion of the Spaniards". Two cautions apply. First, the story rests on Hobbes himself, writing at eighty-four, and on Aubrey repeating him; no independent record of the premature birth exists. Second, the Armada did not reach the Channel until July, three months after the birth, so the fright, if it happened, was at rumours of the coming invasion, which is what the verse says ("Fame had rumour'd"). The line that the library entry gives, "fear and I were born twins together", is a common modern paraphrase rather than the wording of either the Latin or the 1680 English (see the corrections log). It suits a philosopher whose political theory begins from the fear of violent death too well for readers to resist, and Hobbes, who chose to open his life with it, plainly meant it to.

His father, also Thomas, was the vicar of Westport and Charlton (the DNB's description), an ill-educated and quarrelsome clergyman. Aubrey has him falling asleep in church after a Saturday night at cards and calling out that clubs were trumps. In 1604 he struck another clergyman in a churchyard and, in Aubrey's words, "was forced to fly for it" (as the SEP quotes Aubrey); he left Malmesbury and disappears from the record. Of the mother almost nothing is known. The family was kept together by the father's brother Francis Hobbes, a prosperous glover of Malmesbury, who paid for his nephew's schooling and university; the SEP and IEP both stress this patronage, and the IEP calls the father "a disgraced village vicar".

The schooling was good. By Aubrey's account Hobbes went to school in Westport church at four, could read well and "number four figures" by eight, and then studied with Robert Latimer, a young Oxford graduate who kept a private school in Westport and taught a few able boys in the evening. Latimer was, Aubrey says, "a good Graecian", and before Hobbes left for Oxford he had turned Euripides' *Medea* into Latin iambics and presented them to his master. The verse life says he spent six years on Greek and Latin and went up to Oxford at fourteen. His schoolfellows, Aubrey reports, called the black-haired boy "Crowe".

## Magdalen Hall, 1603–1608

Hobbes entered Magdalen Hall, Oxford, at the beginning of 1603, by Anthony Wood's note in Aubrey (the SEP gives 1602 or 1603). Magdalen Hall was one of the university's halls, a separate house from Magdalen College. Hobbes graduated Bachelor of Arts in February 1608 (Wood's record gives 5 February 1607 in the old reckoning, 1608 in ours), and the SEP gives February 1608.

He did not think much of what he was taught. The verse life lists the syllogistic moods he had to learn ("Barbara, Celarent, Darii, Ferio") and the Aristotelian physics of form and matter and of sympathy and antipathy, and says he turned instead to maps of the earth and the heavens. Aubrey adds that Hobbes "did not much care for logick, yet he learnd it, and thought himselfe a good disputant", that he liked to lie "gaping on mappes" in the bookbinders' shops, and that he caught jackdaws with lines baited with cheese. The contempt for the universities' Aristotle never left him. In Part IV of *Leviathan* he says that what the universities teach "is not properly Philosophy, (the nature whereof dependeth not on Authors,) but Aristotelity" (Lev. 46), and the "Review, and Conclusion" proposes that the book itself be "profitably printed, and more profitably taught in the Universities", because the universities are "the Fountains of Civill, and Morall Doctrine" from which preachers and gentry draw what they pass on to the people.

## The Cavendish household, 1608–1628

On leaving Oxford in 1608, Hobbes was recommended by the principal of Magdalen Hall to William Cavendish, Lord Cavendish of Hardwick (created first Earl of Devonshire in 1618), as tutor and companion to his son, also William (1590–1628), later the second earl. The pupil was only two years younger than the tutor, and Aubrey's account suggests a servant and friend more than a schoolmaster: Hobbes was "his lordship's page", rode hunting and hawking with him, and kept his privy purse. The verse life calls the young lord "Not a Lord only, but a Friend to Me", and the DNB adds, from the same source, that Hobbes later remembered these twenty years as the happiest of his life. The SEP stresses what the post gave him: books, and connections to the philosophers and scientists of his day. He would serve the family, with interruptions, for the rest of his life; he died in one of their houses seventy-one years later.

In 1610 Hobbes took his pupil on the grand tour through France, Germany and Italy (the DNB's date; the verse life names the three countries). Aubrey says that the life of a page had made him almost forget his Latin, and that he bought pocket editions printed in Amsterdam, Caesar's *Commentaries* among them, to read in the lobby while his lord paid visits. Back in England he read the Greek and Latin historians and poets in the Cavendish library, which by his own account his lord stocked with every kind of book he wanted. Around 1620, by the SEP's dating, he worked for a time as a secretary to Francis Bacon, who, Aubrey says, liked Hobbes to take down his thoughts on his walks at Gorhambury "because he understood what he wrote". Aubrey also says that Hobbes helped to translate several of Bacon's essays into Latin. How much Bacon influenced him is disputed; the claim that Hobbes was Bacon's disciple goes beyond the evidence, and the anecdote rests on Aubrey.

The second earl died on 20 June 1628 (the date given in Clark's notes to Aubrey). The verse life records the loss, and says that the earl had provided for Hobbes before he died; but the household no longer needed a tutor, and Hobbes "left my pleasant Mansion" for other work.

## Thucydides, 1629

The first book Hobbes published was a translation, *Eight Bookes of the Peloponnesian Warre written by Thucydides*, printed in London in 1629. The IEP calls it brilliant, and it is still read. The verse life says why he chose this author out of all the classics he had read:

> There's none that pleas'd me like Thucydides.
> He says Democracy's a Foolish Thing,
> Than a Republick Wiser is one King.

— *The Life of Mr. Thomas Hobbes of Malmesbury* (1680)

The date matters. The translation appeared in 1629, the year Charles I dissolved Parliament and began eleven years of rule without one, and Hobbes's Thucydides is a history of how a democratic city talked itself into a disastrous war and then into civil strife at Corcyra. The IEP suggests that Hobbes's ideas about human nature came less from his mechanical science than from his reading of contemporary events and of historians like Thucydides. Readers of chapter 13 of *Leviathan* who go on to Thucydides's account of the Corcyrean revolution (III.82–83) will see why.

## Euclid and the second tour, 1629–1631

In 1629 Hobbes went abroad again, as travelling tutor to the son of Sir Gervase Clifton, a Nottinghamshire landowner, and spent about eighteen months on the continent, chiefly in Paris (the DNB's account; the verse life gives the eighteen months). The DNB places the Euclid encounter on this tour, and this is the usual dating; Aubrey gives the story without a date and leaves a blank for the place. Hobbes, he says, was "40 yeares old before he looked on geometry", and it happened by accident: in a gentleman's library "Euclid's Elements lay open" at the forty-seventh proposition of Book I, the theorem of Pythagoras. Hobbes read it and swore that it was impossible ("this is impossible!", Aubrey has him say, with an oath that Aubrey records and then disguises). He read the demonstration, which referred him back to another proposition, which referred him to another, "that at last he was demonstratively convinced of that trueth. This made him in love with geometry." The story rests on Aubrey alone, who had it, he implies, from Hobbes. The SEP notes that it has been made to carry a whole interpretation of Hobbes's method: on one reading his philosophy takes its model from geometry, deducing conclusions from definitions; on another, connected with the Galileo story below, his method of resolution and composition comes from the Aristotelians of Padua. Both readings have their defenders, and the SEP article itself develops the second while flagging the first.

Aubrey adds that Hobbes drew figures on his thigh and on the bedsheets, and that Sir Jonas Moore and others thought it a pity he had come to mathematics so late: had he begun early, he would not have "layn so open to his learned mathematicall antagonists". That remark looks forward to the Wallis quarrels.

## The third tour: Mersenne and Galileo, 1631–1637

In 1631 Hobbes was recalled to the Cavendish family, now to tutor the second earl's son, William Cavendish, third Earl of Devonshire (1617–1684). The verse life says he taught the boy Latin, rhetoric, verse and prose, mathematics, geography and "The whole Design of Law", for seven years. From 1634 to 1636 he took the young earl on a third continental tour, through France and Italy and into Savoy, returning to England in 1637 (the DNB's dates).

This journey turned Hobbes into a philosopher. The verse life says that on horseback, in coaches and on ships he thought about one thing, motion, and that the only real thing in the world was the motion that underlies the appearances of sense:

> Phancie's Internal, th'Issue of our Brain,
> Th'internal parts only Motion contain:
> And he that studies Physicks first must know
> What Motion is, and what Motion can do.

— *The Life of Mr. Thomas Hobbes of Malmesbury* (1680)

In Paris he came into the circle of Marin Mersenne (1588–1648), the Minim friar whose cell was the clearing-house of European science. The verse life is explicit: "Here with Mersennus I acquainted grew, / Shew'd him of Motion what I ever knew." Mersenne's circle, which was Descartes's link to Paris and included Pierre Gassendi, gave Hobbes his first audience and his lasting reputation on the continent. The verse life later praises Mersenne as one "whose single Cell might be / Prefer'd before an University", and pictures every learned man of the age wheeling round him "like an Axis".

The meeting with Galileo is less secure. Aubrey's sentence, as the SEP quotes it, is: "When he [Hobbes] was at Florence … he contracted a friendship with the famous Galileo Galilei". Aubrey goes on that Hobbes "extremely venerated and magnified" him. The DNB dates the meeting to about 1636, when Galileo, condemned by the Roman Inquisition in 1633, was living under house arrest at Arcetri outside Florence. The SEP points out that Hobbes's own autobiographical writings do not mention it, though they do mention Mersenne. The meeting is usually accepted, and Hobbes praised Galileo in print (the dedication of *De Corpore*, 1655, credits him, in the usual paraphrase, with opening the gate of natural philosophy), but it rests on Aubrey, and nothing is known of what the two men said. The interpretive weight sometimes placed on it, a line of method running from the Paduan Aristotelian Zabarella through Galileo to Hobbes, is, the SEP judges, hard to support.

Back in England in 1637, Hobbes moved in two overlapping circles: the Great Tew group around Lucius Cary, Viscount Falkland, which included Edward Hyde (later Earl of Clarendon) and Sidney Godolphin, and the Welbeck circle of William Cavendish, Earl (later Marquis) of Newcastle, the second earl's cousin, and his brother Sir Charles Cavendish, the mathematician (the DNB names both). A short Latin treatise on motion and sensation from these years, the *Short Tract on First Principles*, was long attributed to Hobbes; the attribution is now disputed, and some scholars give it to Newcastle's chaplain Robert Payne. He began to plan a philosophy in three parts, on body, man and citizen. The verse life:

> Man, Body, Citizen, for there I do
> Heap Matter up, designing three Books too.

— *The Life of Mr. Thomas Hobbes of Malmesbury* (1680)

The civil wars put the third part first.

## *The Elements of Law* and the flight to Paris, 1640

In April 1640 Charles I, short of money for his war against the Scots, summoned Parliament for the first time in eleven years. The Short Parliament sat for three weeks and was dissolved in May. That spring Hobbes finished *The Elements of Law, Natural and Politic*, his first systematic work: it starts from sense, imagination and the passions, as all his later political books would, and argues, in his later summary, that the powers the Commons were disputing were "inseparably annexed to the sovereignty". The epistle dedicatory to the Earl of Newcastle is dated "May 9, 1640" in Molesworth's text, the month of the dissolution. It closes with a sentence that states the whole later project in miniature: the conclusions of the book are such that, for want of them, "government and peace have been nothing else, to this day, but mutual fears" (EL, Epistle Dedicatory).

The *Elements* was not printed. It circulated in manuscript copies, and twenty-two years later Hobbes, writing of himself in the third person, described what followed:

> Of this treatise, though not printed, many gentlemen had copies, which occasioned much talk of the author; and had not his Majesty dissolved the Parliament, it had brought him into danger of his life.

— Hobbes, *Considerations upon the Reputation, Loyalty, Manners, and Religion of Thomas Hobbes* (1662), Molesworth IV

The Long Parliament met on 3 November 1640 and at once moved against those who had preached or written for the king's prerogative; Aubrey says that Hobbes, seeing Bishop Roger Maynwaring sent to the Tower for preaching doctrine like his, thought "'tis time now for me to shift for my selfe". The *Considerations* go on: "Mr. Hobbes, doubting how they would use him, went over into France, the first of all that fled, and there continued eleven years". The SEP accepts the outline (Hobbes left England late in 1640 and did not return until 1651) and gives both motives: he was associated with the royalists, and his defence of absolute sovereignty might have brought punishment. The library entry's "by his account the first royalist exile to leave" is exactly right: the claim is Hobbes's own, made in 1662 when Wallis had accused him of disloyalty.

The *Elements* reached print only in 1650, in two parts, as *Human Nature* and *De Corpore Politico* (the DNB's account; the SEP's moral and political article gives the 1650 date). The full text under its own title was first edited by Ferdinand Tönnies in 1889.

## The Third Objections, 1641

In Paris Hobbes went back to Mersenne. Through him, in January 1641 (the DNB's date), he sent Descartes a set of objections to the *Meditations on First Philosophy*; they were printed, with Descartes's replies, as the third set of Objections when the *Meditations* appeared in 1641. The French version describes them as objections "urged by a Celebrated English Philosopher", as Haldane and Ross note. In 1640 Hobbes had already sent Mersenne comments on Descartes's *Discourse* and *Optics*, and Descartes had answered through Mersenne (the SEP reports both).

The Third Objections set out, briefly and bluntly, views that would reappear in *Leviathan*: that a thinking thing may be "something corporeal", that "we have no idea of God" and none of the soul, only names and inferences, and that reasoning may be "nothing more than the uniting and stringing together of names or designations by the word is" (Obj. III, Objs. 2, 5, 7 and 4, tr. Haldane & Ross, vol. II (1911–12; corrected reprint 1934)). Descartes's replies are short and impatient. The SEP sums up the relation between the two as a curious mixture of respect and dismissal. Descartes suggested in 1643 that Hobbes was more accomplished in moral philosophy than elsewhere, but that his moral views were wicked, and he suspected Hobbes of "aiming to make his reputation at my expense, and by devious means" (Descartes's letter of 1641 as the SEP quotes it). Aubrey, for his part, says the two "mutually respected one another", and that Hobbes thought Descartes should have kept to geometry. On the one occasion they are said to have met, in 1648, they did not get on (the SEP, citing Martinich). Week 2 of this dossier reads the Third Objections with Descartes's replies; Argument 3 in [the arguments file](04-arguments.md) reconstructs the objection to the thinking substance.

## *De Cive*, 1642–1647

In Paris Hobbes put the third part of his planned system first. *Elementorum philosophiae sectio tertia de cive*, the "third section of the elements of philosophy, on the citizen", was printed in Paris in 1642 with a dedication to the third Earl of Devonshire (the DNB's account). The Preface to the Reader, printed in the 1647 edition, explains why the last part came out first:

> it so happened in the interim, that my country, some few years before the civil wars did rage, was boiling hot with questions concerning the rights of dominion and the obedience due from subjects, the true forerunners of an approaching war; and was the cause which, all those other matters deferred, ripened and plucked from me this third part.

— *De Cive*, Preface to the Reader, 1651 English (*Philosophical Rudiments*), Molesworth II

The verse life is prouder: the book "Gratifi'd Learned Men" and was translated and praised abroad, "So that my Name was Famous, and far spread". The authoritative edition, with notes answering objections and the new Preface, was published at Amsterdam in 1647, under the supervision of Hobbes's friend Samuel Sorbière, a French physician (the DNB's account). An English translation, *Philosophical Rudiments concerning Government and Society*, appeared in London in 1651; whether Hobbes made it himself has been disputed. The famous Latin tag *bellum omnium contra omnes*, the war of all against all, comes from *De Cive* (the library entry notes this), not from *Leviathan*, which says "of every man, against every man" (Lev. 13).

## The Prince of Wales and the exile court, 1646–1651

The years in Paris were productive and precarious. Hobbes lived on savings, on a legacy, and on a pension from home; the verse life gives the sums, five hundred pounds brought from England, two hundred more that came with the death of Sidney Godolphin, and "Twice forty pounds, a yearly Pension" from his own country (the DNB says from the Earl of Devonshire). Godolphin, the friend Hobbes later praised in *Leviathan*, "was unfortunately slain in the beginning of the late Civill warre, in the Publique quarrel" (Lev., A Review, and Conclusion). Hobbes studied chemistry and anatomy (Aubrey says he read Vesalius and dissected with the young William Petty) and worked on optics and on *De Corpore*. The verse life says that he spent four years trying to write the book on body and was interrupted by the war.

In the summer of 1646 the Prince of Wales, the future Charles II, came to Paris with his court in exile, and Hobbes was engaged to teach him mathematics (the DNB's account). Hobbes was careful about the title. In the *Considerations* he says that he "had the honour to initiate him in the mathematics" but never presumed to call himself the king's tutor, and rebukes Wallis for saying so. In 1647 he fell dangerously ill (the DNB). His secretary James Wheldon later told Aubrey that the palsy that shook his hands began in France before 1650 and grew on him by degrees; the IEP describes it as shaking palsy, probably Parkinson's disease, from about the age of sixty.

It was in these years that he wrote *Leviathan*, in English, for an English audience. The verse life connects it with the prince's arrival, the war and his illness:

> My Prince's studies I then waited on,
> But cou'd not constantly attend my own.
> Then for six Months was sick; but yet at length,
> Though very weak, I did recover strength,
> And finish'd it in my own Mother-Tongue,

— *The Life of Mr. Thomas Hobbes of Malmesbury* (1680)

Aubrey describes the method of composition: Hobbes walked a great deal, "he had in the head of his staffe a pen and inke-horne, carried alwayes a note-booke in his pocket", and wrote each thought down as it came, having already drawn up the design of the book in chapters. He adds that the little 1640 manuscript grew into *De Cive*, "and at last grew there to be the so formidable" *Leviathan*. The book was finished during the worst years of the war: Charles I was executed on 30 January 1649, and the dedicatory letter to Francis Godolphin, Sidney's brother, is signed "Paris APRILL 15/25 1651" (the double date gives the English and continental calendars).


## *Leviathan* and the return, 1651–1652

*Leviathan, or the Matter, Forme, and Power of a Common-Wealth Ecclesiasticall and Civill* was printed in London by Andrew Crooke in 1651 while its author was still in Paris. The Columbia University Libraries exhibit on the book notes that Crooke's shop sent proofs to Paris but did not always wait for corrections, so copies of the first ("head") edition vary; Noel Malcolm's bibliographical work (reported by the Bodleian) showed that the two other editions dated 1651, the "bear" and the "ornaments", were printed decades later. Hobbes presented a manuscript copy on vellum to Charles II (the DNB identifies it as British Library Egerton MS 1910). The verse life is proud of the result, a book "Known by its dreadful Name, LEVIATHAN", and frank about the cost. The exiled court read its account of submission to a conqueror and its attack on the clergy as treachery; Hobbes was accused of seeming "to approve Cromwel's Impiety" and was "Banish'd both the King's Presence and his Court". The DNB adds that the French clergy, angered by the book's assault on the papacy, were thought to be preparing a move against him, and that he remembered the murders of the parliamentary envoys Dorislaus (1649) and Ascham (1650), which the verse life also names.

He went home at the end of 1651, in winter and after another illness ("Th'Wind, Frost, Snow sharp, with Age grown gray, / A plunging Beast, and most unpleasant way"), and in London, "lest I should appear a Spy, / Unto the State my self I did apply": he submitted to the Council of State and was left to live privately. Whether *Leviathan* was written to make that return possible is an old charge and a live debate. Clarendon, Hobbes's friend from Great Tew, later reported Hobbes saying, while the book was in the press, "The truth is, I have a mind to go home" (as the DNB quotes him), and Wallis later accused him of writing the book for Cromwell. Hobbes answered in the *Considerations* (1662) that he had never justified rebellion; the DNB judges the story of an offer from Cromwell groundless while noting Hobbes's own boast in 1656 that the book had reconciled "a thousand gentlemen" to submission. The "Review, and Conclusion" states his own purpose: to set before men's eyes "the mutuall Relation between Protection and Obedience". The [reception file](06-reception-and-debates.md) takes up the modern side of this debate.

## Bramhall, Wallis and the Restoration, 1652–1666

In 1653 Hobbes returned to the Devonshire household, but lived in London, in Fetter Lane, where John Selden and William Harvey were among his friends (the DNB). He completed the system: *De Corpore* in 1655 and *De Homine* in 1658 (the SEP). The rest of his life was spent in controversy.

The first was with John Bramhall, the royalist bishop, over free will. They had argued it out in Paris in 1646 before the Marquis of Newcastle, and each wrote a statement. Hobbes's reply was published in 1654 without his consent as *Of Liberty and Necessity*; Bramhall printed the whole exchange in 1655; Hobbes answered with *The Questions concerning Liberty, Necessity, and Chance* (1656); Bramhall replied in 1658 with *Castigations of Hobbes's Animadversions*, adding an appendix, *The Catching of Leviathan the Great Whale*, which charged him with atheism. Hobbes's answer to that charge was written in 1668 and published only in 1682 (all dates from the DNB). Week 7 and Argument 16 return to the debate.

The second, with the Oxford mathematicians, he lost. *De Corpore* attacked the universities and claimed to square the circle; Seth Ward answered the philosophy, and John Wallis, Savilian Professor of Geometry, published the *Elenchus Geometriae Hobbianae* (1655), showing the errors and, from an early copy, Hobbes's attempts to patch them. Hobbes replied with *Six Lessons* to the Savilian professors (1656), and the exchange of abuse ran, the DNB says, until Wallis's last retort in 1672. The SEP cites Jesseph's study of the quarrel (1999). Wallis's attack also carried the political charge of disloyalty to which the *Considerations* of 1662 replied.

At the Restoration in 1660 Hobbes's position improved. Charles II recognized his old mathematics teacher in the Strand a few days after his return, enjoyed his wit, called him the bear to be baited, and gave him a pension of £100 a year, irregularly paid (the DNB, drawing on Aubrey and the *Sorberiana*). The bishops and Clarendon remained hostile.

## The atheism inquiry, *Behemoth* and Homer, 1666–1675

In 1666, after the plague and the Fire of London, a committee of the House of Commons considering a bill against atheism and profaneness was empowered to receive information about offending books, *Leviathan* by name (the DNB). Aubrey reports that some bishops had moved in Parliament to have "the good old gentleman burn't for a heretique", and that Hobbes, fearing his papers would be searched, "told me he had burn't part of them" (Aubrey I.339). The bill came to nothing. Hobbes studied the law of heresy and argued (in the *Historical Narrative concerning Heresy*, as the DNB titles it, written about 1668, and in an appendix to the Latin *Leviathan*) that since the abolition of the High Commission no court could try him. The king protected him but would not let him publish on politics or religion in England, and the Latin *Opera philosophica*, with the revised Latin *Leviathan*, was printed by Blaeu at Amsterdam in 1668 (the DNB; the SEP notes the Latin version's changes on the Trinity and the nature of God).

*Behemoth*, a dialogue history of the civil wars from 1640 to 1660, was finished about 1668. The king refused permission to print it; a pirated edition appeared in 1679 and an authorized one in 1681, and Tönnies edited it from the St John's College, Oxford, manuscript in 1889 (the DNB). It is Hobbes's own account of the events that made *Leviathan*: the universities, the preachers and the Commons taught the people disobedience. Week 7 reads it in selection.

In old age he went back to the poets. In 1672, at eighty-four ("I've now Compleated my Eighty fourth year", as the 1680 English has it), he wrote the verse life; at eighty-six he finished verse translations of the *Odyssey* (1674) and the *Iliad* (published with it in 1675, again in 1677). In 1675 he left London for good and lived at Chatsworth and Hardwick with the third earl's family (the DNB).

## Hardwick, 1679, and the Oxford burning, 1683

He was still writing in August 1679. In October he fell ill with a urinary complaint; in November he insisted on traveling with the family from Chatsworth to Hardwick, and soon afterwards had a paralytic stroke. He died at Hardwick Hall on 4 December 1679, aged ninety-one, and was buried in the chancel of the parish church of Ault Hucknall nearby (the DNB; the SEP gives the date and place of death).

The books outlived him and were punished in his place. On 21 July 1683, after the Rye House Plot, the University of Oxford in Convocation condemned twenty-seven propositions from Buchanan, Milton, Baxter, Hobbes and others. Hobbes's *De Cive* and *Leviathan* stand against several, among them the seventh: "Self-preservation is the fundamental law of nature, and supersedes the obligation of all others, whenever they stand in competition with it." The decree is quoted in the spelling of its saved EEBO-TCP transcription, except that the long s is printed as a plain s. The decree orders "the before recited Books to be publicly burnt, by the hand of our Marshal in the court of our Scholes" (*Judgment and Decree*, 1683, English version). The university that had taught him Aristotelity burned the book that proposed to replace it in the universities.

## Weighing the sources

**What is solid.** The dates of the publications, the Cavendish service, the exile and return, the death at Hardwick, and the 1683 decree are documented in the books themselves, in the correspondence Malcolm edited, and in the decree's printed text. The SEP, IEP and DNB agree on the outline.

**What rests on Hobbes and Aubrey.** The Armada birth, the motives for leaving in 1640 and returning in 1651, the claim to have fled first, the Euclid story, the Galileo meeting, and the burned papers come from Hobbes's own late writings, which defend his loyalty and religion, or from Aubrey, who had them from Hobbes. This file reports them as testimony.

**What is disputed.** Whether *Leviathan* was written to ease his return (Clarendon and Wallis against Hobbes's *Considerations*); whether the method came from geometry or from Padua by way of Galileo; how much Bacon mattered; the authorship of the *Short Tract*; who translated *De Cive* into English.

**What the library entry says, and where this file differs.** This file agrees with [the library entry](../../eras/03-early-modern/thomas-hobbes.md) on the outline. Three points are hedged: the "twins" line is a paraphrase, not Hobbes's wording; the Galileo meeting rests on Aubrey; and Hobbes taught the prince mathematics and denied being his tutor (see the corrections log).
