# Hobbes Received: Monster, Positivist, Bourgeois, Game Theorist, and the Quarrels Still Open

*Focus study: Thomas Hobbes · [Dossier home](README.md) · [Library entry](../../eras/03-early-modern/thomas-hobbes.md) · [Study guide](../../study/hobbes-leviathan.md)*

By the 1660s "Hobbist" had become a word for a type: a man who believed in nothing but matter, power and his own advantage. A committee of the House of Commons named *Leviathan* in 1666, a Cambridge man was made to recant his "Hobbist principles" in 1669, and Oxford burned *De Cive* and *Leviathan* in 1683. On the Continent the same books were read more coolly, as the boldest of the new natural-law systems. The eighteenth century answered his psychology, the utilitarians took him up as a theorist of sovereignty, and Ferdinand Tönnies gave him his first modern editions. The twentieth century produced a Hobbes for almost every school: a moralist who founded modern political philosophy (Strauss), a skeptical individualist (Oakeshott), a natural lawyer (Taylor and Warrender), a bourgeois ideologist (Macpherson), a man of his polemical moment (Skinner, Tuck, Malcolm), a game theorist before game theory (Gauthier, Kavka, Hampton), a sincere Calvinist (Martinich), and the author of a contract that both includes and subordinates women (Pateman, Hirschmann, Sreedhar). This file tells that story by era, sets out five live debates with names on both sides, and closes with the standard misconceptions in full form and the myths about the life.

Quotations from Hobbes keep the spelling of the 1651 *Leviathan* as transcribed in Project Gutenberg #3207 ("Warre", "Soveraign") and of the 1651 English *De Cive* as printed by Molesworth; the 1683 Oxford decree is quoted from its EEBO-TCP transcription, with the long s printed as plain s. Read *Leviathan* in Tuck's or Curley's edition ([02-works-and-texts.md](02-works-and-texts.md)). *Leviathan* is cited by chapter and paragraph counted in the saved text (Lev. 13.9), and arguments by their numbers in [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md). Dates and scholars' positions are checked against the SEP and IEP, Leslie Stephen's article in the *Dictionary of National Biography* (1891, cited as DNB), and publisher and catalogue pages; points resting on memory are marked "from memory" and listed in the corrections log.

## The Restoration and "Hobbism", 1651–1700

The first answers came from every side. Sir Robert Filmer, the patriarchalist, attacked *Leviathan* in 1652 (the DNB calls him Hobbes's first purely political assailant), accepting its account of the rights of sovereignty while rejecting its foundation in the consent of naturally free and equal individuals (Filmer's position from memory). The royalist bishop John Bramhall, already Hobbes's opponent on free will, closed his *Castigations* (1658) with *The Catching of Leviathan the Great Whale*, which charged him with atheism and with having written, in the phrase the SEP quotes, a "Rebell's Catechism". Edward Hyde, Earl of Clarendon, Hobbes's friend from the Great Tew circle, wrote *A Brief View and Survey* of the errors of *Leviathan*, finished by 1670 and printed in 1676 (DNB); his main target was the doctrine that obedience follows protection, which he read as a license to submit to Cromwell.

The theologians followed: Thomas Tenison's *The Creed of Mr. Hobbes Examined* (1670) and John Eachard's two satirical dialogues (1672, 1673), which went through many editions (DNB). The serious philosophical replies came from the Cambridge Platonists. Henry More attacked the materialism in *The Immortality of the Soul* (1659), and Ralph Cudworth's *True Intellectual System of the Universe* (1678) was, in the DNB's words, an elaborate examination of it; the SEP counts Cudworth among the critics who thought Hobbes denied God outright. Richard Cumberland's *De Legibus Naturae* (1672) attacked chiefly what the DNB calls his theory of selfishness.

What these critics attacked was as much a reputation as a doctrine. Samuel Mintz's *The Hunting of Leviathan* (1962), which the SEP uses, lists what contemporaries meant when they called Hobbes an atheist: that the universe is body and God part of it, that much of Scripture is a later compilation, that miracles had ceased, that witchcraft was a myth, and that the Church must submit to the civil sovereign. Many of them, the SEP stresses, thought Hobbes believed in God but was a dubious Christian. "Hobbism" became the name for a cluster: materialism, determinism, right made by power, and a cynical psychology. In 1669 the Cambridge authorities forced Daniel Scargill, who had defended theses from *Leviathan*, to recant publicly and to blame his vicious life on his "Hobbist principles" (DNB). The nickname "the Monster of Malmesbury" survives in modern scholarship as a joke (Noel Malcolm uses it in a 1984 review). Mark Goldie's "The Reception of Hobbes" (1994) and Malcolm's *Aspects of Hobbes* (2002), both cited by the SEP, have since shown how many readers took up Hobbes's arguments while denouncing his name.

## The 1666 inquiry and the 1683 burning

In 1666, after the plague and the Fire of London, the Commons considered a bill against atheism and profaneness, and a committee was empowered to receive information about offending books, "especially the ‘Leviathan.’" (DNB). Aubrey reports that some bishops wanted the old man burned for a heretic and that Hobbes, fearing a search, burned part of his papers. The bill came to nothing, but it left two marks. Hobbes studied the law of heresy and argued, in the *Historical Narrative concerning Heresy* (written about 1668) and in an appendix to the Latin *Leviathan* (1668), that since the abolition of the High Commission no English court could try him for it. And the king, who protected him and paid him a pension, would not let him publish on politics or religion in England again; *Behemoth* appeared only in a pirated edition in 1679 (DNB; [01-life-and-times.md](01-life-and-times.md)).

The second episode came four years after his death. After the Rye House Plot of 1683 to kill Charles II and his brother, the University of Oxford in Convocation, on 21 July, condemned twenty-seven propositions drawn from Buchanan, Milton, Baxter, the Jesuits, the Quakers, the Fifth Monarchy men and Hobbes, forbade its members to read the books, and ordered them "to be publicly burnt, by the hand of our Marshal in the court of our Scholes" (*Judgment and Decree*, 1683). *De Cive* and *Leviathan* are named as the sources of the seventh proposition, "Self-preservation is the fundamental law of nature, and supersedes the obligation of all others, whenever they stand in competition with it", and of the fourteenth, that a pact obliges no further than it is credited; the tenth, that possession and strength give a right to govern, is ascribed to Hobbes together with the regicide preacher John Owen and Baxter. The eleventh to thirteenth, printed just before the fourteenth without a separate label, are Hobbes's too: the state of nature as a war in which every man has a right to all things, the sovereign's right as natural right "left" to him rather than given, and a close summary of Lev. 21.17, on the liberty of rebels already condemned to "joyn together to assist and defend one another".

The company Hobbes keeps in that list is the point. Oxford condemned him not as an absolutist but as a theorist of self-preservation, resistance and de facto power, beside the Presbyterian and republican writers he had spent his life attacking. Its remedy was the Anglican doctrine of passive obedience, to be "clear, absolute and without exception", which Hobbes's argument from self-preservation could not supply. That is the first public statement of a tension still argued over (debate 4).

## The Continent: Pufendorf, Spinoza, Leibniz

European readers met a different Hobbes. As Malcolm pointed out in reviewing Warrender's edition (LRB, 1984), the Elzevir pocket editions printed at Amsterdam kept *De Cive* at the center of Continental attention: Pufendorf and Leibniz based their readings on it (Leibniz called it *subtilissimus*), it was the only work by Hobbes in Voltaire's library, and Kant cited it when he discussed Hobbes. The Continental Hobbes was the author of a compact treatise on natural law and sovereignty, with less Scripture and less polemic, read alongside Grotius as a founder of a new science of natural law.

**The Dutch.** The SEP's article on Spinoza's political philosophy credits the physician Lambert van Velthuysen with bringing Hobbes into Dutch political debate; his *Dissertatio* is an unabashed defense of Hobbes that puts the duty of self-preservation first. The republican brothers Pieter and Johan de la Court took over Hobbes's psychology while dropping the juridical language of right and contract. In the Dutch Republic, Hobbes's premises served anticlerical republicans, a reminder that they could be detached from his monarchical preferences.

**Spinoza.** The *Theologico-Political Treatise* (1670) identifies the right of nature with power: big fish eat small fish by the supreme right of nature (ch. 16). Asked by Jarig Jelles how he differed from Hobbes, Spinoza answered in Letter 50 that he always kept natural right intact and allowed the sovereign no more right over subjects than its power over them (SEP's translation, paraphrased here). Hobbes needs right to be a title that can be laid down and transferred; Spinoza denies that natural right is a title at all. The SEP adds that Hobbes's own account moved: *De Cive* limited natural right by right reason, so that drunkenness and cruelty are not done by right even in the state of nature, while *Leviathan* seems to drop such limits, a "de facto" turn on which it cites Kinch Hoekstra and Sandra Field. Velthuysen, the defender of Hobbes, denounced Spinoza's book as atheist, which shows how close the two names stood (see the library's [Spinoza](../../eras/03-early-modern/baruch-spinoza.md) entry and dossier).

**Pufendorf.** Samuel Pufendorf's *Elements of Universal Jurisprudence* (1660) is, in the SEP's words, explicitly based on Grotius and Hobbes. In *On the Law of Nature and of Nations* (1672) he took Hobbes's distinction of law from counsel and covenant (*De Cive* 14.1) and added that the superior who obliges needs both the strength to threaten and legitimate reasons to command. On the state of nature he answered Hobbes directly: Hobbes was right about a pure state of nature, but any real natural state is a state of peace, though an imperfect, unstable and insecure one, and natural law commands a peaceable sociality (SEP, citing DJN II.2.7, II.2.12, II.3.15). Through Pufendorf's textbooks a domesticated Hobbes entered the eighteenth-century natural-law curriculum.

**Leibniz.** Of the canonical philosophers from Descartes to Kant, the SEP judges, Leibniz paid most attention to Hobbes. As a young man he adopted the idea that reasoning is computation, praising Hobbes (1666) as "everywhere a profound examiner of principles", and studied his notion of *conatus*, the beginning of motion; he also called him a "super-nominalist" who made truth depend on definitions and so on the human will (1670; both in the SEP's translation). He wrote to Hobbes twice in the 1670s, apparently without reply, and his mature philosophy argues against Hobbes's necessitarianism and his voluntarist account of justice (from memory); the *Theodicy* discusses Hobbes's views (SEP), including, from memory, the dispute with Bramhall.

## The eighteenth century

In Britain the eighteenth century read Hobbes chiefly as the egoist to be refuted. Shaftesbury, Francis Hutcheson and Joseph Butler built their ethics against a selfish psychology they took to be his (see the library's [moral sense school](../../eras/03-early-modern/moral-sense-school.md) entry); Butler's *Fifteen Sermons* (1726) answered Hobbes's account of pity directly (from memory). [Hume](../../eras/03-early-modern/david-hume.md) sided with benevolence against the selfish system, yet he is the most Hobbesian of the empiricists in method: the SEP endorses Paul Russell's argument that the *Treatise* is modeled on the structure of the *Elements of Law*, notes that the copy theory of ideas resembles Hobbes's decaying sense, and suggests that Hobbes, not Locke, might be called the first British empiricist. Hume's justice, an artificial virtue resting on convention and interest, echoes Hobbes too.

[Locke](../../eras/03-early-modern/john-locke.md) is a harder case. The *Two Treatises* (1689) name Filmer, not Hobbes, as their target (from memory), and the DNB judged that Locke owed Hobbes little; the SEP lists the connections, among them anti-nativism, attention to language and its abuses, openness to materialism, and skepticism about revelation. The textbook picture of Locke answering Hobbes is true of the logic of the two theories rather than of Locke's stated targets.

In France the reception was warmer. Rousseau's *Discourse on Inequality* (1755) kept the method of the state of nature and turned it against Hobbes, who had, Rousseau argued, put into natural man passions such as pride that only society produces (the DNB notes Rousseau's interest in the *Inequality* and the *Social Contract*; Argument 6 states the objection). Diderot, in the DNB's account, expressed enthusiastic admiration for Hobbes in the *Encyclopédie*.

## Bentham, Austin and the utilitarians

"After being much neglected in England" Hobbes's fame, Leslie Stephen wrote, "was rehabilitated by the utilitarians", who found congenial his clarity and rationalism, his doctrine of association, his recognition of utility as the aim of social action, and his theory of sovereignty (DNB). Jeremy Bentham, the SEP's history of utilitarianism reports, was influenced by Hobbes's account of human nature as well as by Hume's account of social utility, and his apparently Hobbesian egoism sits uneasily with his principle that we ought to promote the general happiness. The radical politician Sir William Molesworth edited the first collected edition, the *English Works* (11 volumes) and *Opera Latina* (5 volumes), London 1839–45 (SEP). It is still cited, and Malcolm in 1984 called it incomplete, unreliable in its transcriptions and arbitrary in its copy-texts, which is why the Clarendon Edition was begun.

The second inheritance was legal. John Austin's *The Province of Jurisprudence Determined* (1832) defined law as the command of a sovereign backed by a sanction, and separated the existence of law from its merit; the SEP's article on Austin names Hobbes, "with his amoral view of laws as the product of Leviathan", among the theorists who foreshadowed legal positivism. The line runs from *Leviathan*'s definition of civil law as the rules the commonwealth has commanded (Lev. 26.3) through Austin to H. L. A. Hart's *The Concept of Law* (1961), which takes the command theory apart (see the library's [Hart](../../eras/05-20th-century/h-l-a-hart.md) entry). Whether Hobbes was himself a positivist is disputed. He says that "The Law of Nature, and the Civill Law, contain each other" (Lev. 26.8), and David Dyzenhaus and others have read him as a theorist of the rule of law rather than of bare command (Dyzenhaus and Poole, eds., *Hobbes and the Law*, 2013, is in the SEP's bibliography; the positions are from memory).

## Tönnies and the nineteenth-century revival

The scholarly revival was German. In 1889 Ferdinand Tönnies published *The Elements of Law* and *Behemoth*, edited from manuscripts, the first modern critical editions of any work of Hobbes (Routledge's catalogue description of the reprint; details in [02-works-and-texts.md](02-works-and-texts.md)). His *Hobbes: Leben und Lehre* (1896; second edition 1912, third 1925, per the publisher of his collected works) was the first serious intellectual biography. Tönnies's own sociology, *Gemeinschaft und Gesellschaft* (1887), made Hobbes the theorist of *Gesellschaft*, society as an artificial association of calculating individuals bound by contract (from memory), a reading that runs forward to Macpherson. In England, Leslie Stephen's DNB article gave the Victorian verdict on the prose: *Leviathan* is "a model of vigorous exposition, unsurpassed in the language".

## The twentieth century: Strauss, Oakeshott, Warrender, Macpherson

Carl Schmitt had called Hobbes by far the greatest and perhaps the only truly systematic political thinker (*The Concept of the Political*, 1927, as Strauss later recalled it), and his *Der Leviathan in der Staatslehre des Thomas Hobbes* (1938) read the book as the founding myth of the neutral modern state (from memory). **Leo Strauss**'s *The Political Philosophy of Hobbes: Its Basis and Its Genesis* appeared at Oxford in 1936, in Elsa Sinclair's translation of his German text. Its thesis is that the basis of the politics is not the new natural science but a moral attitude formed earlier, in Hobbes's humanist years: the opposition between vanity, which leads to war, and the fear of violent death, which leads to peace. The geometrical method gave the doctrine its form, not its content. Strauss also made Hobbes the founder of modern political philosophy, the first to put natural right, grounded in the passions, in place of a natural law grounded in human perfection. The IEP reports that most commentators now accept his point that science gave Hobbes a method and metaphors but not his substantive view of human nature (it dates Strauss's argument to the 1960s, a slip noted in the corrections log). Strauss's suggestion that Hobbes wrote cautiously about religion became a starting point for the atheist reading (debate 3).

**Michael Oakeshott**'s "Introduction to *Leviathan*" (1946, for Blackwell's student edition), his review of Strauss (1937), "*Leviathan*: A Myth" (1947) and "The Moral Life in the Writings of Thomas Hobbes" (1960) were collected as *Hobbes on Civil Association* (1975; contents per the Liberty Fund edition). Oakeshott read Hobbes as a skeptic about reason in politics and a theorist of individuality: the state does not set ends for its members but prescribes the conditions under which they pursue their own, an "adverbial" conception of law. On this reading, as Malcolm put it in 1984, Hobbes is liberal in a way that rationalist liberalism is not, and Oakeshott was one of the few modern readers to think his picture of human nature and the state true.

**A. E. Taylor and Howard Warrender.** Taylor's "The Ethical Doctrine of Hobbes" (*Philosophy* 13, 1938; reprinted in K. C. Brown, ed., *Hobbes Studies*, 1965) argued that Hobbes's ethics is logically independent of his egoistic psychology and is a strict deontology in which the laws of nature bind as God's commands. Warrender's *The Political Philosophy of Hobbes: His Theory of Obligation* (1957) developed the view. In Malcolm's summary, Warrender held that Hobbes did not reduce moral oughts to prudential shoulds or psychological musts: obligation exists independently and is merely prevented from operating when certain prudential or psychological conditions are not met. That let him honor Hobbes's claim that the laws of nature always bind in conscience even when they cannot safely be obeyed, and put Hobbes close to the natural-law tradition. Malcolm counted Warrender, Skinner and Oakeshott as the three men behind the post-war boom in Hobbes studies, and Warrender's book as the one that produced most discussion. The "Taylor–Warrender thesis" is the usual label, though Clark Tarlton has argued that Taylor's essay drew no response at the time and that a "Taylor thesis" is a later construction (PhilPapers abstract). Thomas Nagel ("Hobbes's Concept of Obligation", 1959) led the reply that Hobbes's obligation is rational self-interest under another name (debate 2).

**C. B. Macpherson**'s *The Political Theory of Possessive Individualism: Hobbes to Locke* (1962) gave the Marxist reading its classic form. The men of Hobbes's state of nature are not natural men but men of a possessive market society, whose capacities are commodities, as Hobbes's own definition has it: "The Value, or WORTH of a man, is as of all other things, his Price" (Lev. 10.16). Hobbes had taken bourgeois society for human nature, and his sovereign is the state such a society needs. Keith Thomas's essay "The Social Origins of Hobbes's Political Thought" (1965, from memory) is often called the refutation, stressing Hobbes's aristocratic values; Isaiah Berlin reviewed the book critically in 1964, and later historians, in the words of a recent *Modern Intellectual History* abstract, find that the evidence does not support either the possessive market society or Hobbes as a bourgeois ideologist. Macpherson's Penguin *Leviathan* (1968) carried his introduction to generations of students.

Two further strands belong to these decades. J. W. N. Watkins's *Hobbes's System of Ideas* (1965) argued, against Strauss, that the politics does follow from the natural philosophy (position from memory). And international-relations realists made "Hobbesian" the name for international anarchy, citing the sovereigns who stand toward one another "in the state and posture of Gladiators" (Lev. 13.12).

## The Cambridge contextualists: Skinner, Tuck, Malcolm

From the 1960s Quentin Skinner, in what Malcolm called a famous series of articles, set Hobbes's ideas in their detailed historical context. Their best-known claim is that in the Engagement controversy of 1649–52, over whether subjects might swear loyalty to the new republic, Hobbes was read as a theorist of de facto power, so that *Leviathan*'s account of "what point of time it is, that a Subject becomes obliged to the Conquerour" (Lev. R&C.6) entered a live debate (article titles and dates from memory). *Reason and Rhetoric in the Philosophy of Hobbes* (1996) traced Hobbes from a humanist trained in rhetoric, through the demonstrative science of the *Elements* and *De Cive*, to a *Leviathan* that returns to rhetorical persuasion (summary from memory); *Hobbes and Republican Liberty* (2008) is discussed below. Richard Tuck's *Natural Rights Theories* (1979) placed Hobbes in the line of rights theorists from Grotius and gave, in Malcolm's judgment, the most penetrating criticism of Warrender; Tuck's *Hobbes* (1989), his Cambridge *Leviathan* (1991) and *Philosophy and Government 1572–1651* (1993) read Hobbes as answering the skeptics Montaigne and Charron with a minimal ethics of self-preservation (titles in the SEP; position from memory). Noel Malcolm edited the *Correspondence* for the Clarendon Edition, collected *Aspects of Hobbes* (2002), and in 2012 published the three-volume Clarendon *Leviathan* with English and Latin side by side (SEP). Jeffrey Collins's *The Allegiance of Thomas Hobbes* (2005) argued that *Leviathan*'s church politics favored the Independents and Cromwell (from memory); Johann Sommerville, and S. A. Lloyd in a 2022 article the SEP lists, argue against reading Hobbes as approving Independency. The contextualists' shared lesson is that Hobbes argued with particular opponents for particular ends, so that differences among his treatises (debate 5) are evidence, not noise.

## Game theorists and rational-choice readers

David Gauthier's *The Logic of Leviathan* (1969) took obligation and authorization as Hobbes's basic moral and political concepts and concluded, in the publisher's summary, that Hobbes bases unlimited political authority on unlimited individualism, but that from unlimited individualism only anarchy follows: the theory fails, though its concepts deserve attention. Gauthier's *Morals by Agreement* (1986) then built a contractarian ethics on Hobbesian ground (SEP, "Contractarianism"; Argument 9). Gregory Kavka's *Hobbesian Moral and Political Theory* (1986) used elementary game theory to model the war of all against all, attributed to Hobbes a "predominant egoism" and a "rule egoism" (chapter and section titles), and argued that his absolutism rests on false hypotheses of political sociology; with those removed, Hobbesian principles support a liberal redistributive state and a limited right of revolution (Princeton abstract). Jean Hampton's *Hobbes and the Social Contract Tradition* (1986) put the sharpest dilemma: if the war comes from the passions, people will still have them after the contract and will not keep it; if it comes from prisoner's-dilemma rationality, rational people will defect from the contract as they defected before (SEP, "Contractarianism"). She also argued that the retained right of self-defense turns Hobbes's "alienation" contract into revocable agency (debate 4).

S. A. Lloyd's *Ideals as Interests in Hobbes's Leviathan* (1992) objected that the rational-choice readings leave out what Hobbes thought mattered most: "transcendent interests", which people rank above the fear of death, religious ones above all, and which in his diagnosis caused most of the disorder of his time (Cambridge UP description). Parts III and IV of *Leviathan* then aim to reshape those interests, not to bribe or threaten selfish agents.

## Feminist and republican readings

**Feminist readings.** What Hobbes says about women pulls two ways. He denies that dominion over children belongs to the man "as being of the more excellent Sex; they misreckon in it" (Lev. 20.4), and holds that in the state of nature, without a contract, "the Dominion is in the Mother" (Lev. 20.5), with the Amazons as his example. Yet in commonwealths the civil law "for the most part" favors the father, "because for the most part Common-wealths have been erected by the Fathers, not by the Mothers of families" (Lev. 20.4). Carole Pateman, in *The Sexual Contract* (1988) and "'God hath ordained to man a helper': Hobbes, Patriarchy and Conjugal Right" (1989), argued that the social contract presupposes a sexual contract by which women are subordinated, so that Hobbes's naturally free women have vanished by the time the covenant is made. Gabriella Slomp ("Hobbes and the Equality of Women", 1994) and Susanne Sreedhar ("Hobbes on 'The Woman Question'", 2012; "The Curious Case of Hobbes's Amazons", 2019) stress the egalitarian premises and ask how far the patriarchal conclusions are integral to the theory. Nancy Hirschmann's *Gender, Class, and Freedom in Modern Political Theory* (Princeton, 2008, year from memory) and her collection with Joanne Wright, *Feminist Interpretations of Thomas Hobbes* (2012), read his concept of freedom as shaped by gender and class. The SEP article treats gender and race together, noting the same tension between Hobbes's use of the peoples of America to illustrate the state of nature and his denial of innate differences between them and Europeans.

**Republican readings.** Skinner's *Hobbes and Republican Liberty* (2008) argued that *Leviathan*'s definition of liberty as "the absence of Opposition; (by Opposition, I mean externall Impediments of motion;)" (Lev. 21.1) was a new and deliberate weapon against the republican view that one is unfree merely by living at the mercy of another's will. Hobbes's jibe that the word "LIBERTAS" painted on the turrets of Lucca gives no citizen there more liberty than a subject in Constantinople (Lev. 21.8) is aimed at that tradition. Philip Pettit's *Made with Words* (2008) reads the politics through Hobbes's theory of language and agrees that he is the great opponent of freedom as non-domination. Others find a democratic Hobbes: Kinch Hoekstra ("A Lion in the House: Hobbes and Democracy", 2007), Jason Martel (*Subverting the Leviathan*, 2007) and Richard Tuck's *The Sleeping Sovereign* (2016, from memory), which reads Hobbes as distinguishing a democratic sovereign from the government it appoints.

## Live debates

Debates that [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md) sets out with their texts are summarized here, with pointers.

**(1) Was Hobbes an egoist?** The IEP calls this the controversy that has dominated interpretation. The traditional answer, from Cumberland and Butler to the textbooks, made Hobbes a psychological egoist for whom benevolence and pity are self-interest in disguise; Kavka's "predominant egoism" is a weaker version, and rational-choice readers model Hobbesian agents as maximizers. Against this, Bernard Gert ("Hobbes and Psychological Egoism", 1967) argued that Hobbes's definitions allow unselfish motives, such as "Desire of good to another, BENEVOLENCE, GOOD WILL, CHARITY. If to man generally, GOOD NATURE" (Lev. 6.22), and that his claims about self-interest describe what usually moves people, not what must. The SEP reports that the formerly dominant egoist reading is now widely rejected, and the IEP adds that much of the trouble Hobbes diagnoses comes from people caring too little for their own interest: they fight for glory, opinion and salvation. What stays open is how far chapters 13–15 need egoism at all. Hampton and the game theorists derive the war from rationality under insecurity, whatever people's final aims, and the IEP argues that the problem cannot be escaped by a kinder view of human nature (Arguments 4–6).

**(2) Prudential advice or moral obligation?** Hobbes supplies both answers in one paragraph. The dictates of reason are called laws "improperly: for they are but Conclusions, or Theoremes concerning what conduceth to the conservation and defence of themselves"; yet "if we consider the same Theoremes, as delivered in the word of God, that by right commandeth all things; then are they properly called Lawes" (Lev. 15.41). Taylor, Warrender, F. C. Hood (*The Divine Politics of Thomas Hobbes*, 1964) and Martinich (1992) take the second clause as the key: the laws bind because God commands them. Nagel (1959), Gauthier (1969) and Kavka (1986) take the first: they are hypothetical imperatives of self-preservation, and covenant and authorization do the rest. A third group looks for a normativity that is neither command nor prudence: Stephen Darwall ("Normativity and Projection in Hobbes's *Leviathan*", 2000) and Lloyd, whose *Morality in the Philosophy of Thomas Hobbes* (2009) grounds the laws in reciprocity (both in the SEP list; positions from memory). The SEP's summary of the state of play: no agreement on whether the laws are precepts of prudence, divine commands or moral imperatives of some other sort, but agreement that they direct people to submit to political authority (Arguments 7 and 10).

**(3) Was Hobbes an atheist?** Cudworth thought so in 1678. Among moderns, Edwin Curley ("I durst not write so boldly", 1988; and "Calvin and Hobbes, or Hobbes as an orthodox Christian", a reply to Martinich, PhilPapers) argues that the chapters on prophecy and miracles contain a circle Hobbes may have meant readers to find, and suggests, as Strauss might have, that he left the conclusion to them; Douglas Jesseph (2002) argues that the claims about a corporeal God do not add up (both as summarized by the SEP). On the other side, Martinich's *The Two Gods of Leviathan* (1992) argues that Hobbes was defending the Calvinist Church of England of James I's reign and that the burden of proof lies on those who call him an atheist (Cambridge UP description), and Hood and Warrender make Hobbes's God the ground of obligation. The SEP takes a middle position that is now common: Hobbes was a theist of an unusual kind, who offered a cosmological argument, held God incomprehensible, and later called God a "corporeal spirit" (*Answer to Bishop Bramhall*, as the SEP quotes it), which is odd theism, not atheism. It adds a practical point: for a man supposedly hiding his unbelief to avoid trouble, Hobbes said a remarkable number of other dangerous things, against free will, immaterial souls, Presbyterians and Catholics alike. Tom Holden's *Hobbes's Philosophy of Religion* (2023) is the latest full study (SEP), and much of the debate now runs through the Latin *Leviathan* and its appendix on the Nicene Creed ([02-works-and-texts.md](02-works-and-texts.md); Week 6).

**(4) Does the right of self-defense undo absolutism?** The sovereign is to be absolute, yet "If the Soveraign command a man (though justly condemned,) to kill, wound, or mayme himselfe; or not to resist those that assault him; or to abstain from the use of food, ayre, medicine, or any other thing, without which he cannot live; yet hath that man the Liberty to disobey" (Lev. 21.12). Condemned rebels may join to defend themselves (Lev. 21.17), and obedience lasts only as long as the power to protect (Lev. 21.21). Bramhall called the book a rebel's catechism, and Oxford in 1683 agreed. Hampton (1986) argued that the retained right makes each subject judge of when his life is threatened, so the alienation of private judgment on which absolutism depends never happens. Kavka (1986) turned the tension into a Hobbesian case for a limited right of revolution. Sreedhar (*Hobbes on Resistance*, 2010) argues that the retained rights extend beyond self-defense, to family and honor, and yet form a coherent doctrine consistent with an unlimited sovereign, because a right to resist is not a right to judge for others or to govern (Cambridge UP chapter summaries; SEP); a reviewer in *Notre Dame Philosophical Reviews* read her as giving subjects a collective right of rebellion, which shows how far readings can stretch. The SEP calls the limits of obligation a possible Achilles' heel of the theory and leaves open whether the charge stands (Argument 14).

**(5) *Leviathan*, *De Cive* and the *Elements*.** Hobbes presented the three works as one theory, and many readers use them interchangeably. Others see real changes. Gauthier (1969) made the theory of authorization, and the sovereign as representative, the decisive novelty of *Leviathan*. The SEP agrees that in the *Elements* and *De Cive* each person lays down his rights in favor of the sovereign, while in *Leviathan* each covenants to "own and authorize" the sovereign's actions, which raises the question who is responsible for acts done at the sovereign's command. Malcolm (1984) argued that *De Cive* already has a theory of authorization, and a populist element, the people as a political body formed before the monarch, that *Leviathan* drops. Skinner adds the new definition of liberty, aimed at the republicans (2008), and the SEP's Spinoza article the "de facto" turn on natural right. The religious chapters also grow with each version until they fill half of *Leviathan* (SEP). The dossier takes the SEP's line: one project, restated three times with changes that matter, so *Leviathan* chs. 13–21 are read with *De Cive* beside them (Week 3 sets the *De Cive* Preface).

## Misconceptions, in full form

The [library entry](../../eras/03-early-modern/thomas-hobbes.md)'s "Misreadings to avoid" and the [study guide](../../study/hobbes-leviathan.md)'s "Common misreadings" give the short forms.

- **"Hobbes says human beings are evil."** He denies it twice. After asking the reader why he locks his doors and chests, he adds: "But neither of us accuse mans nature in it. The Desires, and other Passions of man, are in themselves no Sin" (Lev. 13.10). The Preface to *De Cive* meets the charge head on: "this, that men are evil by nature, follows not from this principle", for even if the wicked were fewer than the righteous, we cannot tell them apart and so must guard against all; a wicked man is "almost the same thing with a child grown strong and sturdy" (*De Cive*, Preface, Molesworth II). The argument of chapter 13 is structural: equality, scarcity, uncertainty and the lack of a common power make even moderate people dangerous to each other (Arguments 5–6). What Hobbes does hold is that people are not naturally sociable in Aristotle's sense and that pride is common and destructive.

- **"Life is 'solitary, poore, nasty, brutish, and short': that is Hobbes's view of human life."** The sentence describes one condition, the time "men live without a common Power to keep them all in awe" (Lev. 13.8), and its list of what that condition lacks ends with "continuall feare, and danger of violent death; And the life of man, solitary, poore, nasty, brutish, and short" (Lev. 13.9). Hobbes adds that "it was never generally so, over all the world" (Lev. 13.11), points to civil war as the experience that shows it, and counts among the passions that incline men to peace the "Desire of such things as are necessary to commodious living" (Lev. 13.14). The commonwealth exists so that life will be none of the five things. The condition is not wholly hypothetical either: he thinks "the savage people in many places of America" live so now (Lev. 13.11), a passage the SEP discusses under race, and that sovereigns live so toward one another (Lev. 13.12).

- **"Hobbes was a royalist absolutist, without qualification."** He was associated with the royalists, fled in 1640, taught the Prince of Wales mathematics and thought monarchy the most convenient form. But the three kinds of commonwealth differ "not in the difference of Power; but in the difference of Convenience, or Aptitude to produce the Peace, and Security of the people" (Lev. 19.4), and an assembly can be Leviathan. Authority comes from covenant, not divine right or descent. Obedience lasts as long as protection (Lev. 21.21), which is why the "Review, and Conclusion" tells defeated royalists when they may submit to a conqueror (Lev. R&C.6), why Clarendon accused him of writing for Cromwell, and why Oxford condemned him beside Milton and Baxter. Subjects keep liberties no covenant can transfer (Lev. 21.12–17). "Absolute" means undivided and unaccountable to subjects, not exempt from the laws of nature, which bind the sovereign before God (debate 4; Arguments 12 and 14).

- **"*Leviathan*'s frontispiece is a single image."** There are at least two versions, and one is not printed. The engraved title page of 1651, attributed to Abraham Bosse (BnF; Utpictura18), shows a crowned giant holding sword and crozier over a landscape and a town, under the motto from Job 41 that there is no power on earth to compare with him; below, in two columns, are temporal emblems on the left (castle, crown, cannon, arms, battle) and spiritual ones on the right (church, thunderbolt, the weapons of disputation, a tribunal), with the title between them (BnF's description). The giant's body is made of small full-length figures seen from behind. The drawing in the vellum manuscript Hobbes presented to Charles II, now British Library Egerton MS 1910, differs: Malcolm describes the body as made of much larger faces looking outward at the viewer ("The Title Page of *Leviathan*, Seen in a Curious Perspective", in *Aspects of Hobbes*). Even the printed image varies, since the "Bear" edition used the worn plate and gives a fainter impression (Columbia RBML). Claims about what the picture means, for representation or for the people's gaze, depend on which version one reads; Horst Bredekamp's book-length study (English edition, De Gruyter, 2020) is the place to start.

- **"The sovereign is a party to the contract."** The covenant is made by each subject with each, and the sovereign is not a party, so the sovereign cannot breach it (Lev. 18; Argument 11). The second proposition Oxford condemned in 1683, a mutual compact between prince and subjects, belongs to the resistance theorists Hobbes opposed.

## Myths about the life

[01-life-and-times.md](01-life-and-times.md) ("Weighing the sources") sorts the stories by witness. The common myths:

- **"Fear and I were born twins together."** A modern paraphrase of the verse autobiography, not Hobbes's wording; the premature birth from fear of the Armada rests on Hobbes, writing at eighty-four, and on Aubrey repeating him.
- **Galileo's friend, Bacon's disciple, Charles II's tutor.** The Galileo friendship rests on Aubrey and is absent from Hobbes's own lives (SEP). Hobbes worked for Bacon around 1620, but discipleship goes beyond the evidence. He taught the Prince of Wales mathematics and himself denied being his tutor.
- **"*Leviathan* was written to win Cromwell's favor."** The charge is Clarendon's and Wallis's. Hobbes rejected it in his *Considerations* (1662), and the DNB judged the story of an offer from Cromwell groundless, while recording Hobbes's own boast (1656) that the book had reconciled many gentlemen to submission. The book's purpose in 1651 is a real debate (the contextualists above); a bargain with Cromwell is not.
- **"Parliament condemned *Leviathan*."** A Commons committee was empowered in 1666 to receive information about it; the bill died and no court tried him. The one formal condemnation was Oxford's, four years after his death. The burned papers rest on Aubrey.
- **The deathbed "leap in the dark".** The last words usually given, about a last voyage and a great leap in the dark, are attributed, not recorded (Oxford Reference lists them as attributed), and in Vanbrugh's *The Provoked Wife* (1697) a character already speaks of setting out on "Hobbes's Voyage". White Kennett's report that the old Hobbes went to chapel and took the sacrament, though he turned his back on the sermon (DNB), is the nearest contemporary evidence about his practice, and it is hearsay.
