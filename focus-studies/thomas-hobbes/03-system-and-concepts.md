## The order of the system

Hobbes planned a system and said what its order was. In the Preface to *De Cive* he explains that he had gathered "its first elements in all kinds" and "digested them into three sections by degrees": the first "of body and its general properties", the second "of man and his special faculties and affections", the third "of civil government and the duties of subjects" (*De Cive* Preface, Molesworth II). The three Latin volumes that carried out the plan appeared out of order. *De Cive* (On the Citizen) came first, in 1642, because, as the same Preface says, his country was "boiling hot with questions concerning the rights of dominion and the obedience due from subjects", so that "what was last in order, is yet come forth first in time." *De Corpore* (On Body) followed in 1655 and *De Homine* (On Man) in 1658. *Leviathan* (1651) is not one of the three parts. It is a single English book that runs through man and commonwealth, with the philosophy of body compressed into its first chapters, and then spends its second half on religion.

The central question is the one the study guide puts at the front: what arrangement could people who fear violent death, and who cannot trust one another, rationally make to get out of a condition in which no one is safe? Hobbes's distinctive bet is that the answer has to be built from the bottom. Bodies in motion explain sensation; sensation explains thought; speech turns thought into reasoning; motion inside the body, felt as appetite and aversion, explains action; and the predictable collisions of many such bodies, each striving to keep itself in motion, explain why there must be a sovereign. The *Leviathan* Introduction sets this up as an analogy. Nature makes animals, and human art imitates nature by making automata, "Engines that move themselves by springs and wheeles as doth a watch". By the same art "is created that great LEVIATHAN called a COMMON-WEALTH, or STATE, (in latine CIVITAS) which is but an Artificiall Man" (Lev. Intro.). To understand the artificial man you study first its matter and its maker, "both which is Man" (Lev. Intro.).

This file sets out the load-bearing concepts in that order, in Hobbes's own definitions, before any criticism. Each Hobbes concept comes with a definition, and his definitions are deliberately different from ordinary usage: "right", "law", "liberty", "person", "author", "justice" and "power" all have technical senses, and most misreadings begin by importing the everyday sense. The arguments that use these concepts are reconstructed in [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), numbered 1–16; the scholarly fights over them are in [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md). The learning path reads *Leviathan* chs. 13–17 in excerpt and the rest at map level (Stage 3, row 6); this dossier goes further, through the psychology of Part I, the theory of sovereignty in Part II and the religion of Parts III–IV, and this file says where it does.

**Texts and quotation.** *Leviathan* is quoted from the 1651 text as printed by Project Gutenberg (eBook #3207), and quotations keep the 1651 spelling and capitals ("Warre", "Soveraign", "Covenants") exactly as that text prints them. (The Gutenberg transcriber kept Hobbes's spelling and punctuation, but rendered most of his italics as initial capitals and printed his marginal notes as short headings, so some capitals in the quotations are the transcriber's way of showing italics.) Citations give chapter and paragraph, e.g. (Lev. 13.9), counting paragraphs in that saved text from the chapter title and skipping the printed marginal headings; the Introduction and "A Review, and Conclusion" (the book's closing chapter) are cited without paragraph numbers. Modern editions number paragraphs too, and an occasional count may differ by one where the Gutenberg text breaks a paragraph differently. The reading text is not this one: read Tuck's Cambridge edition or Curley's Hackett edition, which modernize spelling and, in Curley's case, number the paragraphs and give the main variants of the 1668 Latin version. *De Cive* is quoted from the 1651 English version, *Philosophical Rudiments concerning Government and Society*, as reprinted in Molesworth's *English Works* vol. II (1841); *Of Liberty and Necessity* from Molesworth vol. IV (1840), cited as LN with the volume page. Those Molesworth texts are scanned, and the quotations follow the scan's wording. *De Corpore* and *De Homine* are paraphrased, with chapter and section.

## Method: resolution, composition and the example of geometry

Hobbes defines philosophy, at the start of *De Corpore*, as knowledge acquired by correct reasoning: knowledge of effects from their causes, and of causes from their visible effects (*De Corpore* 1.2 and 6.1, paraphrased; SEP, "Thomas Hobbes," §4, gives the passage in translation). The two directions are the two halves of the method. Starting from an effect you know only confusedly, you take it apart in thought until you reach its causes; that is resolution, or analysis. Starting from causes you understand, you put the effect back together and see how it must come out; that is composition, or synthesis. Commentators call this the *resolutive-compositive* method. Hobbes sometimes uses the mathematicians' words, analysis and synthesis, for it (*De Corpore* 6.1). Whether he took it from the Paduan Aristotelian Jacopo Zabarella, perhaps by way of Galileo or Harvey, or from the practice of geometers, is an open question; the SEP article sets out both stories, finds the Galileo link hard to support, and suggests both may hold some truth (SEP, "Thomas Hobbes," §4).

The *De Cive* Preface gives the method its most quoted image. "For everything is best understood by its constitutive causes. For as in a watch, or some such small engine, the matter, figure, and motion of the wheels cannot well be known, except it be taken insunder and viewed in parts ; so to make a more curious search into the rights of states and duties of subjects, it is necessary, I say, not to take them insunder, but yet that they be so considered as if they were dissolved" (*De Cive* Preface, Molesworth II). That last clause is the method of the state of nature. Hobbes does not claim to have watched commonwealths fall apart into individuals. He asks the reader to consider a commonwealth "as if" dissolved, to see what human nature is like with the common power removed and what such people would have to agree to. The state of nature is resolution; the covenant and the sovereign are composition.

Geometry is the model of how the composing half should go. In *Leviathan* chapter 4 Hobbes calls it "the onely Science that it hath pleased God hitherto to bestow on mankind", and he says what geometers do right: "men begin at settling the significations of their words; which settling of significations, they call Definitions" (Lev. 4.12). A science, on this view, starts from definitions that fix what words mean and then draws out what follows. The story that Hobbes met Euclid at about forty, in a gentleman's library, and was drawn into geometry by working back through a proof he did not believe, comes from John Aubrey's *Brief Lives*; the SEP repeats it as Aubrey's anecdote (SEP, "Thomas Hobbes," §4).

Two features of the method shape what follows. First, Hobbes thinks that all real causes are efficient causes and that the cause of every change is motion (SEP, "Thomas Hobbes," §4). There are no final causes in nature, no substantial forms, no purposes that things strive toward because of what they are. Second, political science has an unusual advantage. We know best what we make, because we know its causes, and the commonwealth is something people make. Hobbes argues in *De Homine* (10.5, paraphrased) that geometry and civil philosophy can be demonstrated because we ourselves construct the figures and the commonwealth. The *Leviathan* Introduction adds a second route into the science of man, introspection: "Nosce Teipsum, Read Thy Self", because the passions are alike in everyone, though their objects differ, so that whoever looks into himself "shall thereby read and know, what are the thoughts, and Passions of all other men, upon the like occasions" (Lev. Intro.). The reader is asked only to check Hobbes's reading against their own, "For this kind of Doctrine, admitteth no other Demonstration" (Lev. Intro.).

There is a tension here that runs through the scholarship. If the politics is deduced from the physics, then a reader who rejects materialism must reject the politics. If it rests on introspection and experience, the physics is decoration. Hobbes himself says, in the *De Corpore* chapter on method, that civil philosophy can also be reached analytically, starting from the experience of one's own passions, by someone who has not learned the earlier parts (*De Corpore* 6.7, paraphrased). The SEP article puts it plainly: how exactly the parts of the system are connected "has long been debated" (SEP, "Thomas Hobbes," §2). On one side, Leo Strauss (*The Political Philosophy of Hobbes*, 1936) argued that the political philosophy rests on a view of human nature that Hobbes did not get from his science; the IEP article on Hobbes's moral and political philosophy reports that most commentators now accept some version of this, holding that the mechanistic science supplies a method and metaphors rather than the substance of the psychology (IEP, "Hobbes: Moral and Political Philosophy," §4a). On the other side, J. W. N. Watkins (*Hobbes's System of Ideas*, 2nd ed. 1973) read the system as tightly unified by its method. The dispute is about how the three parts connect, not about whether Hobbes meant them to.

## Body: matter and motion

The first part of the system is the philosophy of body, and its thesis is short. Everything that exists is body, and every change is motion. *Leviathan* states it in the chapter on darkness from vain philosophy: the universe is body, every part of body is body, "and consequently every part of the Universe, is Body, and that which is not Body, is no part of the Universe: And because the Universe is all, that which is no part of it, is Nothing; and consequently No Where" (Lev. 46.15). *De Corpore* defines body as whatever does not depend on our thought and coincides with, or is coextended with, some part of space (*De Corpore* 8.1, paraphrased). Its qualities, or "accidents", are the ways in which body is conceived: magnitude, figure, motion and rest. Colors, sounds and smells are not in bodies at all; they are how bodies' motions appear to a perceiver (Lev. 1.4, below).

Three consequences follow at once, and Hobbes draws all of them.

First, *incorporeal substance* is a contradiction in terms. "Substance and Body, signifie the same thing; and therefore Substance Incorporeall are words, which when they are joined together, destroy one another" (Lev. 34.2). Chapter 4 puts "an Incorporeall Body, or (which is all one) an Incorporeall Substance" on the list of names that "signifie nothing", together with "Round Quadrangle" (Lev. 4.22). What Scripture calls spirits, Hobbes reads as thin, fluid, invisible bodies (Lev. 34.2–4, paraphrased). This is a philosophical thesis about meaning, not only a physics. A word for a thing that is no body names nothing.

Second, nothing moves itself. Chapter 2 opens with the principle of inertia, which Hobbes takes as obvious for rest and argues for motion: "That when a thing lies still, unlesse somewhat els stirre it, it will lye still for ever, is a truth that no man doubts of", and by the same reason a thing in motion stays in motion "unless somewhat els stay it" (Lev. 2.1). He uses it at once against the scholastics, who said heavy bodies fall "out of an appetite to rest", "ascribing appetite, and Knowledge of what is good for their conservation, (which is more than man has) to things inanimate absurdly" (Lev. 2.1). Every motion has as its cause a prior motion of a contiguous body. The same principle reappears in *Of Liberty and Necessity* as the ground of determinism: nothing begins from itself, but from the action of some other agent outside it, so that even a new appetite or will is caused by something not in the agent's own disposing (LN, Molesworth IV 274, paraphrased).

Third, the smallest units of motion are the link between physics and psychology. Hobbes calls motion through a space and time less than any that can be given "endeavour" (*conatus*) (*De Corpore* 15.2, paraphrased), and he uses the same word in *Leviathan* for the beginnings of animal motion inside the body (Lev. 6.1). Appetite, aversion, fear and hope will turn out to be endeavours. So the theory of the passions is, in principle, a part of mechanics.

How far the materialism reaches is debated. In *Leviathan*, God is said to be incomprehensible and the attributes we give him are expressions of honor, not descriptions (Lev. 3.12, 12.7, 46.15, paraphrased). In the *Answer to Bishop Bramhall* (dated 1662 in the SEP's bibliography; printed only in 1682, after his death) and the Appendix to the 1668 Latin *Leviathan*, Hobbes went further and called God a "corporeal spirit", an extended being mixed through the world (SEP, "Thomas Hobbes," §5, which reports the later view and calls it "strange indeed" but apparently sincere). Whether that is a heterodox theism or a disguised atheism is one of the dossier's running disputes (see Known internal tensions, below, and [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md)).

The account of body also explains why the Third Objections to Descartes matter so much for the system (Argument 3 in [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md)). Descartes had argued from "I think" to "I am a thing that thinks", a mind distinct from body. Hobbes accepts the inference to a thinking thing and rejects the step to an immaterial one. To pass from exercising thought to being thought is like passing from walking to the conclusion "I am the walking" (Obj. III, Obj. 2; Haldane & Ross, vol. II (1911–12; corrected reprint 1934), 61). We know an act only by conceiving a subject for it, and such subjects can be "conceived only after a corporeal fashion, or as in material guise" (Obj. III, Obj. 2; Haldane & Ross, vol. II, 62). So, Hobbes concludes, the thing that thinks seems to be something corporeal (Obj. III, Obj. 2, paraphrased). Descartes replied that he had never identified the thinker with its act, and that acts of thought and bodily acts belong to different kinds of substance, which we tell apart by their acts (Reply to Obj. 2, paraphrased). The exchange is set as a week 2 reading.

## Sense, imagination and trains of thought

*Leviathan* begins its account of man with thoughts taken "first Singly, and afterwards in Trayne" (Lev. 1.1). Singly, each thought is "a Representation or Apparence, of some quality, or other Accident of a body without us" (Lev. 1.1). And every thought begins in sense: "there is no conception in a mans mind, which hath not at first, totally, or by parts, been begotten upon the organs of Sense" (Lev. 1.2). That is an empiricist starting point, though Hobbes's interest in it is causal, not epistemological.

**Sense** is the effect of external motion on the body (Argument 1 in [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md)). The object presses the organ, the pressure is carried by the nerves to the brain and heart, and there it causes "a resistance, or counter-pressure, or endeavour of the heart, to deliver it self: which endeavour because Outward, seemeth to be some matter without" (Lev. 1.4). Light, color, sound and heat are how this counter-pressure appears. In the object they are "but so many several motions of the matter", and in us they are motions too, "for motion, produceth nothing but motion" (Lev. 1.4). "So that Sense in all cases, is nothing els but originall fancy, caused (as I have said) by the pressure, that is, by the motion, of externall things upon our Eyes, Eares, and other organs" (Lev. 1.4). The evidence Hobbes offers is that appearances can be separated from objects: pressing the eye makes us "fancy a light", and in echoes and mirrors "the thing we see, is in one place; the apparence, in another" (Lev. 1.4). The theory is aimed at the scholastic account in which objects send out "species" that enter the senses and the understanding, which he treats as an example of "insignificant Speech" (Lev. 1.5).

**Imagination** is what remains when the object is gone, the motion continuing, more weakly, after the cause has stopped, as waves go on after the wind drops (Lev. 2.2, paraphrased). "Imagination therefore is nothing but Decaying Sense; and is found in men, and many other living Creatures, as well sleeping, as waking" (Lev. 2.2). Memory is the same thing under another description: when we mean the decay itself, "that the Sense is fading, old, and past, it is called Memory. So that Imagination and Memory, are but one thing" (Lev. 2.3). "Much memory, or memory of many things, is called Experience" (Lev. 2.4). Dreams are imaginations in sleep, and the chapter ends with an account of how dreams and half-waking fancies gave rise to belief in ghosts, a first move in the naturalistic account of religion that chapter 12 completes (Lev. 2.5–9, paraphrased; see SEP, "Thomas Hobbes," §5).

A **train of thoughts**, or "Mentall Discourse", is "that succession of one Thought to another" (Lev. 3.1). Thoughts follow one another in the order in which the sensations followed one another, so even an apparently wild train has a thread. Hobbes's example is a conversation about the civil war in which someone asks the value of a Roman penny: the thought ran from the war to the king's betrayal, to the betrayal of Christ, to the thirty pence (Lev. 3.3, paraphrased). Trains are of two sorts. The first is "Unguided, Without Designee, and inconstant" (Lev. 3.3). The second is "Regulated by some desire, and designee" (Lev. 3.4). Regulated trains are again of two kinds: from an effect imagined we seek its causes, which animals do as well, or from something imagined we seek all its possible effects, which Hobbes thinks only humans do (Lev. 3.5). The second kind is the root of curiosity, and it is what makes science possible once speech is added.

Two limits follow from the account. Prudence, foresight drawn from experience, is never certain: though "it be called Prudence, when the Event answereth our Expectation; yet in its own nature, it is but Presumption", and "by how much one man has more experience of things past, than another; by so much also he is more Prudent" (Lev. 3.7). And because every idea comes from sense, "Whatsoever we imagine, is Finite. Therefore there is no Idea, or conception of anything we call Infinite" (Lev. 3.12). The name "God" therefore does not express an idea of God. It expresses our inability to conceive him and our will to honor him (Lev. 3.12, paraphrased), which is the point of contact with the Third Objections on the idea of God.

## Speech, names, definitions and reasoning

What lifts human beings above other animals is speech. "But the most noble and profitable invention of all other, was that of Speech, consisting of Names or Apellations, and their Connexion; whereby men register their Thoughts; recall them when they are past; and also declare them one to another" (Lev. 4.1). Speech has two main uses. Privately, names are marks that let us record and recall the consequences of our thoughts. Publicly, they are signs that let us declare our thoughts, wants and plans to others (Lev. 4.3, paraphrased). Hobbes adds that without speech there would have been "neither Common-wealth, nor Society, nor Contract, nor Peace, no more than amongst Lyons, Bears, and Wolves" (Lev. 4.1). That is the first hint that the commonwealth is, among other things, made with words. Philip Pettit's *Made with Words* (2008) builds a reading of the whole system on this thread (SEP, "Thomas Hobbes," bibliography).

**Names and nominalism.** A name is proper to one thing or common to many. A common name such as "man" is "the name of divers particular things; in respect of all which together, it is called an Universall; there being nothing in the world Universall but Names; for the things named, are every one of them Individual and Singular" (Lev. 4.6). Universals are not things, and not concepts either; generality belongs only to words. Hobbes's account of speech is usually called nominalist for this reason (SEP, "Thomas Hobbes," §2.3).

**Truth.** If generality lives in names, so does truth. In a true proposition the predicate names everything the subject names: *a man is a living creature* is true because the name *living creature* signifies everything the name *man* signifies. "For True and False are attributes of Speech, not of things. And where Speech in not, there is neither Truth nor Falshood" (Lev. 4.11). An animal without speech can be mistaken in its expectations, but it cannot assert a falsehood.

**Definitions.** It follows that the first thing a reasoner must do is fix what words mean, as geometers do: "in Geometry, (which is the onely Science that it hath pleased God hitherto to bestow on mankind,) men begin at settling the significations of their words; which settling of significations, they call Definitions" (Lev. 4.12). A reasoner who skips this step "will find himselfe entangled in words, as a bird in lime-twiggs; the more he struggles, the more belimed" (Lev. 4.12). The same paragraph gives the most quoted line in the chapter, aimed at the scholastics: "For words are wise mens counters, they do but reckon by them: but they are the mony of fooles, that value them by the authority of an Aristotle, a Cicero, or a Thomas" (Lev. 4.13). Names that join contradictory significations, such as "Round Quadrangle" or "Incorporeall Substance", are not false but meaningless: they are "a meere sound" (Lev. 4.22). And names of virtues and vices are of "Inconstant signification", because people name things by how they affect them (Lev. 4.25). That last point prepares the moral theory: in the state of nature, words like "good", "just" and "cruel" will not have a fixed public meaning.

**Reasoning as reckoning** (Argument 2 in [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md)). "When a man Reasoneth, hee does nothing els but conceive a summe totall, from Addition of parcels; or conceive a Remainder, from Substraction of one summe from another" (Lev. 5.1). Reason in the strict sense "is nothing but Reckoning (that is, Adding and Substracting) of the Consequences of generall names agreed upon, for the Marking and Signifying of our thoughts" (Lev. 5.2). Adding names makes an affirmation, adding affirmations makes a syllogism, adding syllogisms makes a demonstration. Leibniz took up the comparison with arithmetic, and the SEP treats the passage as an early statement of the idea that thinking is computation (SEP, "Thomas Hobbes," §2.4).

Three features of the doctrine have political weight. First, reasoning can go wrong, and those most exposed to error are the learned. Human beings alone have "the priviledge of Absurdity; to which no living creature is subject, but man onely. And of men, those are of all most subject to it, that professe Philosophy" (Lev. 5.7). Second, no one's reason is right reason just because it is theirs. When accountants disagree, "the parties must by their own accord, set up for right Reason, the Reason of some Arbitrator, or Judge, to whose sentence they will both stand, or their controversie must either come to blowes, or be undecided" (Lev. 5.3). This sentence anticipates the sovereign, who will be the arbitrator whose reason counts as public reason. Third, science is conditional knowledge: "Science is the knowledge of Consequences, and dependance of one fact upon another" (Lev. 5.17), as opposed to sense and memory, which are knowledge of fact. The chapter closes with a summary: "The Light of humane minds is Perspicuous Words, but by exact definitions first snuffed, and purged from ambiguity; Reason is the Pace; Encrease of Science, the Way; and the Benefit of man-kind, the End" (Lev. 5.20).

## Endeavour, appetite and aversion, the passions, deliberation and will

Chapter 6 turns from knowing to moving. Animals have two sorts of motion. Vital motion, such as the circulation of the blood, the pulse and breathing, runs from birth to death without imagination. Animal or voluntary motion, such as walking or speaking, is "first fancied in our minds" and so depends on imagination (Lev. 6.1, paraphrased). Between the image and the visible act there is invisible motion inside the body: "These small beginnings of Motion, within the body of Man, before they appear in walking, speaking, striking, and other visible actions, are commonly called ENDEAVOUR" (Lev. 6.1). Hobbes adds that the smallness of a motion is no argument against its being there, since whatever moves over a large space must first move over a small part of it (Lev. 6.1).

Endeavour has two directions. "This Endeavour, when it is toward something which causes it, is called APPETITE, or DESIRE", and "when the Endeavour is fromward something, it is generally called AVERSION" (Lev. 6.2). Love and hate are the same as desire and aversion, except that desire signifies the absence of the object and love its presence (Lev. 6.3, paraphrased). The physiology is set out in the paragraph on pleasure: an appetite felt as delight is "a corroboration of Vitall motion, and a help thereunto", and what hinders vital motion is felt as offensive (Lev. 6.10). The mechanism is simple: pleasure is what helps the life-motion of the body, pain what hinders it.

**Good and evil.** The value terms come straight out of this. "But whatsoever is the object of any mans Appetite or Desire; that is it, which he for his part calleth Good: And the object of his Hate, and Aversion, evill" (Lev. 6.7). These words "are ever used with relation to the person that useth them: There being nothing simply and absolutely so; nor any common Rule of Good and evill, to be taken from the nature of the objects themselves" (Lev. 6.7). The same paragraph says where a common rule can come from instead: from the person of the man where there is no commonwealth, or, in a commonwealth, from the person who represents it, or from an arbitrator the disputants agree to set up (Lev. 6.7, paraphrased). The theory of value and the theory of sovereignty meet in one sentence. Since appetites differ between people, and in one person over time, private judgments of good will conflict, and only a public judge can supply a common measure. Hobbes says it again in chapter 15: so long as a man is in the condition of mere nature, private appetite is the measure of good and evil (Lev. 15.40, paraphrased; the SEP quotes the sentence, "Hobbes's Moral and Political Philosophy," §3).

**The passions.** The rest of chapter 6 defines the passions as variants of a few simple ones (appetite, desire, love, aversion, hate, joy and grief), distinguished by the opinion that accompanies them, the object, or the way they succeed one another (Lev. 6.13, paraphrased). The definitions are compact. Hope is "Appetite with an opinion of attaining"; despair, "The same, without such opinion"; fear, "Aversion, with opinion of Hurt from the object" (Lev. 6.14–16). Some definitions show that Hobbes's psychology is not as narrowly selfish as its reputation. "Desire of good to another" is "BENEVOLENCE, GOOD WILL, CHARITY" (Lev. 6.22). "Desire, to know why, and how" is "CURIOSITY; such as is in no living creature but Man" (Lev. 6.35). Others have a self-regarding cast. Pity is grief for another's calamity, and it "ariseth from the imagination that the like calamity may befall himselfe" (Lev. 6.46). Whether Hobbes was a psychological egoist, holding that every voluntary act aims at the agent's own good, is a standing dispute. Bernard Gert argued that he was not, and that the passages that seem to say so describe what usually moves people rather than what must; others read a stronger egoism in the claim that "of the voluntary acts of every man, the object is some Good To Himselfe" (Lev. 14.9; the dispute is surveyed in [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md)). The study guide's first misreading (common misreading 1) gives the point that matters here: the state-of-nature argument does not need universal selfishness.

**Deliberation.** When appetites and aversions, hopes and fears about the same thing "arise alternately", and good and bad consequences of acting come successively to mind, "the whole sum of Desires, Aversions, Hopes and Feares, continued till the thing be either done, or thought impossible, is that we call DELIBERATION" (Lev. 6.49). There is no deliberation about the past or about what we know is impossible. Hobbes offers an etymology: it is called deliberation "because it is a putting an end to the Liberty we had of doing, or omitting, according to our own Appetite, or Aversion" (Lev. 6.50). And "Beasts also Deliberate" (Lev. 6.51).

**Will.** "In Deliberation, the last Appetite, or Aversion, immediately adhaering to the action, or to the omission thereof, is that wee call the WILL; the Act, (not the faculty,) of Willing" (Lev. 6.53). The scholastic definition of will as rational appetite "is not good. For if it were, then could there be no Voluntary Act against Reason" (Lev. 6.53). Hobbes's own formula is short: "Will, therefore, Is The Last Appetite In Deliberating" (Lev. 6.53). An earlier inclination that was overruled made no action voluntary. And acts done from fear count as voluntary: "not onely actions that have their beginning from Covetousness, Ambition, Lust, or other Appetites to the thing propounded; but also those that have their beginning from Aversion, or Feare of those consequences that follow the omission, are Voluntary Actions" (Lev. 6.54).

These definitions carry much of the later argument. Because the will is an appetite, it is caused like any other motion, and so freedom cannot be freedom of the will from causes; that is the basis of Hobbes's compatibilism (Argument 16, and Liberty below). Because will is the last appetite, an act done from fear is still willed, which is why covenants made from fear can bind (Argument 13). And because beasts deliberate and will too, what sets human beings apart is not a faculty of free will but speech, reckoning and curiosity.

## Power and felicity

Two definitions in chapters 10 and 11 turn this psychology toward politics (Argument 4 in [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md)).

**Power.** "The POWER of a Man, (to take it Universally,) is his present means, to obtain some future apparent Good. And is either Originall, or Instrumentall" (Lev. 10.1). Natural power is eminence of body or mind, such as strength, prudence or eloquence. Instrumental power is power acquired by these and serving to get more: riches, reputation, friends and "the Secret working of God, which men call Good Luck" (Lev. 10.2). The definition is relative and forward-looking. Power is means to future goods, and since other people's power can block mine, power is, in effect, the excess of one person's means over another's. Hence the social character of most of the list. "Reputation of power, is Power; because it draweth with it the adhaerance of those that need protection" (Lev. 10.5). And worth is a market value: "The Value, or WORTH of a man, is as of all other things, his Price; that is to say, so much as would be given for the use of his Power: and therefore is not absolute; but a thing dependant on the need and judgement of another" (Lev. 10.16). Honor is the outward sign of such valuation, and the rest of chapter 10 is a catalogue of how giving and withholding honor works.

**Felicity.** Chapter 6 defines felicity as success, not rest. "Continual Successe in obtaining those things which a man from time to time desireth, that is to say, continual prospering, is that men call FELICITY; I mean the Felicity of this life. For there is no such thing as perpetual Tranquillity of mind, while we live here; because Life itself is but Motion, and can never be without Desire, nor without Feare, no more than without Sense" (Lev. 6.58). Chapter 11 draws the anti-Aristotelian conclusion: "the Felicity of this life, consisteth not in the repose of a mind satisfied. For there is no such Finis Ultimus, (utmost ayme,) nor Summum Bonum, (greatest good,) as is spoken of in the Books of the old Morall Philosophers" (Lev. 11.1). "Felicity is a continuall progresse of the desire, from one object to another; the attaining of the former, being still but the way to the later" (Lev. 11.1). There is no highest good at which a well-ordered life comes to rest, and so no shared final end on which a political community could be built. Compare the [Aquinas dossier](../thomas-aquinas/03-system-and-concepts.md#will-action-and-happiness), where every will is ordered to one last end; Hobbes is denying exactly that.

**The restless desire of power.** Put the two definitions together. If felicity is continued success in getting what one will want, and power is present means to future goods, then everyone has reason to want power, and more of it. "So that in the first place, I put for a generall inclination of all mankind, a perpetuall and restlesse desire of Power after power, that ceaseth onely in Death" (Lev. 11.2). The next sentence is the one most often skipped. The cause is "not alwayes that a man hopes for a more intensive delight, than he has already attained to; or that he cannot be content with a moderate power: but because he cannot assure the power and means to live well, which he hath present, without the acquisition of more" (Lev. 11.2). The claim is not that everyone is greedy. Security is relative, so even a moderate person must keep gaining ground in order not to lose it. The structural argument of chapter 13 is already here.

Chapter 11 also lists the passions that dispose people toward a common power. "Desire of Ease, and sensuall Delight, disposeth men to obey a common Power" (Lev. 11.4), and so do fear of death and wounds and the desire of knowledge and the arts of peace (Lev. 11.4–6, paraphrased). Chapter 13 repeats the list at its end: "The Passions that encline men to Peace, are Feare of Death; Desire of such things as are necessary to commodious living; and a Hope by their Industry to obtain them" (Lev. 13.14). The passions that cause the war also supply the motive to end it.

## Equality and the condition of war

Chapter 13 is where the learning path's excerpt begins, and its concepts are the ones most often misread. It opens with **natural equality** (Argument 5): "Nature hath made men so equall, in the faculties of body, and mind" that no one can claim a benefit another may not also pretend to (Lev. 13.1). The equality that matters is equality of vulnerability. The weakest can kill the strongest, by secret plotting or by joining with others. From equal ability comes "equality of hope in the attaining of our Ends" (Lev. 13.3), so two people who want the same thing become enemies.

Hobbes then names "three principall causes of quarrel. First, Competition; Secondly, Diffidence; Thirdly, Glory" (Lev. 13.6). "The first, maketh men invade for Gain; the second, for Safety; and the third, for Reputation" (Lev. 13.7). *Diffidence* means distrust, not shyness, and it carries the argument (Argument 6). Suppose most people are moderate and want only security. As long as some want more, and no one can tell who they are, even a moderate person's best defense is to strike first. That is "Anticipation" (Lev. 13.4). What exactly drives the war is disputed. The SEP lists three answers: people are shortsighted about their long-term interest; they are fully rational but trapped in a prisoner's dilemma; or the passions of some (pride, spite, envy) overrule calmer judgment. It calls the question "a matter of continuing debate among Hobbes scholars" (SEP, "Hobbes's Moral and Political Philosophy," §5). Game-theoretic readings include David Gauthier, *The Logic of Leviathan* (1969), Gregory Kavka, *Hobbesian Moral and Political Theory* (1986), and Jean Hampton, *Hobbes and the Social Contract Tradition* (1986); [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md) sets out what each claims.

**War** is a technical term. Without "a common Power to keep them all in awe, they are in that condition which is called Warre; and such a warre, as is of every man, against every man" (Lev. 13.8). War is not fighting but a disposition to fight that is known to all: "the nature of War, consisteth not in actuall fighting; but in the known disposition thereto", just as "the nature of Foule weather, lyeth not in a showre or two of rain" (Lev. 13.8). Hence the famous list of what such a condition lacks, ending "continuall feare, and danger of violent death; And the life of man, solitary, poore, nasty, brutish, and short" (Lev. 13.9). Hobbes admits there was perhaps never a time of universal war, but points to "the savage people in many places of America", to civil war, and to the relations of sovereigns, who are always in "the state and posture of Gladiators" (Lev. 13.11–12, paraphrased except where quoted). The state of nature is therefore not chiefly a historical claim. It is what we find by resolution when we think the common power away.

The chapter's last point prepares the moral theory. In such a war "nothing can be Unjust. The notions of Right and Wrong, Justice and Injustice have there no place. Where there is no common Power, there is no Law: where no Law, no Injustice" (Lev. 13.13). Justice and injustice are "Qualities, that relate to men in Society, not in Solitude" (Lev. 13.13). Whether this means that the laws of nature do not bind at all in the state of nature, or only that there is no injustice until covenants can safely be kept, is part of the dispute about obligation below.

## Right of nature, liberty and the laws of nature

Chapter 14 begins with three definitions that the rest of the book needs (Argument 7).

**The right of nature** is "the Liberty each man hath, to use his own power, as he will himselfe, for the preservation of his own Nature; that is to say, of his own Life; and consequently, of doing any thing, which in his own Judgement, and Reason, hee shall conceive to be the aptest means thereunto" (Lev. 14.1). A right, here, is a liberty: no one is under an obligation not to do the thing. It is not a claim that others must respect.

**Liberty** in this first sense is "the absence of externall Impediments" (Lev. 14.2). Chapter 21 repeats the definition and extends it (below).

**A law of nature** is "a Precept, or generall Rule, found out by Reason, by which a man is forbidden to do, that, which is destructive of his life, or taketh away the means of preserving the same" (Lev. 14.3). Right and law must be kept apart, "because RIGHT, consisteth in liberty to do, or to forbeare; Whereas LAW, determineth, and bindeth to one of them" (Lev. 14.3). They "differ as much, as Obligation, and Liberty" (Lev. 14.3).

Put these together with the condition of war and the result is drastic: "every man has a Right to every thing; even to one anothers body" (Lev. 14.4), and so no one is secure. Reason therefore dictates a rule with two branches, of which the first is the first and fundamental law of nature, "To seek Peace, and follow it", and the second is the sum of the right of nature, "By all means we can, to defend our selves" (Lev. 14.5). The second law follows: that a man be willing, when others are so too, "to lay down this right to all things; and be contented with so much liberty against other men, as he would allow other men against himselfe" (Lev. 14.6). The clause "when others are so too" matters. No one is bound to lay down a right alone, "For that were to expose himselfe to Prey" (Lev. 14.6).

Chapter 15 adds about seventeen further laws: gratitude, mutual accommodation, pardon, against cruelty in revenge, against contumely and pride, equity, the use of arbitrators and others, all summed up in the negative Golden Rule, "Do not that to another, which thou wouldest not have done to thy selfe" (Lev. 15). Hobbes calls them, in the same breath, laws and not laws. "These dictates of Reason, men use to call by the name of Lawes; but improperly: for they are but Conclusions, or Theoremes concerning what conduceth to the conservation and defence of themselves; whereas Law, properly is the word of him, that by right hath command over others" (Lev. 15). Yet if the same theorems are considered "as delivered in the word of God, that by right commandeth all things; then are they properly called Lawes" (Lev. 15). That double description is the textual root of the obligation debate (Argument 10, and tension 2 below).

## Laying down right, contract and covenant

To **lay down a right** is to give up the liberty of hindering another from the benefit of his own right to the same thing (Lev. 14.7, paraphrased). It can be done by simple renouncing or by transferring to a particular person, and once it is done the person is "said to be OBLIGED, or BOUND, not to hinder those, to whom such Right is granted" the one to whom it was granted (Lev. 14.8). Obligation is thus self-imposed. It arises only from one's own act of laying down a right (Argument 8).

Every such act is voluntary, "and of the voluntary acts of every man, the object is some Good To Himselfe. And therefore there be some Rights, which no man can be understood by any words, or other signes, to have abandoned, or transferred" (Lev. 14.9). The first is "the right of resisting them, that assault him by force, to take away his life" (Lev. 14.9). Hence "A Covenant not to defend my selfe from force, by force, is alwayes voyd" (Lev. 14.30). These **inalienable rights** are the seed of the liberties of subjects in chapter 21.

"The mutuall transferring of Right, is that which men call CONTRACT" (Lev. 14.10). When one party performs now and the other is trusted to perform later, the contract on the trusted party's side "is called PACT, or COVENANT" (Lev. 14.12). A one-sided transfer is a free gift. Two rules about covenants carry the politics. First, in the condition of mere nature a covenant of mutual trust is void "upon any reasonable suspition", but where there is a common power with force sufficient to compel performance, "it is not Voyd" (Lev. 14.19). Second, "Covenants entred into by fear, in the condition of meer Nature, are obligatory" (Lev. 14.28): a ransom promised to an enemy for one's life binds. That second rule is what lets Hobbes treat conquest as a source of sovereignty (Argument 13; Lev. 20).

## Justice, the Fool and the laws as theorems

The third law of nature is "That Men Performe Their Covenants Made: without which, Covenants are in vain, and but Empty words" (Lev. 15.1). In it "consisteth the Fountain and Originall of JUSTICE", and "the definition of INJUSTICE, is no other than The Not Performance Of Covenant. And whatsoever is not Unjust, is Just" (Lev. 15.2). Justice is therefore defined by covenant, and covenants of mutual trust are void without a power to enforce them. So injustice "actually there can be none" until the cause of the fear is taken away, which requires a commonwealth (Lev. 15.3). That is the sense in which justice begins with the state: not that the sovereign decides what is just by fiat, but that there are no valid covenants to break until performance is secured. The same thought appears in chapter 17: "Covenants, without the Sword, are but Words, and of no strength to secure a man at all" (Lev. 17.2).

The **Fool** (Argument 9) "hath sayd in his heart, there is no such thing as Justice", arguing that keeping or breaking covenants "was not against Reason, when it conduced to ones benefit" (Lev. 15.4). The question is about the case where the other party has already performed, or where a power compels both. Hobbes's reply is that breaking faith is a gamble on others' errors. Anyone who breaks a covenant and declares it reasonable cannot be received into a society made for defense, except by others' mistake, and so cannot reasonably count on survival (Lev. 15.5, paraphrased). The study guide gives the reply in more detail (its argument 8 and self-test 8); the dispute over whether the reply works, between those who read it as a rule-based argument from reputation (Gauthier, Kavka) and those who think it fails against the clever, secret breaker, is in [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md).

The laws of nature, as the previous section showed, are both "Conclusions, or Theoremes" of prudence and, considered as God's word, "properly called Lawes" (Lev. 15). They also bind differently in different conditions: they "oblige In Foro Interno; that is to say, they bind to a desire they should take place: but In Foro Externo; that is, to the putting them in act, not alwayes" (Lev. 15). A person who kept them alone, where no one else did, "should but make himselfe a prey to others" (Lev. 15). In conscience the laws always bind; in action, only when there is security.

## Persons, authors and the representative

Chapter 16 supplies the concept that turns a covenant into a state (Argument 11). A person is he "whose words or actions are considered, either as his own, or as representing the words or actions of an other man" (Lev. 16.1). A natural person speaks for himself; a feigned or artificial person represents another (Lev. 16.2). Of artificial persons, some have their words and actions owned by those they represent: "then the Person is the Actor; and he that owneth his words and actions, is the AUTHOR: In which case the Actor acteth by Authority" (Lev. 16.4). **Authority** is "a Right of doing any act", and to act by authority is to act "by Commission, or Licence from him whose right it is" (Lev. 16.4).

The key move is that a crowd can become one person by being represented. "A Multitude of men, are made One Person, when they are by one man, or one Person, Represented; so that it be done with the consent of every one of that Multitude in particular. For it is the Unity of the Representer, not the Unity of the Represented, that maketh the Person One" (Lev. 16.13). There is no people with a will of its own apart from its representative. The SEP treats authorization as the act by which subjects institute a sovereign, and notes that it makes them responsible for what the sovereign does in their name (SEP, "Hobbes's Moral and Political Philosophy," §§7, 9). How new the doctrine is to *Leviathan*, compared with *Elements of Law* and *De Cive*, is taken up in [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md).

## The commonwealth and the sovereign

Chapter 17 puts the pieces together. The only way to erect a common power is for everyone to confer all their power on one man or one assembly, and to "owne, and acknowledge himselfe to be Author of whatsoever he that so beareth their Person, shall Act" (Lev. 17.13). It is as if every man should say to every man: "I Authorise and give up my Right of Governing my selfe, to this Man, or to this Assembly of men, on this condition, that thou give up thy Right to him, and Authorise all his Actions in like manner" (Lev. 17.13). "This done, the Multitude so united in one Person, is called a COMMON-WEALTH" (Lev. 17.13). "This is the Generation of that great LEVIATHAN, or rather (to speake more reverently) of that Mortall God, to which wee owe under the Immortall God, our peace and defence" (Lev. 17.13). The **commonwealth** is defined as "One Person, of whose Acts a great Multitude, by mutuall Covenants one with another, have made themselves every one the Author" (Lev. 17.14), and the one who bears that person is "called SOVERAIGNE, and said to have Soveraigne Power; and every one besides, his SUBJECT" (Lev. 17.15).

Three features of the construction matter for everything that follows. The covenant is among the subjects only, so the sovereign makes no promise and cannot breach one (study guide, chapter 17 notes; Lev. 18). Each subject is the author of the sovereign's acts, so "whatsoever he doth, it can be no injury to any of his Subjects" (Lev. 18). The sovereign may commit "Iniquity; but not Injustice, or Injury in the proper signification" (Lev. 18): a sovereign can act against the laws of nature, and answer to God for it, but cannot wrong a subject in the covenant-based sense of injustice. And a commonwealth may also arise "by Acquisition", when people submit to a conqueror out of fear; chapter 20 holds that the rights of sovereignty are the same in both cases (Lev. 20, paraphrased; the study guide's self-test 11).

Chapter 18 lists those rights (Argument 12): the sovereign cannot be changed by the subjects, forfeited, or lawfully protested against by a dissenting minority; he judges what doctrines may be taught, makes the rules of property, judges controversies, makes war and peace, chooses ministers, rewards and punishes, and confers honors (Lev. 18, paraphrased). These rights are "incommunicable, and inseparable" (Lev. 18). Chapter 29 lists, among the diseases of a commonwealth, the opinion that sovereign power may be divided, and the setting up of "a Ghostly Authority against the Civill" (Lev. 29). Hobbes's sovereign can be one man, an assembly of some, or an assembly of all; chapter 19 prefers monarchy on grounds of convenience, not of right (Lev. 19, paraphrased).

## Liberty: natural, civil and the liberty of subjects

Chapter 21 gives liberty its full definition. "Liberty, or FREEDOME, signifieth (properly) the absence of Opposition; (by Opposition, I mean externall Impediments of motion;) and may be applyed no lesse to Irrational, and Inanimate creatures, than to Rationall" (Lev. 21.1). A **free man** is "he, that in those things, which by his strength and wit he is able to do, is not hindred to doe what he has a will to" (Lev. 21.2). Freedom belongs to bodies that move, never to the will: when "Free, and Liberty, are applyed to any thing but Bodies, they are abused" (Lev. 21.2). Three consequences follow (Arguments 14 and 16).

**Fear and liberty.** "Feare and Liberty are consistent; as when a man throweth his goods into the Sea for Feare the ship should sink, he doth it neverthelesse very willingly" (Lev. 21.3). Acting from fear of the law, or of a conqueror, is acting freely, which is why consent extracted by fear is still consent.

**Liberty and necessity.** "Liberty and Necessity are Consistent: As in the water, that hath not only Liberty, but a Necessity of descending by the Channel" (Lev. 21.4). Voluntary actions proceed from the will and so from liberty, and yet every act of will proceeds from causes "in a continuall chaine (whose first link in the hand of God the first of all causes)" (Lev. 21.4). This is Hobbes's **compatibilism**, argued at length against John Bramhall in *Of Liberty and Necessity* (first printed in 1654, according to Molesworth's note) and the longer *Questions concerning Liberty, Necessity, and Chance*. There the definition is refined: "Liberty is the absence of all the impediments to action that are not contained in the nature and intrinsical quality of the agent" (LN, Molesworth IV 273). A man tied up lacks liberty; a sick or lame man lacks power, because the impediment is in himself (LN, Molesworth IV 274, paraphrased). And since every new will has a cause outside it, "voluntary actions have all of them necessary causes, and therefore are necessitated" (LN, Molesworth IV 274). Bramhall held that this destroys praise, blame and punishment; Hobbes answered that punishment is justified as a cause that shapes future wills, not by an uncaused choice (LN, paraphrased; the exchange is Argument 16).

**Civil liberty.** People have made "Artificiall Chains, called Civill Lawes, which they themselves, by mutuall covenants, have fastned at one end, to the lips of that Man, or Assembly, to whom they have given the Soveraigne Power; and at the other end to their own Ears" (Lev. 21.5). The liberty of subjects, in the ordinary sense, is what those chains leave free: "As for other Lyberties, they depend on the silence of the Law" (Lev. 21.18). In this sense the liberty of a subject is consistent with unlimited sovereign power, and the "Libertie" that ancient writers praised was the liberty of commonwealths, not of individuals (Lev. 21.8, paraphrased).

**The true liberties of subjects** are the rights that no covenant could transfer (Lev. 14.9, above). If the sovereign commands a man, "though justly condemned", to kill, wound or maim himself, or not to resist those who assault him, or to go without food, air or medicine, "yet hath that man the Liberty to disobey" (Lev. 21.12). He need not confess a crime without assurance of pardon, and he may refuse a dangerous command where refusal does not defeat the end of sovereignty, as a "naturall timorousnesse" excuses a soldier who runs (Lev. 21.13–16, paraphrased). No one has liberty to resist the sovereign's sword "in defence of another man, guilty, or innocent" (Lev. 21.17); yet those who have already rebelled and expect death may join to defend themselves (Lev. 21.17). Finally, "The Obligation of Subjects to the Soveraign is understood to last as long, and no longer, than the power lasteth, by which he is able to protect them" (Lev. 21.21). It states in one sentence the "mutuall Relation between Protection and Obedience" that the book's last paragraph names as its purpose (Lev. Review and Conclusion), and it bears directly on obedience to a new power after a defeat or a revolution ([06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md)).

## Law and counsel

Hobbes's concept of law is a command concept, and he sharpens it by contrast with counsel in chapter 25. Command is where a man gives an order "without expecting other reason than the Will of him that sayes it" (Lev. 25.2). Counsel is where a man says "Doe" or "Doe not this", and "deduceth his own reasons from the benefit that arriveth by it to him to whom he saith it" (Lev. 25.3). A command aims at the commander's good, counsel at the hearer's. The laws of nature, as theorems, are counsel; they become law only as commands of God or of the sovereign.

**Civil law** is "to every Subject, those Rules, which the Common-wealth hath Commanded him, by Word, Writing, or other sufficient Sign of the Will, to make use of, for the Distinction of Right, and Wrong" (Lev. 26.3). The sovereign is the only legislator and is not subject to the civil laws, since he can repeal them (Lev. 26.5–6, paraphrased). Custom has the force of law only by the sovereign's tacit consent (Lev. 26.7). And "The Law of Nature, and the Civill Law, contain each other, and are of equall extent" (Lev. 26.8): in a commonwealth the laws of nature become civil law, because the sovereign commands obedience to them, and obedience to civil law is itself a law of nature. Interpretation of law belongs to the sovereign or to judges he appoints, not to private reason or to the authority of lawyers, against the common-law view that Hobbes attacked again in *A Dialogue between a Philosopher and a Student, of the Common Laws of England* (Lev. 26, paraphrased; title as in Alan Cromartie's Clarendon edition, reviewed by Mark Murphy, NDPR 2005.12.01). Whether this makes Hobbes a **legal positivist** in the later sense, the forerunner of Bentham and Austin, or a natural-law thinker of an unusual kind, is a live dispute (SEP, "Hobbes's Moral and Political Philosophy," §6, for the laws of nature; the positions are in [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md)).

## Religion: its natural seed, the Christian commonwealth and the Kingdom of Darkness

Religion enters the system twice. In Part I it is a natural phenomenon explained by the psychology. Chapter 6 defines it among the passions: "Feare of power invisible, feigned by the mind, or imagined from tales publiquely allowed, RELIGION; not allowed, superstition. And when the power imagined is truly such as we imagine, TRUE RELIGION" (Lev. 6.36). Chapter 12 traces it to curiosity about causes and anxiety about the future. "Opinion of Ghosts, Ignorance of second causes, Devotion towards what men fear, and Taking of things Casuall for Prognostiques" are together "the Naturall seed of Religion" (Lev. 12). These seeds "have received culture from two sorts of men": founders of commonwealths and lawgivers, whose religion "is a part of humane Politiques", and those who acted by God's command, whose religion is "Divine Politiques" (Lev. 12). Both cultivated religion to make people more apt to obedience and peace. Religion is thus, among other things, a political force, and a dangerous one when it is in other hands than the sovereign's.

In Parts III and IV, *Of a Christian Common-wealth* and *Of the Kingdome of Darknesse*, Hobbes argues from Scripture (Argument 15). He does not set reason aside. "we are not to renounce our Senses, and Experience; nor (that which is the undoubted Word of God) our naturall Reason" (Lev. 32.2). Where Scripture says things above reason, the "Captivity of our Understanding" it requires is "not meant a Submission of the Intellectual faculty, to the Opinion of any other man; but of the Will to Obedience, where obedience is due" (Lev. 32.4). Since private claims to revelation cannot be verified, and the books of Scripture are made law only by the sovereign's authority (Lev. 33, paraphrased), the conclusion of chapter 42 follows: "in every Christian Common-wealth, the Civill Soveraign is the Supreme Pastor, to whose charge the whole flock of his Subjects is committed" (Lev. 42). The clergy teach by his authority; the church has no coercive power of its own. Hobbes's reading of Scripture also turns against traditional doctrine: the soul is not naturally immortal, hell is not eternal torment, and the Kingdom of God is a future kingdom on earth (Lev. 38, 44, paraphrased).

Part IV names the enemy. The **Kingdom of Darkness** is a confederacy of deceivers who, by misinterpreting Scripture, mixing in pagan demonology and "Vain Philosophy" (scholastic Aristotelianism, with its separated essences and incorporeal substances), win dominion over people in this world (Lev. 44–46, paraphrased). The Papacy is "no other, than the Ghost of the deceased Romane Empire, sitting crowned upon the grave thereof" (Lev. 47). The book's last paragraph calls the whole a "Discourse of Civill and Ecclesiasticall Government, occasioned by the disorders of the present time", written "to set before mens eyes the mutuall Relation between Protection and Obedience" (Lev. Review and Conclusion). The scholarly debate over his sincerity runs from readers who see a hidden atheist (Edwin Curley, Douglas Jesseph) to readers who see a sincere if unusual Christian (A. P. Martinich, *The Two Gods of Leviathan*, 1992), and the SEP notes that critics have called him an atheist since his lifetime, while "atheist" did not always mean then what it means now (SEP, "Thomas Hobbes," §5).

## How the pieces interlock

Everything is body in motion, so sense is motion in the organs and thought is decaying sense. Speech fixes thoughts in names, and reasoning is reckoning with names whose meanings must first be settled by definition; where definitions conflict, an arbitrator's reason must stand for right reason. Motion inside the body is endeavour, felt as appetite and aversion, and "good" names only what someone desires. With no common measure of good, no final end and a restless desire for power as the means to future goods, equal and distrustful people fall into a condition of war. Reason then finds theorems of peace, the laws of nature, which can be followed only if rights are laid down by covenant, and covenants hold only under a power that can enforce them. Authorization makes one representative the person of a multitude; that sovereign must be absolute and undivided, sets the civil law, and governs religion, since doctrines move people to obey or resist. Liberty, as the absence of external impediments, is compatible with fear, with necessity and with an unlimited sovereign. A good self-test is to trace any political claim in the book back to the definitions of chapters 4, 6 and 14.

## Key terms

In the format of the repo [glossary](../../glossary.md), restricted to Hobbes's usage, with everyday senses flagged.

- **Resolution and composition:** taking an effect apart in thought into its causes, then rebuilding it from them (*De Corpore* 6). Applied to politics, the state of nature is the resolution and the covenant the composition.
- **Body:** whatever exists independently of thought and fills some part of space (*De Corpore* 8.1). For Hobbes everything real is body.
- **Incorporeal substance:** a contradiction in terms, like "Round Quadrangle" (Lev. 4.22, 34.2). Not a rejection of all religion, but of the scholastic soul and separated essences.
- **Endeavour (*conatus*):** the small, invisible beginnings of motion inside the body before visible action (Lev. 6.1). The bridge between mechanics and psychology.
- **Sense:** the appearance produced by external motion pressing on the organs and the inward counter-pressure (Lev. 1.4). Colors and sounds are in us, not in objects.
- **Imagination:** decaying sense (Lev. 2.2). Memory is the same thing considered as old and fading.
- **Train of thoughts (mental discourse):** the succession of thoughts, either unguided or regulated by a desire (Lev. 3).
- **Prudence:** foresight from experience, never more than presumption (Lev. 3.7). Not the classical virtue of practical wisdom.
- **Name:** a mark or sign for a thought. Only names are universal (Lev. 4.6); generality is a feature of words, not of things or concepts.
- **Definition:** the settling of a word's signification, where every science must start (Lev. 4.12).
- **Reason (reckoning):** adding and subtracting the consequences of general names (Lev. 5.2). Not a faculty that grasps natural ends.
- **Science:** conditional knowledge of consequences, as against sense and memory, which are knowledge of fact (Lev. 5.17).
- **Appetite and aversion:** endeavour toward or away from an object (Lev. 6.2). Love and hate are the same with the object present.
- **Good and evil:** names for the objects of a person's appetite and aversion, with no common rule outside a person, an arbitrator or a commonwealth (Lev. 6.7).
- **Deliberation:** the alternating succession of appetites, aversions, hopes and fears about an act (Lev. 6.49). Beasts deliberate too.
- **Will:** "The Last Appetite In Deliberating" (Lev. 6.53); an act, not a faculty. Not a rational power able to act against all inclination.
- **Felicity:** continued success in getting what one desires from time to time (Lev. 6.58). Not rest in a highest good; there is no *summum bonum* (Lev. 11.1).
- **Power:** present means to obtain some future apparent good (Lev. 10.1). Effectively comparative: what counts is one's power relative to others'.
- **Diffidence:** distrust of others, which leads to anticipation (Lev. 13.3–4). Not shyness.
- **War:** a known disposition to fight over a tract of time, not actual fighting (Lev. 13.8).
- **State of nature:** the condition of people without a common power to keep them in awe (Lev. 13). Primarily a thought experiment, not a claim about prehistory.
- **Right of nature (*jus naturale*):** the liberty to use one's own power, by one's own judgment, to preserve one's life (Lev. 14.1). A liberty, not a claim-right.
- **Liberty:** the absence of external impediments to motion (Lev. 14.2, 21.1). Applies to water as well as people; never to the will itself.
- **Law of nature (*lex naturalis*):** a general rule found out by reason forbidding what is destructive of one's life (Lev. 14.3). Called "Theoremes", and properly law only as God's command (Lev. 15).
- **Obligation:** the state of having laid down a right by one's own act, so that hindering its beneficiary is injury (Lev. 14.8).
- **Contract and covenant:** the mutual transferring of right; a covenant is a contract in which at least one party is trusted to perform later (Lev. 14.10–12).
- **Justice and injustice:** keeping and breaking valid covenants (Lev. 15.2). Not a natural virtue that exists before any agreement.
- **In foro interno / externo:** the laws of nature always bind to a desire that they take place, but bind to action only where it is safe (Lev. 15).
- **Person, author, actor:** a person is one whose words or actions are taken as his own or another's; the actor acts, the author owns the acts (Lev. 16.1, 16.4).
- **Authorization:** making another one's representative, so that his acts count as one's own (Lev. 16.4, 17.13). The basis of the claim that a sovereign cannot injure a subject.
- **Commonwealth (Leviathan, "Mortall God"):** one person made by mutual covenants, of whose acts every member is author (Lev. 17.13–14). By institution or by acquisition.
- **Sovereign:** the one man or assembly that bears the person of the commonwealth (Lev. 17.15). Absolute, undivided, not party to the covenant.
- **Civil law and command:** rules the commonwealth has commanded for distinguishing right and wrong (Lev. 26.3); a command looks to the commander's will, counsel to the hearer's benefit (Lev. 25.2–3).
- **Compatibilism:** Hobbes's thesis that liberty and necessity are consistent (Lev. 21.4; LN). The term is modern.
- **Kingdom of Darkness:** the confederacy of deceivers who gain power by false doctrine, demonology and vain philosophy (Lev. 44–47).

## Known internal tensions

**(1) The right to resist and absolute sovereignty.** Hobbes argues that sovereignty must be absolute and that subjects may not resist it as a body (Lev. 18, 21.17). Yet every subject keeps the right to resist those who would kill or wound him, including the sovereign's officers, and obligation lasts only as long as the power to protect (Lev. 14.30, 21.12, 21.21). Critics since Bramhall, whose *The Catching of Leviathan* appeared in 1658 as an appendix to his *Castigations of Hobbes's Animadversions* (DNB 1891, "Hobbes, Thomas"), have asked whether these liberties dissolve the absolute state they were meant to found. Defenders reply that the retained rights are personal, cannot be exercised for others, and do not license a collective rebellion except by those who already expect death; the SEP discusses the limits in "Hobbes's Moral and Political Philosophy," §9.

**(2) Are the laws of nature laws?** If they are "Theoremes" of prudence, obligation reduces to self-interest. If they are "properly called Lawes" as God's commands (Lev. 15), their binding force is theological. A. E. Taylor and Howard Warrender (*The Political Philosophy of Hobbes*, 1957) read Hobbes's ethics as a deontology grounded in God's command; Thomas Nagel and others read it as prudence; A. P. Martinich defends a religious reading (the SEP, "Thomas Hobbes," §5, cites Warrender 1957 and Martinich 1992 on one side and Nagel 1959 and Darwall 1994 on the other, on whether God plays a fundamental role in the ethics).

**(3) Materialism and the God of *Leviathan*.** God is incomprehensible and his attributes are only honorific (Lev. 3.12), yet the later works make God a "corporeal spirit" (*Answer to Bishop Bramhall*, Molesworth IV; SEP §5). Neither sits easily with a philosophy in which a word for something that is not body "signifie nothing" (Lev. 4.22), and readers divide between hidden atheism (Curley, Jesseph) and heterodox but sincere belief (SEP, "Thomas Hobbes," §5).

**(4) Egoism and the motives for peace.** The psychology seems to make every voluntary act aim at the agent's own good (Lev. 14.9), yet the passions include benevolence (Lev. 6.22), and the reply to the Fool needs agents who will keep covenants when breach would pay. If people are as Hobbes describes, can they keep the covenant that founds the state, and if they can, do they need so strong a sovereign?

**(5) The method and the system.** If civil philosophy can be reached analytically from experience without the physics (*De Corpore* 6.7), the deduction from body through man to commonwealth is not the only route, and the unity of the system becomes a question rather than a premise (the Strauss–Watkins dispute in the method section above).
