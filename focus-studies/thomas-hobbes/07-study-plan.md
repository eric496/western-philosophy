## What this plan is, and where it plugs in

The [learning path](../../learning-path.md) meets Hobbes in **Stage 3 (Early Modern to Kant)** at row 6, as a ◐ figure: *Leviathan* chs. 13–17 (state of nature, laws of nature, the sovereign) in excerpt, map level for the rest, with [Suárez and Grotius](../../eras/03-early-modern/suarez-and-grotius.md) at map level as the natural-law background he writes against. The path sets him before [Locke](../../eras/03-early-modern/john-locke.md) (row 7) so that the contrast lands, with the [study guide to *Leviathan* chs. 13–17+](../../study/hobbes-leviathan.md) as companion. This dossier keeps that core, order and guide, and goes deeper on purpose: the path's chapters are Weeks 3–5, and around them come the project (Week 1), the materialist mind (Week 2), religion in Parts III–IV, which the SEP tells newcomers not to skip (Week 6), and free will against Bramhall, with *Behemoth* (Week 7). [02-works-and-texts.md](02-works-and-texts.md), "The works in the dossier's reading order," gives the reasons.

Run it as one block in place of row 6. Week 2 assumes [Descartes](../../eras/03-early-modern/rene-descartes.md) (row 1), since it reads the Third Objections; the [Descartes dossier](../rene-descartes/README.md) gives the other side. Locke follows at row 7; Hume (row 10) picks up Weeks 6 and 7, and Rousseau (row 11) rewrites Week 3.

**Total cost, stated honestly: ≈38 hours all-in across seven weeks: 27.5 hours at the desk, ≈4.25 hours of assigned media, and ≈6.5 hours of the daily [Anki](https://apps.ankiweb.net) [FSRS](https://docs.ankiweb.net/deck-options.html) queue (about 8 minutes a day for 49 days, 392 minutes).** The table rounds each week's 56 minutes of cards to 0.9 hours and each week's media to one decimal, so its columns sum to 27.5 + 4.1 + 6.3 = 37.9; unrounded, 27.5 + 4.25 + 6.53 = 38.3. Weekly totals run from 4.7 to 6.6 hours; optional items are not counted. Running times are those of [08-bibliography.md](08-bibliography.md), §5: *Philosophize This!* eps. 26 and 27 (30 min 31 s, 29 min 36 s), *In Our Time*'s "Hobbes" (45 minutes), and three Yale PLSC 114 lectures, whose pages state no length; the course page gives 50-minute sessions, so each is priced at 50 minutes, an upper estimate. 30.5 + 29.6 + 45 + 150 = 255 minutes. The Wi-Phi lessons state no lengths and sit inside desk hours, as the path does with Wi-Phi.

**Card time is an estimate tied to deck size.** The path's daily queue is about 10 minutes, about six days a week, for the whole cumulative deck (§2). This plan counts 8 minutes a day as the share this dossier's cards add, the house rate the [Aquinas plan](../thomas-aquinas/07-study-plan.md) uses for a deck of similar size, here **42 cards**: 16 argument cards, one per argument in [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), and 26 concept cards keyed to the key terms of [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md). At a different rate, card hours are rate × 49 days ÷ 60.

**The accounting against the path, stated plainly: these hours sit on top of Stage 3's budget (16 weeks, 72 desk hours, ≈111 all-in), the load-bearing stage the path says never to compress.** The path fixes no week count for the row; at the stage's rate this dossier estimates it at about one week and 4.5 desk hours, its own estimate, not the path's. None of the media is in the path's budget (Adamson has not reached Hobbes, *Philosophize This!* starts at Locke in the path, PLSC 114 is optional there). Less the row's desk time and its share of the path's queue (about 1 hour, an estimate), the net addition is 38.3 − 4.5 − 1 ≈ 33 hours: Stage 3 at about 144 all-in instead of ≈111, before any other Stage 3 dossier.

The path (§1.4) allows a stage at most two extra weeks; seven weeks for a one-week row is about six. Choose deliberately. (a) Run the whole plan in the comfortable version (Calendar B, path §10). (b) Run Weeks 3–5 as the row (12.5 desk hours, 17.7 all-in): about two weeks' extension, everything the path and guide set, and Locke straight after the sovereign. Take Weeks 1, 2, 6 and 7 (15 desk, 20.2 all-in) after Stage 3's gate as new Calendar B time, not buffer weeks, which path §10 keeps for illness, holidays, two named re-reading passes and media spill. Under (b), read Lev. 6 at the start of Week 3 (about forty minutes), since Arguments 4 and 7 lean on it.

**Reading texts.** Follow [08-bibliography.md](08-bibliography.md), §1. *Leviathan* in Tuck (Cambridge Texts, 1996), which keeps Hobbes's spelling and prints the 1651 page numbers, or Curley (Hackett, 1994), which modernizes, numbers paragraphs and translates the Latin variants, the better choice for Weeks 6–7. The *Elements* in Gaskin; *De Cive* in Tuck & Silverthorne; the Bramhall debate in Chappell; the Third Objections in any *Meditations* with the objections and replies, such as Cottingham's, which the path names; *Behemoth* in Seaward or Tönnies. The dossier quotes Project Gutenberg #3207 and Molesworth because they are public domain and checkable, not because you should read them at the desk. Quotations keep the 1651 spelling.

**Cite by chapter and paragraph, and budget by chapter.** (Lev. 13.9) is chapter 13, paragraph 9, counted in the saved 1651 text; Curley's numbers almost always agree. In Parts III–IV, read in selection, this plan cites the chapter and describes the passage. Other works as in [02-works-and-texts.md](02-works-and-texts.md), "A note on citation and editions": EL I.14.11, DCv Preface, Obj. III, Obj. 2, LN by Molesworth page, *Behemoth* by dialogue.

**The guide is your companion, by design.** Its chapter sections, three reconstructions, fifteen checkpoints and seven misreadings are assigned by number in Weeks 3–5 and not duplicated; the checkpoints printed here are the dossier's own. Its five-week plan is folded into Weeks 3–5, its fifth week (re-reading chs. 13 and 17) into Week 6's Session C.

## Week 1 — Life, the Civil War, the project: Lev. Intro and R&C; EL Epistle Dedicatory and I.1 (~4.7 hrs: 3 desk + 0.8 media + 0.9 cards)

**Primary.** About an hour and a half, the lightest week of text. The Introduction: nature as God's art, the commonwealth as "an Artificiall Man" (Lev. Intro) whose soul is sovereignty, the method under the maxim "Nosce Teipsum, Read Thy Self" (Lev. Intro), and the plan of four parts; read it twice. Then "A Review, and Conclusion," about forty-five minutes, written for a country that had just changed masters: the point at which a man becomes a conqueror's subject, the admission that hardly any commonwealth's beginnings can be justified in conscience, and the book's aim, the relation between protection and obedience. Then, in Gaskin, the Epistle Dedicatory of May 1640, on mathematical learning, free of dispute, against dogmatical learning, where passion overrules reason, and EL I.1, the division of human nature into powers of body and mind. Read the Introduction and the Epistle side by side: one written at the war's end, one on its eve.

**Alongside.** [01-life-and-times.md](01-life-and-times.md) to "The third tour" at a steady pace, then "*The Elements of Law* and the flight to Paris, 1640," "The Prince of Wales and the exile court, 1646–1651," "*Leviathan* and the return, 1651–1652" and "Weighing the sources" closely; [02-works-and-texts.md](02-works-and-texts.md), "The forms Hobbes wrote in" and the *Elements* and *Leviathan* entries; [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md), "The order of the system" and "Method," with tension (5); [05-conversations.md](05-conversations.md), "Thucydides" through "Galileo, Harvey and geometry"; [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md), "Myths about the life." The library entry on [Thomas Hobbes](../../eras/03-early-modern/thomas-hobbes.md) as the map. In Session B, Martinich and the IEP's ["Hobbes, Thomas: Methodology"](https://iep.utm.edu/hobmeth/).

**Media.** *Review, after the reading:* Yale, PLSC 114, [Lecture 12](https://oyc.yale.edu/political-science/plsc-114/lecture-12) (priced at 50 minutes): who Hobbes was, Hobbes against Machiavelli and Aristotle, and what makes legitimate authority possible. *Optional, not counted:* *In Our Time*, ["Thucydides"](https://www.bbc.co.uk/programmes/b050bcf1) (43 minutes) or ["The Putney Debates"](https://www.bbc.co.uk/programmes/b01rw1k7) (42 minutes).

**Checkpoints (book closed).**
1. *What is the "Artificiall Man," and what are the four parts?* The commonwealth, made by art in imitation of nature, with sovereignty as its soul. Its matter and maker, both man; how and by what covenants it is made; a Christian commonwealth; the kingdom of darkness. [02](02-works-and-texts.md), the *Leviathan* entry; [03](03-system-and-concepts.md), "The order of the system."
2. *What does "Read Thy Self" mean as method, and what does tension (5) ask?* Each can find the passions of all by looking into his own, since passions are alike though their objects differ. If civil philosophy can be had from that experience, does it need the physics at all? [03](03-system-and-concepts.md), "Method" and "Known internal tensions."
3. *When does a man become a conqueror's subject, and why did royalists call the book a betrayal?* When, having liberty to submit, he consents by words or other sufficient sign; obligation follows protection. Exiles read it as advice to make peace with the Commonwealth, and Clarendon and Wallis later said it was written for Cromwell; say what the DNB judged of that. [01](01-life-and-times.md), "*Leviathan* and the return"; [04](04-arguments.md), Argument 13.
4. *What does the Epistle Dedicatory propose, and how did the* Elements *reach readers?* To put justice and policy on principles passion cannot unsettle, as mathematics is. Dated during the Short Parliament, circulated in manuscript, printed without Hobbes's direction in 1650 as two books. [02](02-works-and-texts.md), the *Elements* entry.
5. *Which life stories rest only on Hobbes and Aubrey?* The Armada birth, the Euclid story, the Galileo meeting, the claim to have fled first, the burned papers; "fear and I were born twins together" is a modern paraphrase. The 1666 committee and the 1683 burning are on record. [01](01-life-and-times.md), "Weighing the sources"; [06](06-reception-and-debates.md), "Myths about the life."

**Reconstructions.** No numbered argument. Write the Introduction's plan book closed, four parts each with its question, and put the Epistle's case for a civil science in numbered premises; it returns as the opening of Argument 2.

**Cards.** 2 concept cards: the resolutive-compositive method; the book's stated purpose (Review and Conclusion). Import after the week's reading; if you use the shipped decks in [`study/cards/`](../../study/cards/README.md), suspend any Hobbes card these duplicate. Deck at 2.

## Week 2 — Mind and language: Lev. 1–6; the Third Objections (~4.9 hrs: 4 desk + 0 media + 0.9 cards)

**Primary.** About two and a half hours. Ch. 1, sense as motion pressing inward and seeming outward (Lev. 1.4), against the schools' species (Lev. 1.5). Ch. 2, imagination as decaying sense (Lev. 2.2). Ch. 3 at a steady pace, stopping on its last paragraph (Lev. 3.12): what we imagine is finite, and God's name is used to honor him, not to conceive him. Ch. 4 slowly, the week's center: only names are universal (Lev. 4.6), definitions as the start of science on the model of geometry, and the names that signify nothing at the chapter's end, *incorporeal substance* among them. Ch. 5, reasoning as reckoning and the arbitrator (Lev. 5.1–3) closely, the rest steadily. Ch. 6, its first seven paragraphs closely (endeavour, appetite, and good as the object of appetite, Lev. 6.7), the catalogue of passions quickly, the closing paragraphs on deliberation, will and felicity twice (Lev. 6.49–58). Then the Third Objections, about forty-five minutes: all sixteen with Descartes's replies, Obj. 2, 4 and 5 twice, Meditation II open beside Obj. 2.

**Alongside.** [01](01-life-and-times.md), "The Third Objections, 1641"; [02](02-works-and-texts.md), the Third Objections and *De Corpore* entries; [03](03-system-and-concepts.md), "Body: matter and motion" through "Endeavour, appetite and aversion, the passions, deliberation and will," with tension (3) and the key terms "Body" to "Will"; [04](04-arguments.md), the opening note, then Arguments 1–3; [05](05-conversations.md), "Mersenne and Gassendi," "Descartes: the optics quarrel and the Third Objections" and "Leibniz." In Session B, the SEP's "Thomas Hobbes" on mind, language and materialism; Pettit's *Made with Words* if Argument 2 holds you.

**Media.** None assigned. *Optional, not counted:* Adamson, ep. 466, ["Well Hidden: Descartes' Life and Works"](https://hopwag.podbean.com/e/hop-466-well-hidden-descartes-life-and-works/) (20 minutes).

**Checkpoints (book closed).**
1. *Reconstruct sense as motion, and say what it owes.* Pressure on the organ runs to brain and heart, whose outward endeavour seems to be something without; color is in us (Lev. 1.4–5), imagination is the same motion decaying. Owed: why one motion seems red and another a sound. Reply: appearing is a motion, the rest is physiology. [04](04-arguments.md), Argument 1.
2. *Why does reckoning need an umpire?* Truth is a relation of names (Lev. 4.11), reasoning adds and subtracts their consequences (Lev. 5.2), and where reckoners disagree they must set up an arbitrator's reason as right reason (Lev. 5.3). Give Descartes's Frenchman and German, the reply that the arbitrariness is in the counters, not the sum, and where the umpire returns. [04](04-arguments.md), Argument 2.
3. *What does Obj. 2 grant and deny, and how does Descartes answer?* It grants that a thinking thing exists, denies that it is therefore a mind any more than walking makes me a walk, and suggests what thinks is body. Descartes: "mind" names the thing with the faculty, and the real distinction waits for Meditation VI. Say why that concedes Hobbes's point. [04](04-arguments.md), Argument 3; [05](05-conversations.md), "Descartes."
4. *Why is there no common rule of good?* Good names the object of someone's appetite, so the rule comes from the person, an arbitrator or the commonwealth (Lev. 6.7); the will is the last appetite in deliberating, an act, not a faculty. Name the arguments this feeds (4, 6, 16). [03](03-system-and-concepts.md), "Endeavour, appetite and aversion."
5. *Why is* incorporeal substance *absurd speech, and does atheism follow?* It joins contradictory names. Not atheism: God is incomprehensible and his names honor him (Lev. 3.12); the late works call him a corporeal spirit. That is tension (3), Week 6's question. [03](03-system-and-concepts.md), "Known internal tensions."

**Reconstructions.** Arguments 1 and 2 in full, Argument 3 in premise skeleton; all three get their +2-day touches in Session C.

**Cards.** +8: Arguments 1–3; endeavour with appetite and aversion, good and evil, deliberation and will, science and prudence, the principle of inertia. Deck at 10.

## Week 3 — Toward war: Lev. 10–13; the *De Cive* Preface (~5.4 hrs: 4 desk + 0.5 media + 0.9 cards)

**Primary.** About two and a half hours. Ch. 10, power as present means to some future apparent good (Lev. 10.1) and the paragraphs on its kinds, closely; the list of honoring at a steady pace. Ch. 11, its first two paragraphs twice: no greatest good, felicity as progress, and the restless desire of power after power, with the reason given (Lev. 11.1–2). Ch. 12 skimmed for its four seeds of religion (Lev. 12.11); Week 6 reads it. Ch. 13 whole, three times, as the path's reading rule asks: equality (Lev. 13.1–2), diffidence (13.3–5), the three causes (13.6–7), war as a tract of time (13.8), its incommodities (13.9), the locked chests (13.10), the examples (13.11–12), nothing unjust (13.13). Then the *De Cive* Preface to the Reader, about thirty minutes: the method, the denial that men are evil by nature, and why the politics came out first.

**The guide.** Its section on Chapter 13; reconstruction **(a)**, written book closed first; **checkpoints 1–3**; misreadings 1 and 2.

**Alongside.** [02](02-works-and-texts.md), the *De Cive* entry; [03](03-system-and-concepts.md), "Power and felicity" and "Equality and the condition of war," with tension (4); [04](04-arguments.md), Arguments 4–6; [05](05-conversations.md), "Grotius and Suárez," "Rousseau," "Game theory and contractarianism" and "Realism in international relations"; [06](06-reception-and-debates.md), "The twentieth century," "Game theorists and rational-choice readers," "Feminist and republican readings," live debate (1) and the first two misconceptions. The [Suárez and Grotius](../../eras/03-early-modern/suarez-and-grotius.md) entry, the path's companion for this row. In Session B, the SEP's ["Prisoner's Dilemma"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/prisoner-dilemma/). *Optional, not counted:* DCv 1.

**Media.** *Preview, before the reading:* [*Philosophize This!*](https://philosophizethis.libsyn.com/episode-026-thomas-hobbes-pt-1-the-social-contract), ep. 26 (30 min 31 s). *Watch-before, inside desk hours:* Wi-Phi's [*Democracy* module](https://www.wi-phi.com/modules/democracy/) (Geoff Pynn), "The Anarchist's Challenge" and "The Basis of State Authority."

**Checkpoints (the dossier's own).**
1. *Why is the desire of power restless?* Not for more delight, but because one cannot assure the means to live well one has now without acquiring more (Lev. 11.2); power is comparative and felicity never rests. Objection: it slanders human nature. Reply: the moderate are drawn in by the immoderate. Name Strauss's and Macpherson's readings. [04](04-arguments.md), Argument 4.
2. *In what sense are people equal, and what does the ninth law add?* The weakest can kill the strongest by stealth or confederacy. Even if nature made men unequal, those who think themselves equal will make peace only on equal terms, so equality must be admitted (Lev. 15.21). Say what the SEP draws from this about women. [04](04-arguments.md), Argument 5.
3. *What makes the state of nature a war?* Competition, diffidence and glory (Lev. 13.6–7), and war as a known disposition to fight (Lev. 13.8), in which "the life of man, solitary, poore, nasty, brutish, and short" (Lev. 13.9). Then the three readings: rationality (Gauthier, Kavka), passions (Strauss), judgment (the IEP, Lloyd). [04](04-arguments.md), Argument 6.
4. *Is it history?* Never generally so (Lev. 13.11); the evidence is our own locked doors, civil war, and sovereigns as gladiators (Lev. 13.10–12), with the American example poorly evidenced. Give Locke's objection and what the analytical reply concedes about scale. Guide reconstruction (a); misreading 2.
5. *What does the* De Cive *Preface add?* A commonwealth considered as if dissolved, like a watch taken apart; the denial that the argument needs men evil by nature, since we cannot tell the wicked from the good; and a country boiling with questions of dominion and obedience before the war. [02](02-works-and-texts.md), the *De Cive* entry; [06](06-reception-and-debates.md), "Misconceptions," first entry.

**Reconstructions.** Argument 4 in full; Arguments 5 and 6 as one chain, 5 in skeleton and 6 in full, with the guide's (a) as the check. Their +2-day touches in Session C; Arguments 1–3 their +1-week touches.

**Cards.** +7: Arguments 4–6; felicity, power and worth, diffidence and anticipation, war in Hobbes's technical sense. Deck at 17.

## Week 4 — Laws of nature and covenant: Lev. 14–16 (~5.7 hrs: 4 desk + 0.8 media + 0.9 cards)

**Primary.** About two and a half hours, the guide's Weeks 2–3 in one. Ch. 14: its first six paragraphs twice (right of nature, liberty, law of nature, the right to every thing, the first and second laws, Lev. 14.1–6); then the theory of contract, stopping on four passages: injustice as undoing what one has voluntarily done (Lev. 14.8), covenants of mutual trust void on reasonable suspicion (Lev. 14.19; with the rule that the voiding fear must arise after the covenant, Lev. 14.21), covenants made in fear (Lev. 14.28), and the covenant not to defend oneself, always void (Lev. 14.30). Ch. 15: the third law and justice (Lev. 15.1–3), the Fool and the reply (Lev. 15.4–5) twice, the other laws steadily, and the closing paragraphs closely, on the inward and outward courts and the laws as theorems (Lev. 15.36–41). Ch. 16: persons, authors, actors (Lev. 16.1–8), fictions (16.9–12), and how a multitude is made one person (Lev. 16.13), twice.

**The guide.** Its sections on Chapters 14–16; premises 1–3 of reconstruction **(b)**; **checkpoints 4–9**; misreading 6.

**Alongside.** [03](03-system-and-concepts.md), "Right of nature, liberty and the laws of nature," "Laying down right, contract and covenant," "Justice, the Fool and the laws as theorems" and "Persons, authors and the representative," with tension (2); [04](04-arguments.md), Arguments 7–10; [05](05-conversations.md), "Selden," "Cumberland and Pufendorf" and "Hume"; [06](06-reception-and-debates.md), live debate (2). The [David Hume](../../eras/03-early-modern/david-hume.md) entry, for the sensible knave. In Session B, the SEP's ["Contractarianism"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/contractarianism/) and the IEP's ["Social Contract Theory"](https://iep.utm.edu/soc-cont/). *Optional, not counted:* EL I.15–17.

**Media.** *Review, after Arguments 7–9:* Yale, [Lecture 13](https://oyc.yale.edu/political-science/plsc-114/lecture-13) (priced at 50 minutes), on the state of nature, pride and fear, and the laws of nature. *Optional, not counted:* *In Our Time*, ["The Social Contract"](https://www.bbc.co.uk/programmes/b008w3xm) (45 minutes).

**Checkpoints (the dossier's own).**
1. *Right of nature against law of nature, and the two branches of the first law.* Liberty to use one's power for one's preservation (Lev. 14.1) against a rule of reason forbidding self-destruction (Lev. 14.3), as liberty against obligation; in war a right to every thing (Lev. 14.4); so seek peace, and failing it, defend ourselves by all means (Lev. 14.5). Answer the objection that such a right is empty. [04](04-arguments.md), Argument 7; guide checkpoints 4–5.
2. *Why is breach unjust, and why does mutual trust fail without a sovereign?* Laying down a right obliges; breach undoes one's own voluntary act, which Hobbes likens to absurdity. Words bind only through fear of what follows, so whoever performs first in the state of nature betrays himself. State the assurance problem for the founding covenant. [04](04-arguments.md), Argument 8; guide checkpoint 6.
3. *The Fool and the reply.* "The Foole hath sayd in his heart, there is no such thing as Justice" (Lev. 15.4). Reply: judge by what can be foreseen; no one survives without confederates; a known breaker is received only by others' error, which no one can reasonably reckon on (Lev. 15.5). Objections: the secret breach (Hume's knave) and Hampton's dilemma; Kavka's and Hoekstra's readings. [04](04-arguments.md), Argument 9; guide checkpoints 7–8.
4. *Are the laws of nature laws?* Improperly, as theorems of self-preservation; properly, as God's word; binding always in the inward court, in action only where safe. Taylor and Warrender against Nagel, Watkins and Gauthier, with Darwall and Lloyd between. Tension (2). [04](04-arguments.md), Argument 10.
5. *What makes a multitude one person?* A representer, one man or assembly, authorized by each; the unity of the representer, not the represented (Lev. 16.13). Say why authorization is more than consent to one act. Guide checkpoint 9; [04](04-arguments.md), Argument 11, read ahead.

**Reconstructions.** Arguments 7 and 8 as one chain in ch. 14, in full; Argument 9 in full; Argument 10 in skeleton, completed in Week 5. The plan's heaviest first-reconstruction week, so Argument 11 waits for ch. 17. Arguments 7–8 and 9 get their +2-day touches in Session C; Arguments 4 and 5–6 their +1-week touches.

**Cards.** +9: Arguments 7–10; the right of nature (*jus naturale*), the law of nature (*lex naturalis*), contract, covenant and gift, justice and injustice, the Fool. Deck at 26.

## Week 5 — The sovereign: Lev. 17–21, 29–30, with 26.1–8 (~6.6 hrs: 4.5 desk + 1.2 media + 0.9 cards)

**Primary.** About three hours, the heaviest reading. Ch. 17 whole: why bees and ants need no sovereign and men do (Lev. 17.6–12), and the covenant and the definition of the commonwealth (Lev. 17.13–14), twice. Ch. 18: the first nine rights closely (Lev. 18.3–12), with Lev. 18.4 (no breach on the sovereign's part) and the right to judge doctrines (Lev. 18.9); the rest carefully. Ch. 19 steadily, with Lev. 19.4 (the kinds of commonwealth differ in convenience, not power). Ch. 20: acquisition and institution (Lev. 20.1–3) and conquest (Lev. 20.10–12) closely, paternal dominion steadily. Ch. 21 whole, with the attack on ancient liberty, "LIBERTAS" on the turrets of Lucca (Lev. 21.8), and the true liberties (Lev. 21.10–25), 21.11–17 and 21.21 twice. Then Lev. 26.1–8, about twenty minutes: civil law as command, ending with the law of nature and the civil law containing each other (Lev. 26.8); it completes Argument 10 and is the one chapter this plan adds to the list in [02](02-works-and-texts.md). Ch. 29 on the diseases of a commonwealth (Lev. 29.5–15), and ch. 30 on the sovereign's office, the safety of the people (Lev. 30.1).

**The guide.** Its sections on Chapter 17 and "Chapters 18–21 in summary"; premises 4–5 of reconstruction **(b)** and reconstruction **(c)**, each book closed first; **checkpoints 10–15**; misreadings 3, 4, 5 and 7.

**Alongside.** [03](03-system-and-concepts.md), "The commonwealth and the sovereign," "Liberty: natural, civil and the liberty of subjects" and "Law and counsel," with tension (1); [04](04-arguments.md), Argument 10 again, then Arguments 11–14; [05](05-conversations.md), "Clarendon," "Filmer and Harrington," "Locke," "Bentham, Austin and legal positivism" and "Skinner and the historians"; [06](06-reception-and-debates.md), "The Cambridge contextualists," live debates (4) and (5), and the misconceptions on royalist absolutism and the sovereign as party. The entries on [John Locke](../../eras/03-early-modern/john-locke.md) and [Montesquieu](../../eras/03-early-modern/montesquieu.md), whose separation of powers answers Lev. 29 at row 9. In Session B, the SEP's ["Legal Positivism"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/legal-positivism/), Skinner's *Hobbes and Republican Liberty* for Argument 14, Springborg and Sommerville. *Optional, not counted:* the *Dialogue* on the common laws.

**Media.** *Preview, before ch. 17:* [*Philosophize This!*](https://philosophizethis.libsyn.com/episode-027-thomas-hobbes-pt-2), ep. 27 (29 min 36 s). *Review, at the week's end:* *In Our Time*, ["Hobbes"](https://www.bbc.co.uk/programmes/p003k9l1) (1 December 2005, 45 minutes, with Quentin Skinner, David Wootton and Annabel Brett); the podcast file is shorter. *Optional, not counted:* ["The Levellers"](https://www.bbc.co.uk/programmes/m002wkhq) (55 minutes).

**Checkpoints (the dossier's own).**
1. *State the covenant of ch. 17 and what authorization adds.* Each gives up his right of governing himself to one man or assembly on condition that the others do the same, and authorizes all its actions (Lev. 17.13); the result is one person, the commonwealth (Lev. 17.14), a real unity, not concord. Give Filmer's objection, the responsibility puzzle of Lev. 42, and Lloyd's reply. [04](04-arguments.md), Argument 11; guide checkpoint 10.
2. *Why absolute and undivided?* The sovereign is no party (Lev. 18.4); a condition needs a judge, and without one it returns to the sword; the rights hang together; divided powers destroy each other (Lev. 29.11). Locke's polecats, foxes and lions; the reply from civil war; Kavka's limited sovereign. [04](04-arguments.md), Argument 12; guide (c), checkpoint 12.
3. *Why do covenants made in fear bind?* Fear and liberty are consistent (Lev. 21.3); acquisition differs from institution only in whom men fear (Lev. 20.2), and the right comes from the vanquished man's covenant, not the victory (Lev. 20.11). Locke's robber, Clarendon's Cromwell charge, the Engagement reading. [04](04-arguments.md), Argument 13; guide checkpoint 11.
4. *Do the true liberties undo absolutism?* Free where the law is silent; no injury from the sovereign, whose acts we author (Lev. 21.6–7); yet a right to resist assault and to refuse commands to harm oneself, starve or self-accuse (Lev. 21.11–13), no right to defend another (Lev. 21.17), and obligation only while protection lasts (Lev. 21.21). Hampton against Sreedhar; Skinner on Lucca. Tension (1). [04](04-arguments.md), Argument 14; guide checkpoints 14–15.
5. *How does Lev. 26 make the laws of nature law, and which doctrines are diseases?* In the state of nature they dispose to peace; in a commonwealth they are commanded, so natural and civil law contain each other (Lev. 26.8). Diseases: private judgment of good and evil, inspiration, the sovereign under the civil law, absolute property, divided sovereignty, ghostly authority (Lev. 29.5–14). [04](04-arguments.md), Argument 10; [03](03-system-and-concepts.md), "Law and counsel."

**Reconstructions.** Arguments 11 and 12 as one chain through chs. 16–18, in full, checked against the guide's (b) and (c); Argument 13 in skeleton; Argument 14 in full; Argument 10 completed from Lev. 26.1–8, which counts as its +2-day touch. Arguments 11–12 and 14 get their +2-day touches in Session C; Arguments 7–8, 9 and 10 their +1-week touches. Arguments 1–3 wait a week for their +3-week touch so that the week stays at six recalls.

**Cards.** +11: Arguments 11–14; person, author and actor, the commonwealth and the sovereign, sovereignty by institution and by acquisition, iniquity and injury, the definition of liberty, the liberty of subjects, counsel and command. Deck at 37.

## Week 6 — Religion and the church: Lev. 12; 32–33; 42 in selection; 46–47 (~5.2 hrs: 3.5 desk + 0.8 media + 0.9 cards)

**Primary.** About two hours, in Curley if you can, for his notes on the Latin of 1668. Ch. 12 closely: the natural causes of religion, the four seeds, opinion of ghosts, ignorance of second causes, devotion toward what men fear and taking casual things for prognostics (Lev. 12.11), and the two sorts of men who cultivated them, founders of commonwealths and God's prophets (Lev. 12.12); the rest steadily. Ch. 32, short and whole: natural reason is not to be renounced, and private revelation cannot be verified by anyone else. Ch. 33 at a steady pace, for the claim that only the books the sovereign establishes are canonical, that is, law. Ch. 42 in selection, about forty-five minutes: the opening on the church's power as a power to teach, not command, with belief that cannot be commanded and Naaman bowing in the house of Rimmon; the passage that names the civil sovereign Supreme Pastor; and a skim of the long reply to Bellarmine. Ch. 46, on vain philosophy and separated essences, and ch. 47, on who profits from the kingdom of darkness, the papacy as the ghost of the Roman empire, and the Independency of the primitive Christians.

**Alongside.** [01](01-life-and-times.md), "The atheism inquiry, *Behemoth* and Homer" and "Hardwick, 1679, and the Oxford burning, 1683"; [02](02-works-and-texts.md), the *Leviathan* entry on the Latin of 1668; [03](03-system-and-concepts.md), "Religion: its natural seed, the Christian commonwealth and the Kingdom of Darkness," tension (3) and the last key terms; [04](04-arguments.md), Argument 15; [05](05-conversations.md), "Bramhall," "Cudworth, More and Cavendish" and "Spinoza"; [06](06-reception-and-debates.md), "The Restoration and 'Hobbism'," "The 1666 inquiry and the 1683 burning" and live debate (3). The [Baruch Spinoza](../../eras/03-early-modern/baruch-spinoza.md) entry, for the *Theological-Political Treatise*. In Session B, Martinich's *The Two Gods of Leviathan* against Curley's "I durst not write so boldly."

**Media.** *Review of Week 5, at the start of the week:* Yale, [Lecture 14](https://oyc.yale.edu/political-science/plsc-114/lecture-14) (priced at 50 minutes), on sovereignty as an office, law as command and the modern state. No episode on Parts III–IV was found.

**Checkpoints (book closed).**
1. *What is the natural seed of religion, and who cultivated it?* The four seeds of Lev. 12.11; founders who used them for policy, and prophets who taught by God's command (Lev. 12.12). Say why this account of religion's causes made readers doubt the author's faith. [03](03-system-and-concepts.md), "Religion."
2. *Why must the civil sovereign govern religion?* A ghostly authority beside the civil one divides sovereignty; a church that can command is a commonwealth of Christian men; Scripture is law only where the sovereign makes it so; revelation cannot be checked; belief cannot be commanded, profession is external, and compelled profession is the sovereign's act; the one necessary article is that Jesus is the Christ. So the sovereign is Supreme Pastor. [04](04-arguments.md), Argument 15.
3. *Give the martyrs' objection and the sincerity objection, with replies.* Martyrdom stays open and rebellion does not; martyrs proper are witnesses of the resurrection. On sincerity, the SEP's point that a man hiding atheism would not have defended so many other dangerous views. Martinich against Strauss, Curley and Jesseph. [04](04-arguments.md), Argument 15; [06](06-reception-and-debates.md), live debate (3).
4. *What is the kingdom of darkness, and what does ch. 47 say of Independency?* A confederacy of deceivers who gain power by false doctrine and vain philosophy; ch. 47 asks who profits, and calls the Independency of the primitive Christians perhaps the best, so far as it is without contention. Say why readers dispute whether that is toleration. [04](04-arguments.md), Argument 15, "Where scholars disagree."
5. *What did 1666, 1668 and 1683 do to the book?* A Commons committee received information about it; the Latin of 1668 changed the passages on the Trinity and God and added an appendix on the Nicene Creed; Oxford condemned propositions from it and burned it. [01](01-life-and-times.md); [02](02-works-and-texts.md); [06](06-reception-and-debates.md), "The 1666 inquiry and the 1683 burning."

**Reconstructions.** The civil sovereign governs religion (Argument 15) in full, with its +2-day touch in Session C. The +3-week touches: Arguments 1–3 as one account of the materialist mind, Argument 4, and Arguments 5–6 with a re-reading of ch. 13, the guide's fifth week; Arguments 11–12 and 14 their +1-week touches, Argument 13 its +2-day and +1-week touches in separate sessions. That is the plan's heaviest Session C, eight recalls, and the reason the week's desk reading is light: keep each one short and book closed.

**Cards.** +4: Argument 15; incorporeal substance, the natural seeds of religion, the kingdom of darkness. Deck at 41.

## Week 7 — Free will; the capstone: *Of Liberty and Necessity*; Lev. 21.1–4; *Behemoth* in selection (~5.4 hrs: 4.5 desk incl. the essay + 0 media + 0.9 cards)

**Primary.** About an hour and a half, then the capstone, about two and a half hours. *Of Liberty and Necessity* whole, about forty-five minutes, in Chappell, with Bramhall's side of the exchange beside it: liberty as the absence of impediments not in the agent's nature, deliberation, the will as the last appetite, every sufficient cause a necessary one, and the replies to the bishop on laws, counsel, praise and punishment. Then Lev. 21.1–4 closely, twice: liberty as the absence of opposition, the water in its channel, and "Liberty and Necessity are Consistent" (Lev. 21.4). Then *Behemoth* in selection, about thirty minutes: Dialogue I, for Hobbes's account of who taught the people disobedience; the dossier saved no text of it, so check the selection against your edition's contents.

**Alongside.** [01](01-life-and-times.md), "Bramhall, Wallis and the Restoration," with the "Chronology" as a self-test; [02](02-works-and-texts.md), "The debate with Bramhall" and "The late works"; [03](03-system-and-concepts.md), "How the pieces interlock" as the week's self-test, the key term "Compatibilism" and all five tensions; [04](04-arguments.md), Argument 16; [05](05-conversations.md), "Bramhall," "Leibniz," "Hume" and "The themes trace"; [06](06-reception-and-debates.md) in full as capstone raw material; [08-bibliography.md](08-bibliography.md) for what next. In Session B, the SEP's ["Compatibilism"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/compatibilism/).

**Media.** None assigned; the time goes to the essay. *Optional, after the capstone, not counted:* *In Our Time*, ["Free Will"](https://www.bbc.co.uk/programmes/b00z5y9z) (45 minutes).

**Checkpoints (book closed).**
1. *State Hobbes's compatibilism.* Liberty is absence of external impediment; the will is the last appetite; nothing begins from itself; a sufficient cause is a necessary cause; so a free agent is one who can do if he will and forbear if he will, and liberty and necessity are consistent. [04](04-arguments.md), Argument 16.
2. *Bramhall's two objections and Hobbes's replies.* A liberty shared with water is brutish; necessity makes laws unjust, counsel vain, praise pointless, and God the author of sin. Replies: punishment shapes wills, consultation is itself a cause of choice, praise and blame work by example. [04](04-arguments.md), Argument 16; [05](05-conversations.md), "Bramhall."
3. *What is the modern objection, and who follows Hobbes?* One may act as one wills without impediment under compulsion or hallucination and still not act freely (McKenna, SEP). Hume's *Enquiry* VIII takes the same line, and Locke's *Essay* II.xxi, at row 7, is next. [04](04-arguments.md), Argument 16.
4. *How does* Behemoth *apply Lev. 29?* Universities, preachers and the Commons taught disobedience, so the diseases of ch. 29 become a history of 1640–1660. [01](01-life-and-times.md), "The atheism inquiry"; [02](02-works-and-texts.md), "The late works."
5. *Trace the claim that the sovereign cannot injure a subject back to the definitions of chs. 4, 6 and 14.* Authorization (ch. 16) rests on covenant, covenant on laying down a right, right on liberty (ch. 14), the need for a common judge on there being no common rule of good (ch. 6), and all of it on reasoning as reckoning with defined names (ch. 4). [03](03-system-and-concepts.md), "How the pieces interlock."

**Reconstructions.** Argument 16 in full, with its +2-day touch before the essay; Argument 15 its +1-week touch; Arguments 7–8, 9 and 10 their +3-week touches, or let the capstone count for those it reconstructs.

**Cards.** +1: Argument 16. The deck is complete at 42, permanent in the cumulative FSRS queue.

## Capstone essay (800–1,200 words)

Write in Week 7, after the checkpoints, book closed for the first draft. **The gate relationship, stated exactly:** Stage 3's gate essay is Hume's problem of induction, and none of its exit checkpoints is about Hobbes, so this essay is the dossier's own consolidation, not the gate and not a substitute for it. Prompt 1 is built to prepare the Locke row. Each prompt draws on at least three weeks.

1. **From the state of nature to Leviathan: does the chain hold?** Reconstruct Arguments 5–8 and 11–12 as one argument. Test it with Locke's polecats and lions, Kavka's limited sovereign and Hampton's assurance problem, and say what Locke must deny.
2. **Can Hobbes answer the Fool?** Reconstruct Arguments 9 and 10. Set Kavka's and Gauthier's rational-policy readings against Hoekstra's, with Hume's sensible knave, and say whether the reply needs God's command.
3. **Protection and obedience: does the right of self-defense undo absolutism?** Reconstruct Arguments 8, 13 and 14 with the Review and Conclusion. Set Hampton against Sreedhar, and use *Behemoth* or the exiles of 1651 as your test case.
4. **Does the politics need the materialism?** Reconstruct Arguments 1–4 and 16, and weigh tension (5), the system reading against the claim of the *De Cive* Preface that the civil philosophy stands on experience of one's own passions.
5. **Supreme Pastor: is Hobbes's Christian commonwealth coherent, and was its author a Christian?** Reconstruct Argument 15 with Lev. 12 and 42. Set Martinich against Curley, and test the answer on Naaman and on ch. 47's Independency.

## Mastery checklist — you are done when you can…

- □ Reconstruct the argument of *Leviathan* chs. 13–17 from equality to the commonwealth, and name the step Locke rejects. *(The path's row: chs. 13–17, read before Locke.)*
- □ Explain sense as motion, reasoning as reckoning and the Third Objections' case against a thinking substance, with Descartes's replies.
- □ State the restless desire of power and the three causes of quarrel, and say why the war of all against all does not require that people are evil.
- □ Distinguish the right of nature from a law of nature, state the first two laws, and explain why covenants of mutual trust fail without a common power.
- □ Reconstruct the Fool and the reply, with Hume's knave, and state both sides of whether the laws of nature are laws.
- □ Explain authorization and why sovereignty must be absolute and undivided, with Locke's objection and the reply.
- □ State the true liberties of subjects and say whether they undo absolutism, naming Hampton and Sreedhar.
- □ Explain why the civil sovereign governs religion, and state both sides of the question of Hobbes's sincerity.
- □ State Hobbes's compatibilism, Bramhall's objections and Hobbes's replies.
- □ Say which stories about the life rest only on Hobbes and Aubrey, and what happened to the book in 1666, 1668 and 1683.
