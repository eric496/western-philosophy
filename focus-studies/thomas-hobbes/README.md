# Focus Study: Thomas Hobbes (1588 – 1679)
**English** | [简体中文](../../zh/focus-studies/thomas-hobbes/README.md)

## The figure in brief

Thomas Hobbes was born in April 1588 at Westport, beside Malmesbury in Wiltshire, the son of a quarrelsome country curate; his verse autobiography tells that his mother went into labor early at the news of the Spanish Armada, and "Did bring forth Twins at once, both Me, and Fear" (the English version of 1680). He studied at Magdalen Hall, Oxford, and spent most of his working life in the household of the Cavendish family, as tutor, secretary and companion to two Earls of Devonshire. His first book was a translation of Thucydides (1629). On his continental tours he met Galileo and joined the circle of Marin Mersenne in Paris, and in middle age, by John Aubrey's account, he fell in love with geometry. *The Elements of Law* circulated in manuscript in 1640; fearing Parliament, he fled to Paris, where he wrote the Third Objections to Descartes's *Meditations* (1641) and *De Cive* (1642), and tutored the exiled Prince of Wales in mathematics. *Leviathan* (1651) took him back to England. The rest of a long life went on quarrels: with Bishop Bramhall over free will, with John Wallis over geometry, with Robert Boyle over the air-pump, and with a Restoration Parliament that in 1666 looked into atheistic books with *Leviathan* in view. He died at Hardwick Hall in December 1679, aged ninety-one; in 1683 the University of Oxford condemned propositions drawn from his books and had them burned.

He matters to this library because he built the first modern political philosophy on a materialist account of human beings. Sense is motion, reasoning is reckoning with names, and the will is the last appetite in deliberation. From those premises and the natural equality of human beings he derives the war of every man against every man, the laws of nature as theorems of peace, the covenant that authorizes a sovereign, and the claim that sovereignty must be absolute if it is to work at all. The contract tradition starts here: Locke, Pufendorf, Rousseau and Kant all write in his terms, and much of modern moral and political theory, from the moral sense school's reply to egoism to game theory and legal positivism, still argues with him.

## Why he rewards weeks of study

First, *the system*. *Leviathan* is one argument that runs from the senses to the sovereign and on to the Kingdom of God. The famous chapters on the state of nature depend on the account of the passions and of reasoning that comes before them, and the theory of authorization in chapter 16 is what makes the sovereign of chapter 17 possible. Read in order, the book is tighter and stranger than its reputation.

Second, *the arguments*. The state of nature, the reply to the Fool, authorization, the case for absolute sovereignty and the right of resistance that no covenant can remove are among the most carefully argued and most often simplified texts in political philosophy. Each can be put in numbered premises, and each has a live scholarly dispute about what it is.

Third, *the quarrels*. Hobbes argued with Descartes, Bramhall, Clarendon, Filmer, Wallis and Boyle, and he was answered by Cumberland, Locke, Pufendorf, Butler, Hume and Rousseau. Knowing him well makes the whole early-modern argument about nature, consent and authority legible as a conversation.

## On the quotations

The dossier quotes only public-domain texts, so that every quotation can be checked word for word, and it keeps their spelling. *Leviathan* is quoted from the 1651 text as transcribed by Project Gutenberg (eBook #3207); the *Elements of Law*, *De Cive* in its 1651 English version, *Of Liberty and Necessity* and the other works from Molesworth's *English Works* (1839–45); and the Third Objections, with Descartes's replies, from Haldane and Ross, vol. II (1911–12; corrected reprint 1934). For reading, the dossier recommends Tuck's Cambridge edition, which keeps Hobbes's spelling, or Curley's Hackett edition, which modernizes it.

*Leviathan* is cited by chapter and paragraph (Lev. 13.9). The paragraphs are counted in the saved 1651 text, leaving out its marginal side-headings. Curley numbers paragraphs too, and the count agrees with the citations the *Stanford Encyclopedia* gives from it where the dossier has checked them, but a reader using another edition may find a number off by one where an edition divides a paragraph differently; the chapter is always right. "A Review, and Conclusion" is cited as R&C.
