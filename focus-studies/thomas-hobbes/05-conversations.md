# Hobbes in Conversation — Backward, Sideways, Forward
**English** | [简体中文](../../zh/focus-studies/thomas-hobbes/05-conversations.md)

*Focus study: Thomas Hobbes · [Dossier home](README.md) · [Library entry](../../eras/03-early-modern/thomas-hobbes.md) · [Study guide](../../study/hobbes-leviathan.md)*

Hobbes liked to present himself as a man without teachers. *Leviathan* calls the learning of the schools "Vain Philosophy" (Lev. 46) and offers its civil science as something new. The pose hides a great deal of borrowing and answering. He took his first political lesson from a Greek historian, his vocabulary of right and law from natural lawyers he rarely named, his idea of science from Galileo and Euclid, and his first audience from a friar in Paris. Sideways, he argued in print with Descartes, Bramhall, Wallis and Boyle. Forward, nearly every moral and political philosopher of the next century defined a position against him, and the twentieth century rebuilt him several times over. This file traces those exchanges. The scholarly controversies and the reception by period are in [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md), the life behind the quarrels is in [01-life-and-times.md](01-life-and-times.md), and the arguments cited by number are reconstructed in [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md).

Quotations from Hobbes keep the spelling of the saved public-domain texts, so "Warre" and "Covenants" stand as printed: *Leviathan* in the 1651 text (Project Gutenberg #3207), *De Cive* in the 1651 English *Philosophical Rudiments* (Molesworth II), *Of Liberty and Necessity* and the *Considerations* in Molesworth IV, and the verse life in its 1680 English translation. Descartes is quoted from Haldane & Ross, vol. II (1911–12; corrected reprint 1934). Read *Leviathan* in Tuck's or Curley's edition, as [02-works-and-texts.md](02-works-and-texts.md) advises. Everyone else is paraphrased.

## Backward: what he inherited and what he revised

### Thucydides

Hobbes's first book was a translation, *Eight Bookes of the Peloponnesian Warre* (1629), and the verse life gives the reason: "There's none that pleas'd me like Thucydides. / He says Democracy's a Foolish Thing, / Than a Republick Wiser is one King" (Life, 1680). That is Hobbes's Thucydides more than Thucydides, but it shows what he took: a history of an assembly talked by orators into a ruinous war, and of Corcyra's civil war, in which words changed their meanings (III.82–83). The IEP suggests that Hobbes's picture of human nature owed less to his mechanics than to contemporary events and to historians such as Thucydides (week 1). Read *Leviathan* 13 beside the Corcyra chapters: fear, advantage and reputation drive both (Argument 6). The library has no Thucydides entry.

### Aristotle and the Schoolmen

[Aristotle](../../eras/01-ancient/aristotle.md) is the opponent Hobbes names most. Chapter 46 sums up: "I beleeve that scarce any thing can be more absurdly said in naturall Philosophy, than that which now is called Aristotles Metaphysiques, nor more repugnant to Government, than much of that hee hath said in his Politiques; nor more ignorantly, than a great part of his Ethiques" (Lev. 46). Each charge has a counter-thesis in this dossier. Against the metaphysics, everything real is body, and the doctrine of "Separated Essences" (Lev. 46) is a confusion of names (Arguments 1–2). Against the ethics, there is no "Finis Ultimus" or "Summum Bonum" (Lev. 11.1; Argument 4). Against the politics, the ninth law of nature answers the claim that some are born to rule: "I know that Aristotle in the first booke of his Politiques, for a foundation of his doctrine, maketh men by Nature, some more worthy to Command" (Lev. 15; Argument 5). Chapter 21 adds that the liberty praised by "Aristotle, Cicero, and other men, Greeks and Romanes" was copied from popular states, "not from the Principles of Nature" (Lev. 21.9; Argument 14). The [Aristotle dossier](../aristotle/05-conversations.md) tells the story from the other end.

The schoolmen fare worse. Chapter 8 quotes a chapter title from [Suárez](../../eras/03-early-modern/suarez-and-grotius.md) on God's concourse and asks, "When men write whole volumes of such stuffe, are they not Mad, or intend to make others so?" (Lev. 8). Chapter 1 mocks the theory of species taught "through all the Universities of Christendome" (Lev. 1.5; Argument 1), and *Behemoth*, as the SEP reports, calls Peter Lombard and Duns Scotus two of the most egregious blockheads in the world. The SEP adds the caution that matters: Hobbes kept many connections to the scholastic Aristotelians he rejected, as Leijenhorst's study shows. For this dossier's reader [Aquinas](../../eras/02-medieval/thomas-aquinas.md) is the natural foil, law as an ordinance of reason for the common good against law as the sovereign's command (Arguments 10, 12); the [Aquinas dossier](../thomas-aquinas/05-conversations.md) ends its forward section with Hobbes's mockery.

### Machiavelli

The library entry lists [Machiavelli](../../eras/03-early-modern/niccolo-machiavelli.md) as the figure Hobbes responds to. The IEP's comparison shows why: both treat politics without theological premises. But the textual link is weak. Machiavelli is not named in the saved texts of *Leviathan*, *De Cive* or the *Elements*, and Hobbes's single sovereign is the opposite of the *Discourses*' praise of Roman tumults. Read it as a family resemblance, not a debt.

### Bacon

Around 1620, on the SEP's dating, Hobbes worked for a time as secretary to [Francis Bacon](../../eras/03-early-modern/francis-bacon.md); Aubrey says he took down Bacon's thoughts on walks and helped put some *Essays* into Latin. The DNB judged that Hobbes was scarcely influenced by Bacon's program of induction and experiment, and his method bears that out: his science deduces from definitions, and his quarrel with Boyle (below) was partly a quarrel with Baconian experiment. He may have kept Bacon's attack on the idols of words, which becomes chapter 4's campaign against "insignificant" names (Lev. 4.21; Argument 2). The anecdotes rest on Aubrey and the debt is disputed ([01-life-and-times.md](01-life-and-times.md)).

### Galileo, Harvey and geometry

Two Aubrey stories stand for two readings of Hobbes's method. In one, about 1629–31 (the DNB's dating), he opens Euclid at I.47, refuses to believe it, and is led back proof by proof until convinced. In the other, about 1636, he visits Galileo at Florence. On the geometric reading, Hobbes's science composes conclusions from definitions as Euclid does. On the Paduan reading, the "resolutive-compositive" method that the IEP says most scholars attribute to him came from the Aristotelian logician Zabarella by way of Galileo. The SEP judges that line hard to support, since Galileo's Zabarellan texts are early and Hobbes knew the later works, and offers William Harvey, a Padua graduate whom Hobbes admired, as an alternative route (week 2). Galileo's physics is not in doubt: the *Elements of Law* cites his dialogues on motion (EL I.8.2), and the account of sense as motion carried to brain and heart is Galilean in spirit (Argument 1).

### Grotius and Suárez

[Suárez and Grotius](../../eras/03-early-modern/suarez-and-grotius.md) are, in that entry's phrase, the two hinges between Aquinas's natural law and the contract theorists. Hobbes works inside their vocabulary while barely naming them. Grotius answered the skeptic Carneades by grounding natural law in a desire for society that would bind even if there were no God (*De iure belli ac pacis*, 1625). Hobbes keeps the aim of a natural law the skeptic must accept and reverses the psychology: no natural sociability, only fear and the will to survive (Arguments 6–7). Suárez held that power belongs first to the community, which confers it by pact; Hobbes keeps the pact and denies that the people survive as a body that could take power back (Arguments 11–12). His sharpest correction is to their *ius*: "For though they that speak of this subject, use to confound Jus, and Lex, Right and Law; yet they ought to be distinguished" (Lev. 14.3). Right is liberty, law obligation, and chapter 14 turns on the difference (Argument 7). Richard Tuck's *Natural Rights Theories* (1979, in the SEP bibliography) places Hobbes at the end of a line of rights theorists running through Grotius and Selden (the summary is from memory).

### Selden

John Selden, the legal antiquary, is the one living scholar *Leviathan* praises without reserve: chapter 10 sends readers on titles of honor to "Mr. Seldens most excellent Treatise of that subject" (Lev. 10). The DNB names Selden and Harvey as Hobbes's chief London friends in the 1650s; Selden left him £10 in 1654. Selden's *De jure naturali et gentium juxta disciplinam Ebraeorum* (1640) grounded natural law in God's command, a minimal theory some historians, Tuck among them, have set beside Hobbes's view that the laws of nature are properly laws only as commands (from memory; unchecked; Argument 10). The library has no Selden entry.

## Sideways: friends, rivals and opponents

### Mersenne and Gassendi

Marin Mersenne, the Minim friar whose Paris cell was the clearing house of European science, met Hobbes on the tour of 1634–1637: "Here with Mersennus I acquainted grew, / Shew'd him of Motion what I ever knew" (Life, 1680). The DNB reports that Hobbes contributed scientific papers to books Mersenne published, and it was through Mersenne that he received the *Meditations* and corresponded with Descartes. In the exile of 1640–1651 the circle was his home, and the SEP singles out Pierre Gassendi, reviver of Epicurean atomism, as a particular friend. Gassendi wrote the Fifth Objections, and the two shared a materialist bent and a distrust of innate ideas, though Gassendi kept an immaterial rational soul (from memory; unchecked). The [early modern women philosophers](../../eras/03-early-modern/early-modern-women-philosophers.md) entry adds the Cavendish household in exile, whose guests included Hobbes, Descartes, Mersenne, Gassendi and Kenelm Digby.

### Descartes: the optics quarrel and the Third Objections

It began badly. In 1640, the SEP reports, Hobbes sent Mersenne comments on Descartes's *Discourse* and *Optics*; replies went back and forth through Mersenne, and Descartes suspected Hobbes of, in the SEP's translation, "aiming to make his reputation at my expense, and by devious means". In 1641 Hobbes's comments on the *Meditations* were printed as the Third Objections, with [Descartes](../../eras/03-early-modern/rene-descartes.md)'s curt replies (week 2).

They are the best short statement of Hobbes's philosophy of mind. He grants the cogito and denies the next step: I may infer that I think, not that I am a thought, any more than "I am walking; hence I am the walking" (Obj. III, Obj. 2); the thinking thing may be a body (Argument 3). Reasoning may be only a joining of names (Argument 2). And since every idea is an image, "Hence it appears that we have no idea of God" (Obj. III, Obj. 5), which strikes at the Third Meditation's proof. Descartes replied that by "idea" he meant whatever the mind directly perceives, not a picture in the imagination, and that "in reasoning we unite not names but the things signified by the names", or "a Frenchman and a German" could not reason about the same things (Obj. III, reply to Obj. 4). Whether the two talked past each other is still argued (Argument 3). Their one recorded meeting, in 1648, went no better (SEP). The [Descartes dossier](../rene-descartes/05-conversations.md) tells it from his side.

### Bramhall: liberty, necessity and the "Rebell’s Catechism"

John Bramhall, royalist bishop of Derry (archbishop of Armagh after 1660), was Hobbes's most persistent theological opponent. The DNB dates their first debate to 1646 in Paris, before the Marquis of Newcastle (some modern accounts say 1645). Hobbes's written reply was printed without his consent in 1654 as *Of Liberty and Necessity*; Bramhall printed the whole exchange in 1655; Hobbes answered with *The Questions concerning Liberty, Necessity, and Chance* (1656); Bramhall replied with *Castigations* (1658) and an appendix, *The Catching of Leviathan*; Hobbes's *Answer* to it, written in 1668, appeared in 1682 (all from the DNB).

The core is Argument 16. Bramhall defended liberty as freedom from necessity, a power to do otherwise with all else the same. Hobbes held that every volition has a sufficient cause, so liberty is the absence of impediments to doing what one wills: "I acknowledge this liberty, that I can do if I will; but to say, I can will if I will, I take to be an absurd speech" (LN, EW IV 240). *Leviathan* compresses it: "Liberty and Necessity are Consistent" (Lev. 21.4). The SEP's "Compatibilism" calls Hobbes's definition an exemplary expression of classical compatibilism (week 7). Bramhall's political charge lasted as long: he called *Leviathan* a "Rebell’s Catechism" (as the SEP quotes him), because the retained right of self-defense and the end of obedience when protection fails seemed to license deserting a losing king (Arguments 13–14). The quarrel over God's nature ended with Hobbes's oddest theology, a God who is, in the SEP's report of the *Answer*, a "corporeal spirit" (Argument 15). The DNB's verdict, "The argument upon necessity shows Hobbes at his best", is worth testing against Bramhall's strongest point, that a necessitated agent cannot justly be blamed.

### Clarendon

Edward Hyde, later Earl of Clarendon, knew Hobbes from the Great Tew circle ([01-life-and-times.md](01-life-and-times.md)) and became his most eminent royalist critic. The DNB reports Clarendon's story that Hobbes, warned against publishing *Leviathan*, answered, "The truth is, I have a mind to go home"; the SEP notes that Clarendon took him for a supporter of Cromwell. Clarendon's *Brief View and Survey* of *Leviathan* was written by 1670 and printed in 1676 (DNB). Its charges, paraphrased, are that chapter 13 draws mankind from its worst specimens (as [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md) reports under Argument 4), and, in outline and unchecked against the text, that the "mutuall Relation between Protection and Obedience" (Lev. Review and Conclusion) excuses submission to usurpers (Argument 13). The library has no Clarendon entry.

### Filmer and Harrington

Sir Robert Filmer, the patriarchalist later refuted in Locke's *First Treatise*, was in the DNB's account Hobbes's first purely political assailant. His *Observations concerning the Originall of Government* (1652) accepted Hobbes's rights of sovereignty and rejected his route: a multitude cannot covenant as one body, men never assembled to authorize anyone, so the covenant is a fiction (paraphrased; Argument 11). The SEP marks the deeper split: Filmer held women subordinate by nature, while Hobbes held them naturally free, allowed that they can be sovereign, and gave the mother original dominion over a child (Argument 5). James Harrington, republican author of *Oceana* (1656), cited Hobbes often and, the DNB notes, paid him a high compliment in *The Prerogative of Popular Government*. He agreed that the state rests on power and turned the point against monarchy: power follows the balance of property, so a land of freeholders needs a commonwealth, a government of laws rather than of men (paraphrased from memory; unchecked; Arguments 12, 14). Neither has a library entry.

### Wallis and Boyle

The quarrels Hobbes lost were with the new science. *De Corpore* (1655) claimed to square the circle; John Wallis, Savilian Professor of Geometry, answered with the *Elenchus Geometriae Hobbianae* (1655), and the exchange ran until 1672 (DNB). Wallis added that *Leviathan* had been written for Cromwell, and in the *Considerations* (1662) Hobbes defended himself in the third person, quoting a French letter that ranked him with "the Galileos, the Descarteses, the Hobbeses, the Bacons, and the Gassendis" (Considerations, EW IV 436). With Robert Boyle the issue was method. Hobbes's *Dialogus physicus* (1661) attacked Boyle's air-pump experiments: experiments cannot settle causes, which must be demonstrated (DNB; SEP). The SEP cites Shapin and Schaffer's *Leviathan and the Air-Pump*, which reads the quarrel as a dispute about how agreed knowledge, and so order, is produced. The Royal Society never admitted him.

### Cudworth, More and Cavendish

Cambridge answered the metaphysics. Ralph Cudworth attacked Hobbes's principles as early as a thesis of 1644 (DNB), and his *True Intellectual System of the Universe* (1678) is, as the SEP describes it, a sustained argument against Hobbesian atheism and materialism; he was among those who thought Hobbes denied God. Henry More argued against Hobbes's materialism in *The Immortality of the Soul* (1659). Margaret Cavendish answered from the other side: the [women philosophers](../../eras/03-early-modern/early-modern-women-philosophers.md) entry calls her materialism the opposite of a mechanism, since for Hobbes matter is inert and must be pushed, while for her it moves itself (Arguments 1, 3).

### Spinoza

[Spinoza](../../eras/03-early-modern/baruch-spinoza.md) took most from Hobbes and changed it most. The DNB found Hobbes's influence "remarkably shown in Spinoza's political treatises", and the SEP credits both with the critical reading of Scripture that *Leviathan* Part III begins (Argument 15). The *Theological-Political Treatise* (1670) agrees that natural right extends as far as power and that the sovereign controls public religion. In Letter 50 (1674) Spinoza told Jarig Jelles the difference: he keeps natural right intact, so a sovereign has right over subjects only as far as its power exceeds theirs (paraphrased; the [Spinoza dossier](../baruch-spinoza/05-conversations.md) quotes Curley's translation). For Hobbes the covenant transfers right; for Spinoza right never outruns power (Arguments 8, 12).

## Forward: the heirs and the critics

### Cumberland and Pufendorf

Richard Cumberland's *De Legibus Naturae* (1672) attacks chiefly Hobbes's "theory of selfishness" (DNB). It argues that the laws of nature are real laws, promulgated by God through the order of things, and that they enjoin universal benevolence (Argument 10, where the reply is paraphrased). The SEP's history of utilitarianism counts him among the first "theological" utilitarians, so the first systematic answer to Hobbes is also an ancestor of the school that later revived him.

Samuel Pufendorf built the century's most influential natural-law system from Hobbes's materials. His *Elements of Universal Jurisprudence* (1660) was, in the SEP's words, "explicitly based on Grotius and Hobbes", and *On the Law of Nature and of Nations* (1672) engages him throughout. Pufendorf took law to be the command of a superior, citing *De Cive* 14.1, and added that the superior needs legitimate reasons as well as strength (Argument 10). He granted that a pure state of nature would be as Hobbes described it and argued that any real one is a peace, if an insecure one (DJN II.2.7, 12; Argument 6). His fundamental law, to cultivate sociality, is Grotius's against Hobbes's, and his revised edition of 1684 played up Cumberland and the Stoics to make its "problematic Hobbesian inheritance" less obvious (SEP). Neither has a library entry.

### Locke

[Locke](../../eras/03-early-modern/john-locke.md) keeps state of nature, consent and covenant and changes what each does. His state of nature has a law forbidding harm to life, health, liberty or possessions, so its trouble is "inconveniences" (the Locke entry's word), not war. Against absolutism he offers the polecats and foxes: it is odd to guard against the harms of neighbors and then consent to be devoured by a lion (*Second Treatise* §93, paraphrased; Argument 12). Government becomes a dissoluble trust, which reopens the resistance Hobbes's narrow residual right was meant to close (Argument 14), and "Of Conquest" denies the conqueror's rights (Argument 13). The debt is disputed. The DNB thought Locke owed Hobbes little; the SEP points to quieter debts: anti-nativism, the abuse of words, the admission that God could make matter think, skepticism about revelation. Locke never names Hobbes in his own voice, though the *Essay* sets "a Hobbist", who keeps his word "Because the public requires it, and the Leviathan will punish you if you do not", beside the Christian and the old philosophers (E I.iii.5), and the *First Treatise* names Filmer's *Observations on Mr. Hobbes's Leviathan* (FT §14).

### Shaftesbury, Hutcheson and Butler

The [moral sense](../../eras/03-early-modern/moral-sense-school.md) entry describes one argument in three stages against "the selfish theory", with Hobbes its famous source (Argument 4). Shaftesbury answered with irony: Hobbes was a good sociable man who spent his life warning others of danger, which no purely selfish man would do (*Sensus Communis*, 1709, as the entry reports him). Hutcheson made the moral sense a faculty and pressed the case of approving a generous act in a distant country, which can do us no good. Butler's *Fifteen Sermons* (1726) supplied the logic: passions aim at their objects, not at our pleasure, so a desire's being mine does not make its object me, and benevolence no more opposes self-love than hunger does. Whether this hits Hobbes depends on whether he was a psychological egoist, which Bernard Gert denied and Gregory Kavka qualified (Argument 4).

### Rousseau

[Rousseau](../../eras/03-early-modern/jean-jacques-rousseau.md)'s *Discourse on Inequality* (1755) charges that Hobbes carried into the state of nature passions formed in society. The Rousseau entry puts it sharply: Hobbes missed pity, and so built war into a nature that held the seed of peace (Argument 6). The *Social Contract* (1762) keeps a single sovereign will and the total alienation of each associate's rights, and gives sovereignty to the people, inalienable and unrepresentable (Arguments 11–12; from memory). In the chapter on civil religion Rousseau praises Hobbes as the one Christian writer who proposed to reunite the eagle's two heads, civil and religious power (*Social Contract* IV.8; from memory; unchecked; Argument 15).

### Hume

[Hume](../../eras/03-early-modern/david-hume.md) is closer than his footnotes admit. The SEP reports Paul Russell's argument that the *Treatise* follows the plan of the *Elements of Law*, and notes that ideas as fainter copies of impressions resemble Hobbes's "Decaying Sense" (Lev. 2.2; Argument 1). In ethics Hume put Hobbes with Locke among holders of "the selfish system of morals" (EPM App. 2, as the [Hume dossier](../david-hume/05-conversations.md) reports), and his sensible knave restates the Fool (Argument 9). "Of the Original Contract" (1748) grounds allegiance in interest and habit, not promise, which drops the covenant and keeps the diagnosis (from memory; unchecked). On liberty they agreed (Argument 16).

### Bentham, Austin and legal positivism

The utilitarians rehabilitated Hobbes. The DNB says they valued his clarity, his recognition of utility as the aim of social action and his theory of sovereignty, and that their interest produced Molesworth's edition. The SEP says [Bentham](../../eras/04-19th-century/jeremy-bentham.md) drew on Hobbes's account of human nature, and that the inherited egoism sits uneasily with the principle of utility. John Austin's *Province of Jurisprudence Determined* (1832) defined law as the command of a sovereign habitually obeyed who obeys no one, which is *Leviathan* 26 made general: "Law in generall, is not Counsell, but Command; nor a Command of any man to any man; but only of him, whose Command is addressed to one formerly obliged to obey him" (Lev. 26; Argument 10). [H. L. A. Hart](../../eras/05-20th-century/h-l-a-hart.md) opened *The Concept of Law* (1961) by dismantling Austin's model, and his entry lists Hobbes among those he answers. Whether Hobbes was a positivist or an unusual natural lawyer is a live dispute ([03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md)).

### Leibniz

The SEP judges that [Leibniz](../../eras/03-early-modern/gottfried-wilhelm-leibniz.md) paid Hobbes more attention than any canonical philosopher from Descartes to Kant. The young Leibniz adopted the view that "Reason, in this sense, is nothing but Reckoning" (Lev. 5.2), criticized the nominalism behind it, studied Hobbes on *conatus*, wrote to him twice in the 1670s without known reply, and returned to him in the *Theodicy* (Arguments 2, 16).

### Game theory and contractarianism: Gauthier, Kavka, Hampton

The SEP reports a continuing debate over whether the war of all against all comes from shortsightedness, from rational agents caught in a prisoner's dilemma, or from passions such as pride, and says game theorists have been particularly active in it. David Gauthier's *The Logic of Leviathan* (1969) read the laws of nature as theorems of prudence, and his *Morals by Agreement* (1986) built a contractarian ethics on Hobbesian premises, answering the Fool with the constrained maximizer (Arguments 9–10). Gregory Kavka's *Hobbesian Moral and Political Theory* (1986) worked from a weaker "predominant egoism" and concluded that the argument supports a strong, not an absolute, sovereign (Arguments 4, 12). Jean Hampton's *Hobbes and the Social Contract Tradition* (1986) named Hobbes's an "alienation contract" and posed the dilemma that has shaped the field: if the war comes from passions, the passions survive the contract; if from rationality, rational agents will defect from it too (Arguments 6, 8, 12). The SEP's "Contractarianism" sets this "Hobbesian line" beside the Kantian contractualism of [Rawls](../../eras/05-20th-century/john-rawls.md) and Scanlon.

### Realism in international relations

Chapter 13 offers one state of nature no one can deny: "Kings, and persons of Soveraigne authority, because of their Independency, are in continuall jealousies, and in the state and posture of Gladiators; having their weapons pointing, and their eyes fixed on one another" (Lev. 13.12). The SEP notes that this made Hobbes "the representative example of a “realist” in international relations". *De Cive* had put it as a pair: "that man to man is a kind of God; and that man to man is an arrant wolf. The first is true, if we compare citizens amongst themselves; and the second, if we compare cities" (*De Cive*, Epistle Dedicatory). Realists from Hans Morgenthau to Kenneth Waltz read interstate anarchy this way (from memory; unchecked). They tend to drop the next sentence: because sovereigns uphold their subjects' industry, "there does not follow from it, that misery, which accompanies the Liberty of particular men" (Lev. 13.12). Hobbes proposed no world Leviathan.

### Schmitt and Strauss

Two German readers of the 1930s made Hobbes the hinge of modernity. Carl Schmitt's *Der Leviathan in der Staatslehre des Thomas Hobbes* (1938), from lectures given that year in Leipzig and Kiel, is subtitled "Meaning and Failure of a Political Symbol" (checked at the University of Chicago Press record of the English translation). Its thesis, in outline, is that Hobbes's reservation of private belief from public confession (Lev. 37, 42) was the crack through which liberal individualism later broke the state (from memory; unchecked; Argument 15). [Leo Strauss](../../eras/05-20th-century/leo-strauss.md)'s *The Political Philosophy of Hobbes* (1936) argued that the moral basis came before the science and lay in the opposition of vanity and fear of violent death, and that Hobbes made right rather than law the foundation of morals, founding modern natural-rights theory (Arguments 4, 7). The Strauss entry places Hobbes in the "first wave" of modernity. Strauss's 1932 notes on Schmitt's *Concept of the Political* had already argued that Schmitt's critique of liberalism stayed inside Hobbes's horizon (from memory; unchecked). The library has no Schmitt entry.

### Oakeshott

Michael Oakeshott read Hobbes as the philosopher of civil association: a theorist of authority rather than power, of will and artifice rather than reason and nature, whose state is a framework of rules within which individuals pursue their own ends, and whose just man keeps faith from pride rather than calculation (from memory; unchecked; Arguments 9, 11). The sources are his introduction to the Blackwell *Leviathan* (1946) and *Hobbes on Civil Association* (1975, in the SEP bibliography). The library has no Oakeshott entry.

### Skinner and the historians

Quentin Skinner changed how Hobbes is read by reading him in context. "Conquest and Consent" (1972; unchecked) set the Review and Conclusion among the de facto theorists of the Engagement controversy (Argument 13). *Reason and Rhetoric in the Philosophy of Hobbes* (1996, in the SEP) traced his turn from humanist rhetoric to civil science and back (Argument 2). "Hobbes and the Purely Artificial Person of the State" (1999) argued that the commonwealth, not the sovereign, is the person represented (Argument 11). *Hobbes and Republican Liberty* (2008; unchecked) argued that the definition of liberty as absence of external impediment was built to defeat the republican claim, the claim of the "LIBERTAS" on the turrets of Lucca (Lev. 21.8; Argument 14). With Tuck, Malcolm, Hoekstra and Sommerville, all in the SEP bibliographies, historical reading became a scholarly default; [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md) weighs it against the analytic reconstructions.

## The themes trace

The library's [problem index](../../themes.md) places Hobbes in two of its nine threads, and this dossier argues for two more.

- [§5 Politics & Justice](../../themes.md#5-politics--justice--how-should-we-live-together-and-who-may-rule) — The social contract: the war of all against all, covenant and authorization, an absolute sovereign resting on consent, the narrow right to resist. Weeks 3–5; Thucydides, Aristotle, Grotius and Suárez behind, Bramhall, Filmer, Harrington and Spinoza beside, Locke, Pufendorf, Rousseau, Hume, Austin, the game theorists, the realists, Schmitt and Strauss ahead (Arguments 5–14).
- [§7 Language & Meaning](../../themes.md#7-language--meaning--how-do-words-and-thoughts-hook-onto-the-world) — Reasoning is reckoning with names; truth lies in their right ordering. Week 2; the schoolmen behind, Descartes beside, Leibniz and Locke ahead (Argument 2).

His absence from §2 and §4 is a filing gap, not a gap in his thought. The [Mind thread](../../themes.md#2-mind--body--what-is-mind-and-how-does-it-relate-to-the-body) is where his materialism belongs: sense as motion, the thinking thing as a body, the Third Objections as the first materialist reply to Descartes (week 2; Arguments 1, 3; Gassendi beside, Cudworth, More and Cavendish against). It is also the natural home of free will, which the library files nowhere by itself: liberty as the absence of external impediment, consistent with the necessity of every volition (week 7; Argument 16; Bramhall against, Leibniz and Hume ahead). The [Ethics thread](../../themes.md#4-ethics--the-good-life--how-should-i-live-and-what-is-right) already lists Shaftesbury, Hutcheson and Butler as writing "Against Hobbesian egoism", so the opponent should stand beside them: good as the object of desire, power after power, the laws of nature as theorems of peace, the Fool (weeks 3–4; Arguments 4, 7, 9, 10; Cumberland, the moral-sense writers and Bentham ahead). The corrections log proposes both additions.

Three threads are near misses: [§3 God & Religion](../../themes.md#3-god--religion--does-god-exist-and-what-follows-for-faith-and-reason) for Parts III–IV and the corporeal God (week 6; Argument 15), [§8 Science & Method](../../themes.md#8-science--method--how-should-inquiry-proceed-and-what-does-it-prove) for the resolutive-compositive method and the Boyle quarrel, and [§1 Knowledge & Skepticism](../../themes.md#1-knowledge--skepticism--what-can-we-know-and-how-do-we-know-it) for the empiricism the SEP traces from Hobbes through Locke to Hume. §6 (Self & Identity) and §9 (History & Progress) are fair omissions: *Behemoth* is a history of the Civil War, not a philosophy of history.
