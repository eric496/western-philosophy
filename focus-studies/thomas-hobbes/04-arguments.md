# Hobbes: The Arguments
**English** | [简体中文](../../zh/focus-studies/thomas-hobbes/04-arguments.md)

*Focus study · [Dossier home](README.md) · Library entry: [Thomas Hobbes](../../eras/03-early-modern/thomas-hobbes.md) · The system these arguments build: [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md) · Study guide: [*Leviathan* chs. 13–17 and following](../../study/hobbes-leviathan.md)*

Each argument below is reconstructed in numbered premises in the house style: the text and its setting, the reconstruction, what each premise rests on, the strongest objection, and the best reply. The reply is Hobbes's own where he gave one and the literature's where he did not, and it is labeled either way. Where scholars disagree about what the argument even is, the readings are set out with names on each side. Reconstruct each argument yourself, book closed, before reading the version here; the reconstructions are a check on your own, not a substitute for it.

Hobbes argues in a chain, and the chain is the reason to read him in order. A theory of mind as moving matter (Arguments 1 to 3) gives him a theory of desire and power (Argument 4). Desire and rough equality give him the state of nature as a state of war (Arguments 5 and 6). The war gives reason something to say: the right of nature, the first law of nature, and the laying down of rights by covenant (Arguments 7 and 8). Arguments 9 to 16 then carry the chain through justice, authorization and sovereignty to religion and free will. The learning path reads *Leviathan* chapters 13 to 17 in excerpt and the rest at map level; the study guide reconstructs three of these arguments, the state-of-nature argument, the passage from laws of nature to covenant, and the case for undivided sovereignty. This file goes further into the text and the disputes, and starts earlier, with the materialism that Hobbes thought the politics needed. Arguments 1 to 3 belong to week 2 of the study plan, Argument 4 to weeks 2 and 3, Arguments 5 and 6 to week 3, and Arguments 7 and 8 to week 4.

**Citations and spelling.** *Leviathan* is cited by chapter and paragraph: (Lev. 13.9) means chapter 13, paragraph 9. Quotations come from the 1651 text as transcribed by Project Gutenberg (eBook #3207, prepared from the Pelican Classics edition, which was itself set from the first edition), because it is in the public domain and anyone can check it. Quotations keep the 1651 spelling exactly as that text prints it: "Warre", "Covenants", "els", "onely". Nothing inside quotation marks is modernized, and nothing that is a paraphrase is put in quotation marks. Paragraphs are counted in that saved text from the chapter title, leaving out the short marginal headings that the transcription prints as separate lines ("The Second Law Of Nature"). Editions that print paragraph numbers, such as Curley's (Hackett) and Malcolm's (Clarendon), may divide a few paragraphs differently. In chapter 14 the transcription prints the statement of the first law of nature as a paragraph of its own (14.5 here), after the paragraph on the right to every thing (14.4); if your edition joins the two, the later chapter 14 numbers here will run one ahead of yours, so look a paragraph back.

The reading editions are different from the quoted ones. Read *Leviathan* in Tuck's Cambridge Texts edition, which keeps Hobbes's spelling, or Curley's Hackett edition, which modernizes it, with the dossier's study plan; read the *Elements of Law* in Gaskin's Oxford World's Classics edition and *De Cive* in Tuck and Silverthorne's *On the Citizen* (Cambridge). Other works are cited by part, chapter and section as their editions print them. The *Elements of Law* is cited as EL, with part, chapter and section (EL I.2.4), and quoted from Molesworth's edition (vol. IV, 1840), where Part I appears under its old title *Human Nature*. *De Cive* is cited by chapter and section (*De Cive* I.3) or as the Preface, and quoted from the 1651 English translation, *Philosophical Rudiments concerning Government and Society*, in Molesworth vol. II; Molesworth modernized that text's spelling, and the quotations keep his. The Third Objections to Descartes's *Meditations* (1641), which are Hobbes's, are cited by objection number (Obj. III, Obj. 2) and quoted, with Descartes's replies, in Haldane and Ross's translation, *The Philosophical Works of Descartes*, cited as Haldane & Ross, vol. II (1911–12; corrected reprint 1934). Hobbes's critics Bramhall, Clarendon and Cumberland have no saved public-domain text in this dossier and are paraphrased.

**Argument 1. Sense is motion: the materialist account of perception (Lev. 1; Third Objections)**

*What it proves:* that perception is a chain of motions running from the object to the brain and heart, that the colors, sounds and smells we perceive are not in the objects as they appear to us, and that the schoolmen's *species* which objects were said to send into the senses do not exist.

**Text and setting.** *Leviathan* opens with this argument because everything else in the book is built on thought, and Hobbes holds that all thought begins in sense. He keeps the chapter short. Knowing the natural cause of sense, he says, "is not very necessary to the business now in hand", and he has written of it at large elsewhere (Lev. 1.3). The elsewhere includes *The Elements of Law* of 1640, whose second chapter argues the same case at more length, and the later *De Corpore* (1655). The Introduction has already announced the frame: "life is but a motion of Limbs", and the heart is "but a Spring" (Lev. Introduction). The same conviction runs through the objections Hobbes sent to Descartes in 1641, which end on the claim that "mind will be nothing but the motions in certain parts of an organic body" (Obj. III, Obj. 4). See [René Descartes](../../eras/03-early-modern/rene-descartes.md) and the [Descartes dossier](../rene-descartes/README.md).

1. Every thought begins in sense: "there is no conception in a mans mind, which hath not at first, totally, or by parts, been begotten upon the organs of Sense" (Lev. 1.2).
2. "The cause of Sense, is the Externall Body, or Object, which presseth the organ proper to each Sense", directly in taste and touch, through a medium in sight, hearing and smell (Lev. 1.4).
3. The pressure is carried inward by the nerves "to the Brain, and Heart", where it causes "a resistance, or counter-pressure, or endeavour of the heart, to deliver it self", and because that endeavour is outward it "seemeth to be some matter without" (Lev. 1.4).
4. Nothing but motion is present at any stage: the sensible qualities are, in the object, "so many several motions of the matter", and in us they are nothing else "but divers motions; (for motion, produceth nothing but motion.)" (Lev. 1.4).
5. The qualities as they appear can be separated from the objects, as images are by mirrors and sounds by echoes, so "the object is one thing, the image or fancy is another" (Lev. 1.4).

Therefore "Sense in all cases, is nothing els but originall fancy, caused (as I have said) by the pressure, that is, by the motion, of externall things upon our Eyes, Eares, and other organs" (Lev. 1.4). The doctrine of visible and intelligible species taught "through all the Universities of Christendome" is false (Lev. 1.5). Chapter 2 extends the result to the rest of the mind: once the object is gone the motion continues, weakening, and "Imagination therefore is nothing but Decaying Sense" (Lev. 2.2). Memory, experience, dreams and understanding are all, on this account, aftereffects of the same motions.

**What each premise rests on.** Premise 1 is the empiricist starting point, asserted, not argued. Premises 2 and 3 are seventeenth-century physiology, in which nerves carry motions to the brain and the heart is the seat of vital motion. Premise 4 is the deepest commitment: the mechanical philosophy's rule that bodies change only by being moved by other bodies in contact, which *De Corpore* tries to make the foundation of all science. Premise 5 is the Galilean argument from illusions. The *Elements* sets it out at more length, with double vision, reflection in water and glass, the flash seen when the eye is struck, and multiplied echoes, and it states the conclusion more sharply than *Leviathan* does: "the subject wherein colour and image are inherent, is not the object or thing seen", but "the sentient" (EL I.2.4). The SEP article on Hobbes (Duncan) notes the difference. In *Leviathan* Hobbes is happy to say that red in the object is motion and red in us is motion, and to leave aside where the color itself belongs; the *Elements* had put colors firmly in the perceiver.

**Objection.** The strongest objection is aimed at the step from premise 4 to the conclusion. Hobbes says that sense "consisteth" in a light, a color or a sound, and that this "Seeming, or Fancy" is caused by motion; he also says that nothing but motion is there. If seeming is a motion, the account owes us an explanation of why one particular motion seems to be a red patch out there and another a sound, and it gives none. If seeming is not a motion, then motion has produced something other than motion, and premise 4 is false. The SEP article puts a smaller version of the point: it is unclear why an endeavour from inside outward should make the sensation seem to come from outside, since motions from outside run the other way. Descartes made the large version against the last of Hobbes's objections. There are, he replied, no two things in the world between which there is less agreement than "between motion and spirit, which are of two entirely different natures", so that it would be "as correct to infer that earth is heaven" as to conclude that mind is motion (Obj. III, reply to Obj. 4). Later critics gave the gap famous shapes: Leibniz's mill in the *Monadology* (1714), and in the twentieth century Joseph Levine's *explanatory gap*.

**Reply.** The literature's, built on Hobbes's text. First, Hobbes does not need to deduce appearance from motion; he identifies them. "apparence" and "Fancy" are names for the motion as it is had by the body in which it occurs, the way "Imagination" and "Memory" are, by his account, two names for one decaying motion (Lev. 2.3, as the SEP article reads it). On that reading premise 4 is consistent with the conclusion: appearing is a kind of motion, and the question why this motion appears as red and that one as a sound is a question for physiology, not a refutation. Second, Hobbes's case is partly an argument by explanation, not by proof. SEP observes that he offers few explicit arguments for materialism and may simply have trusted the new science, as William Harvey had brought the circulation of the blood under mechanical explanation, to do the same for the mind. If sense, imagination, memory and (Argument 2) reasoning can be accounted for by motions plus language, an immaterial soul is idle. Third, Descartes's reply assumes what is in dispute, namely that thought and motion have "entirely different natures". The reply leaves a debt: Hobbes says *that* sense is a motion, never how a motion comes to appear, and the identity theory he anticipates still argues about the same gap.

**Where scholars disagree.** Three points are open. (a) Does Hobbes identify sensation with motion or make it an effect of motion? The SEP article says only that sensation is grounded in the internal motions and perhaps identical with them, and the text of Lev. 1.4 supports both readings, since it calls the fancy both the motion's "apparence" and something the motion "causeth". (b) How new is the theory? Cees Leijenhorst (*The Mechanisation of Aristotelianism*, 2002) reads Hobbes's account of sense as a mechanized reworking of the scholastic theory he mocks, keeping its structure while replacing species with motions; older studies, such as Frithiof Brandt's *Thomas Hobbes' Mechanical Conception of Nature* (1928), read it as Galilean physics applied to the mind. (c) What does the account do for the politics? One line, which Tom Sorell's *Hobbes* (1986) and the system reading generally take, holds that the civil philosophy is meant to follow from the physics. Another, stated in SEP's discussion of method and in much historical work (Skinner, Malcolm), holds that Hobbes himself said the political principles can be known from experience of one's own passions without the physics, as the Preface to *De Cive* suggests, so that Argument 1 supports the politics without being a premise of it.

**Argument 2. Reasoning is reckoning: names, definitions and truth in speech (Lev. 4–5)**

*What it proves:* that reasoning is nothing but adding and subtracting names, that truth belongs to speech and not to things, that science is knowledge of the consequences of definitions, and that a name joining contradictory meanings, such as "Incorporeall Substance", signifies nothing.

**Text and setting.** Chapter 4 is Hobbes's philosophy of language and chapter 5 his account of reason. Speech, he says, is the invention without which there would have been "neither Common-wealth, nor Society, nor Contract, nor Peace, no more than amongst Lyons, Bears, and Wolves" (Lev. 4.1). Names serve first as marks to aid one's own memory and then as signs that tell others what we think (Lev. 4.3). The chapter is also where Hobbes's materialism meets its opponents: if words are the only universals, the schoolmen's abstract essences are words, and some of their words are nonsense.

1. Names are marks or signs arbitrarily imposed on our conceptions; universal names are the only universals, one name imposed on many things "for their similitude in some quality, or other accident" (Lev. 4.7).
2. An affirmation joins two names. It is true if the later name signifies all that the former signifies, otherwise false: "For True and False are attributes of Speech, not of things. And where Speech in not, there is neither Truth nor Falshood" (Lev. 4.11).
3. So "Truth consisteth in the right ordering of names in our affirmations", and whoever seeks precise truth must fix what each name stands for. Settling significations is what geometers call definition, which they place at the start of their reckoning (Lev. 4.12).
4. Errors in definitions "multiply themselves, according as the reckoning proceeds" (Lev. 4.13). Hence "words are wise mens counters, they do but reckon by them: but they are the mony of fooles" (Lev. 4.13).
5. A name made of two names whose significations are inconsistent, "as this name, an Incorporeall Body, or (which is all one) an Incorporeall Substance", signifies nothing (Lev. 4.22).
6. "Reason, in this sense, is nothing but Reckoning (that is, Adding and Substracting) of the Consequences of generall names agreed upon, for the Marking and Signifying of our thoughts" (Lev. 5.2).
7. Reason itself is always right, but "no one mans Reason, nor the Reason of any one number of men, makes the certaintie". In a disputed account the parties must set up the reason "of some Arbitrator, or Judge", or their dispute "must either come to blowes, or be undecided, for want of a right Reason constituted by Nature" (Lev. 5.3).

Therefore science is reckoning from definitions to their consequences; it is conditional knowledge, of what follows if things are so named; and where men dispute what reason requires, there is no natural umpire, only an appointed one.

**What each premise rests on.** Premise 1 is Hobbes's nominalism, stated in the paragraph before: there is "nothing in the world Universall but Names; for the things named, are every one of them Individual and Singular" (Lev. 4.6). The SEP article treats it as a central commitment of his philosophy of mind. Premise 2 is a definition of truth that follows from it: if there are no universal things, a general truth can only be a relation among general names. Premise 3 is the model of geometry, "the onely Science that it hath pleased God hitherto to bestow on mankind" (Lev. 4.12), which Hobbes, by Aubrey's well-known story, fell in love with in middle age. Premise 6 generalizes arithmetic: Hobbes argues in Lev. 5.1 that geometers, logicians, writers on politics and lawyers all add and subtract, the last two adding "Pactions, to find mens Duties". Premise 7 is the hinge to the politics. The same structure, a disputed reckoning with no umpire set by nature, is the structure of the state of nature (Argument 6), and the umpire men appoint is the sovereign.

**Objection.** Descartes made the first objection, against the version of premise 6 in Hobbes's fourth objection. Hobbes had suggested that reasoning may be "nothing more than the uniting and stringing together of names or designations by the word is", so that reason tells us nothing about things, only about names (Obj. III, Obj. 4). Descartes replied that "in reasoning we unite not names but the things signified by the names", and asked how else "a Frenchman and a German" could reason in exactly the same way about the same things while conceiving the words quite differently (Obj. III, reply to Obj. 4). Leibniz pressed the point later, calling Hobbes a super-nominalist who made truth itself depend on the arbitrary will that fixes definitions; arithmetical truths, he observed, stay the same in decimal and duodecimal notation (SEP, citing Leibniz's 1670 preface to Nizolius). If truth is "the right ordering of names", and names are arbitrary, then truth looks arbitrary too. A second, narrower objection hits premise 5. SEP points out that Hobbes's ground for calling the phrase *incorporeal substance* insignificant is that the sentence *a substance is incorporeal* is false, and that this derives the insignificance from materialism, which is the point at issue. The claim that "Incorporeall Body" and "Incorporeall Substance" are "all one" is exactly what Descartes denies.

**Reply.** Partly Hobbes's, partly the literature's. Hobbes accepts that the choice of names is arbitrary but denies that the consequences are. Once "Man" and "Living Creature" have been given significations, whether the one signifies all that the other does is not up to anyone, and the reckoning that follows can be "a certain and infallible art" (Lev. 5.3). That is why Lev. 5.3 can say both that reason is always right and that individual reasoners err. The arbitrariness is in the counters, not in the sum. SEP adds two points on Hobbes's side. On his account of signification, an English sentence and its German translation have the same signification, which is what Descartes's Frenchman and German need; and it is far from clear, SEP says (citing Bolton 1977), that the arbitrariness Hobbes does accept, in where we draw the lines between kinds, has the consequences Descartes and Leibniz feared. On premise 5 the honest reply concedes the objection: the chapter 4 argument does beg the question. Hobbes's better argument is elsewhere, in the claim that philosophers infer that thought can exist without body because it can be considered without body, which SEP cites from *De Corpore* (1655, 3.4) and which *Leviathan* restates in chapter 46: "every part of the Universe, is Body, and that which is not Body, is no part of the Universe" (Lev. 46.15). Argument 3 takes up that charge against Descartes.

**Where scholars disagree.** (a) Is "reckoning" a theory of thinking or a metaphor? Leibniz took it up as a program, and John Haugeland (1985) called Hobbes the prophet of artificial intelligence. The SEP article is skeptical: Hobbes's sketches of syllogism as addition are left undeveloped, and the link to the computational theory of mind is a resemblance, not an anticipation. (b) Is Hobbes a conventionalist about truth or only about meaning? Leibniz read him as the first; Bolton, and Hungerland and Vick, cited in SEP, as the second. (c) How does language bear on the politics? Philip Pettit (*Made with Words*, 2008, cited in SEP) argues that language is what makes humans both capable of the commonwealth and prone to the conflict it cures, since words let us compare, promise and claim precedence. Quentin Skinner (*Reason and Rhetoric in the Philosophy of Hobbes*, 1996) traces the other side of chapter 4's attack on names that are "but insignificant sounds" (Lev. 4.21): Hobbes's early humanist training, his later suspicion of eloquence, and his turn in *Leviathan* back to rhetoric in support of science.

**Argument 3. Against the thinking substance: the Third Objections on the cogito (Obj. 2)**

*What it proves:* that Descartes's *I think, therefore I am* establishes that a thinking thing exists but nothing about what kind of thing it is, and that, if anything, what thinks should be taken to be a body.

**Text and setting.** Mersenne collected objections to the *Meditations* before publication, and Hobbes's, sixteen short objections with Descartes's even shorter replies, were printed in 1641 as the Third Set (SEP, which also records the earlier exchange of 1640 over Descartes's *Optics*). The French version names the author only as a celebrated English philosopher. The two men did not like each other, and the exchange shows it: Descartes answers most points in a few lines and several with open impatience. The second objection is the one aimed at the Second Meditation's move from *I exist* to *I am a thinking thing, that is, a mind*. Read it beside Meditation II itself and beside the [Descartes dossier](../rene-descartes/04-arguments.md)'s reconstruction of the cogito.

Hobbes's argument, in the order of the objection:

1. The cogito is granted: "I am a thing that thinks; quite correct". From the fact that I think it follows that I am, "since that which thinks is not nothing" (Obj. III, Obj. 2).
2. It does not follow that I am thought, or mind, or intellect: "I am exercising thought, hence I am thought" is no better than "I am walking; hence I am the walking" (Obj. III, Obj. 2).
3. All philosophers, Hobbes says, distinguish a subject from its faculties and activities, as the thing itself from its properties and essence. So a thing that thinks may be the subject of mind, reason or understanding, "and hence to be something corporeal; and the opposite of this has been assumed, not proved" (Obj. III, Obj. 2).
4. We know *I think* only because we can conceive no activity apart from its subject, no leaping apart from what leaps and no thinking without a thinker (Obj. III, Obj. 2).
5. The subjects of all activities can be conceived only as bodies, "as in material guise", as Descartes's own wax shows: the wax is the same matter under its changing qualities (Obj. III, Obj. 2).
6. We cannot know that we think by a further thought about our thinking, on pain of an infinite regress of knowing that we know (Obj. III, Obj. 2).

Therefore, since *I exist* depends on *I think*, and *I think* on our inability to separate thought from a matter that thinks, "the proper inference seems to be that that which thinks is material rather than immaterial" (Obj. III, Obj. 2). The fourth objection adds the positive picture from Argument 2: if reasoning joins names, names depend on imagination, and imagination perhaps, as Hobbes thinks, on the motion of the bodily organs, then the mind is motion in an organic body (Obj. III, Obj. 4).

**What each premise rests on.** Premise 2 is a grammatical point: an activity is not its own agent. Premise 3 is scholastic common ground, the distinction of substance from accident and power from act, which Hobbes uses against a philosopher who claimed to have left the schools behind. Premise 4 is shared with Descartes, and Descartes says so. Premise 5 is the weak point. It is Hobbes's materialism (Arguments 1 and 2), given as what "appears", with the wax as an illustration, not a proof. Premise 6 is Hobbes's own view that reflection on one's thinking is memory of it, which Descartes simply sets aside.

**Objection.** Descartes's replies are the standard objection, and on two points they are decisive. First, Hobbes has misread the word. By "mind" Descartes meant "not merely faculties, but rather what is endowed with the faculty of thinking" (Obj. III, reply to Obj. 2). There is no parity, he answers, between walking and thinking, because *walking* names only the act, while *thinking* is used now for the act, now for the faculty, and now for that in which the faculty exists (Obj. III, reply to Obj. 2). Second, Descartes denies the charge of assuming. He did not assume that the thinking thing is incorporeal; "I left it wholly undetermined until Meditation VI, in which its proof is given" (Obj. III, reply to Obj. 2). And he denies premise 5 outright. Hobbes adds, "wholly without any reason, and in opposition to the ordinary use of language and good Logic", that what thinks must be corporeal. The subjects of acts are substances, and may be said to wear the guise of "metaphysical matter", "but not on that account are they to be defined as bodies" (Obj. III, reply to Obj. 2). We know substances only through their acts, so we should name them by their acts, and acts such as understanding, willing and feeling "have no affinity with corporeal activities" (Obj. III, reply to Obj. 2). Whether the thinking thing is also a body is then a further question, answered in Meditation VI. On this objection, Hobbes's argument at best restates his materialism and at worst rests on a pun.

**Reply.** The literature's, on Hobbes's behalf. The objection concedes Hobbes's main point. Descartes agrees that the Second Meditation does not prove the mind incorporeal, and says the proof waits for the Sixth. That is exactly what Hobbes claimed: the cogito delivers a thinking *something*, and the step to an immaterial substance needs another argument. Read this way the objection is dialectically successful even though premise 5 is unsupported: it moves the whole weight of dualism onto Meditation VI. Hobbes did then answer Meditation VI, though not here. The SEP article reads the *gross errors* passage of *De Corpore* (1655, 3.4) as aimed at the Sixth Meditation's inference from conceiving mind apart from body to mind's existing apart from body; Hobbes's charge is that the ability to consider thinking without considering body does not show that thinking can be without a body. *Leviathan*'s claim that every part of the universe is body (Lev. 46.15) is the same position stated as metaphysics. The reply has its own weakness, which SEP also records: Descartes's premise is not that whatever can be conceived apart can exist apart, but that whatever can be *clearly and distinctly* conceived apart can, and Hobbes's criticism does not engage that qualification. The exchange therefore ends where many later ones do, with the materialist saying that conceivability proves nothing and the dualist saying that clear and distinct conceivability does.

**Where scholars disagree.** (a) How seriously to take the exchange. Descartes's curt replies have made many readers treat the Third Objections as a misfire between two philosophers talking past each other; SEP reports that they did not get on (citing Martinich's biography, 1999). Others treat the objections as the first statement of Hobbes's own system of mind, and read them as evidence that he was a materialist about the mind by 1641, years before *Leviathan* said so in print. The SEP article is more cautious, saying that Hobbes was convinced of human materialism by the time of *Leviathan* and *De Corpore*. (b) Whether premise 5 is a bad argument or a theory of concepts. On one reading Hobbes simply asserts that substances are bodies. On another, which draws on his account of imagination (Argument 1), his point is that we can form no image of a subject that is not an image of something extended, so *incorporeal substance* has no idea to stand for and is, in the language of Lev. 4.22, insignificant. Descartes's reply to the fifth objection, which turns on this, is that he takes the term idea to stand "for whatever the mind directly perceives", not only for images in the corporeal imagination (Obj. III, reply to Obj. 5). The dispute over whether thought requires images runs from this exchange through Locke and Berkeley. (c) The regress in premise 6 has been read both as a serious anticipation of later arguments against inner perception and as a slip, since knowing that one thinks need not be a second act of thinking about the first.

**Argument 4. The restless desire of power after power (Lev. 6, 10, 11)**

*What it proves:* that all human beings have, as a general inclination, "a perpetuall and restlesse desire of Power after power, that ceaseth onely in Death" (Lev. 11.2), and that this follows from what desire is, not from any special greed.

**Text and setting.** Chapter 6 gives the passions their mechanical basis, chapter 10 defines power and worth, and chapter 11 turns them into a thesis about human life. The argument sits between the theory of mind and the state of nature: chapter 13's three causes of quarrel draw on it, and its central sentence is one of the most quoted, and most misquoted, in the book. It is not the claim that every human being wants to dominate everyone else. It is the claim that no one can stop acquiring the means to future satisfaction, because present means cannot be made secure.

1. Voluntary motion begins in small motions within the body called endeavour; endeavour toward something is "APPETITE, or DESIRE", and endeavour away from it is aversion (Lev. 6.1–6.2).
2. Whatever a man desires "that is it, which he for his part calleth Good", and what he hates he calls evil. These words are "ever used with relation to the person that useth them: There being nothing simply and absolutely so" (Lev. 6.7).
3. The will is the last appetite in deliberation: "Will, therefore, Is The Last Appetite In Deliberating" (Lev. 6.53).
4. There is no final end of desire. "For there is no such Finis Ultimus, (utmost ayme,) nor Summum Bonum, (greatest good,) as is spoken of in the Books of the old Morall Philosophers", and "Felicity is a continuall progresse of the desire, from one object to another" (Lev. 11.1).
5. The object of desire is "not to enjoy once onely, and for one instant of time; but to assure for ever, the way of his future desire" (Lev. 11.1).
6. "The POWER of a Man, (to take it Universally,) is his present means, to obtain some future apparent Good" (Lev. 10.1).
7. Power is comparative. A man's worth is "his Price; that is to say, so much as would be given for the use of his Power", and "not the seller, but the buyer determines the Price" (Lev. 10.16). Power in this sense is "like to Fame, increasing as it proceeds" (Lev. 10.2).

Therefore there is "a perpetuall and restlesse desire of Power after power, that ceaseth onely in Death" (Lev. 11.2). Hobbes adds at once the reason, and the reason is the point: it is not always that a man hopes for a more intense delight, or "that he cannot be content with a moderate power: but because he cannot assure the power and means to live well, which he hath present, without the acquisition of more" (Lev. 11.2).

**What each premise rests on.** Premises 1 and 3 are the mechanics of Argument 1 applied to action; deliberation is an alternation of appetites, so beasts deliberate too (Lev. 6.51). Premise 2 is a subjectivism about value that Hobbes uses twice. Here it says that what is good is relative to the desirer; in the political chapters it says that, since there is no common measure in nature, a common measure must be set up, by "an Arbitrator or Judge" or by the person who represents the commonwealth (Lev. 6.7). Premise 4 is a deliberate rejection of Aristotle and the schools: life is motion, and a mind whose desires had ended would be as dead as one whose senses had stopped (Lev. 11.1). Premises 5 to 7 carry the argument. Desire looks to the future, power is the means to future goods, and power is measured against other people's power, so that no amount is enough once others may gain more.

**Objection.** The standard objection, made by Hobbes's contemporaries and repeated ever since, is that the argument slanders human nature: it generalizes from the ambitious to all, and it reduces all motives to self-interest. Clarendon's *Brief View and Survey* (1676) of *Leviathan* argued that Hobbes took his picture of mankind from the worst men; the moral-sense writers after him, Shaftesbury, Hutcheson and Butler, argued that benevolence and compassion are as natural as self-love (see [Shaftesbury, Hutcheson & Butler](../../eras/03-early-modern/moral-sense-school.md)). A sharper version is internal. Hobbes's own text says that the "Desire of Ease, and sensuall Delight" and the "Fear of Death, and Wounds" dispose men to obey a common power and so to stop competing (Lev. 11.4–11.5), and chapter 13 says that only "some" take pleasure in conquest beyond what security requires (Lev. 13.4). How then can the restless desire be a "generall inclination of all mankind"?

**Reply.** Hobbes's own, from the text. The restless desire is not a craving for more pleasure; the sentence says so. It is the rational response to insecurity of anyone who wants anything in the future, and it is restless because security in a world of other agents is never final. That answers the generalization charge: the moderate are drawn in by the immoderate, since "if others, that otherwise would be glad to be at ease within modest bounds, should not by invasion increase their power, they would not be able, long time, by standing only on their defence, to subsist" (Lev. 13.4). It also answers the internal objection. Desire of ease and fear of death incline men to obey a common power precisely because the common power is the only way to stop the race; the inclination to power after power is the condition the commonwealth exists to manage, and under a secure sovereign the acquisition can take the harmless forms Hobbes lists, wealth, knowledge and honor (Lev. 11). As for egoism, the argument does not need it. A person who desires the good of children, friends or God also needs power to secure it, and the same reasoning applies.

**Where scholars disagree.** Few passages carry so many readings. (a) Leo Strauss (*The Political Philosophy of Hobbes*, 1936) read the root of the desire as vanity, the passion for precedence, and Hobbes's moral basis as the opposition between vanity and the fear of violent death. (b) C. B. Macpherson (*The Political Theory of Possessive Individualism*, 1962) read the restless pursuit of power, with Lev. 10's market valuation of worth, as a portrait of man in a competitive market society, not of human nature as such. (c) The egoism question, which the IEP article calls the controversy that has dominated interpretation, divides commentators. Bernard Gert (*Hobbes and Psychological Egoism*, an article of 1967) argued that Hobbes is not a psychological egoist, pointing to the definitions of benevolence and pity in Lev. 6. Gregory Kavka (*Hobbesian Moral and Political Theory*, 1986) attributed to him a weaker *predominant egoism*, the view that self-interested motives usually win when they conflict with others, which is enough for the political argument. (d) S. A. Lloyd (*Ideals as Interests in Hobbes's Leviathan*, 1992) argued, in the terms of her publisher's summary, that *transcendent interests*, interests that override the fear of death, above all religious ones, are crucial to Hobbes's account of disorder; on her reading the dangerous desires are not for power at all but for salvation and righteousness, and the restless pursuit of power is only part of the story.

**Argument 5. Natural equality (Lev. 13)**

*What it proves:* that human beings are by nature equal in the only sense the political argument needs, so that no one can claim a benefit by nature that another may not claim as well, and that every one has equal hope of attaining their ends.

**Text and setting.** Chapter 13 opens with equality, and the order matters. The state of war (Argument 6) follows from equality, not from human wickedness, and the equality is the reason Hobbes's sovereign is made by agreement and not found in nature. Aristotle had held that some are by nature fit to rule and others to serve; Hobbes's claim is aimed at him, and at any argument from natural superiority to a natural right of command. The *Elements of Law* (EL I.14.1–2, printed in Molesworth vol. IV as *De Corpore Politico* I.1) and *De Cive* (I.3) make the same argument, and the *De Cive* version is the shortest.

1. People differ in strength of body and quickness of mind, but "when all is reckoned together, the difference between man, and man, is not so considerable, as that one man can thereupon claim to himselfe any benefit, to which another may not pretend, as well as he" (Lev. 13.1).
2. In body, "the weakest has strength enough to kill the strongest, either by secret machination, or by confederacy with others, that are in the same danger with himselfe" (Lev. 13.1).
3. In mind, the equality is greater still, once science is set aside as an acquired art that few have. Prudence is the faculty that matters, and "Prudence, is but Experience; which equall time, equally bestowes on all men, in those things they equally apply themselves unto" (Lev. 13.2).
4. What makes this equality hard to believe is "but a vain conceipt of ones owne wisdome", and that conceit itself counts for equality, since "there is not ordinarily a greater signe of the equall distribution of any thing, than that every man is contented with his share" (Lev. 13.2).

Therefore people are equal by nature in the relevant sense, and "From this equality of ability, ariseth equality of hope in the attaining of our Ends" (Lev. 13.3). *De Cive* reduces the argument to one line: "They are equals, who can do equal things" against one another, and those who can do the greatest thing, kill, can do equal things; so all are by nature equal, and "the inequality we now discern, hath its spring from the civil law" (*De Cive* I.3). The same sentence returns as the ninth law of nature in *Leviathan*: "The inequallity that now is, has been introduced by the Lawes civill" (Lev. 15).

**What each premise rests on.** Premise 2 rests on the body's fragility, which *De Cive* states directly: one need only consider "how easy a matter it is, even for the weakest man to kill the strongest" (*De Cive* I.3). It also rests on sleep and on alliances; the weak person need not win a fair fight, only find the strong person off guard or find friends. Premise 3 rests on Hobbes's account of prudence as accumulated experience, which chapters 3 and 8 set out; science, being an art grounded on words (Argument 2), is excluded from the comparison as rare and acquired. Premise 4 is a joke with a point. Hobbes knows that people's satisfaction with their own wit is no evidence of equal wit, and the sentence is ironic. The serious point is the one the ninth law draws: whether or not nature has made people equal, people think themselves equal, and that belief is a fact the political argument has to take as given.

**Objection.** The obvious objection is that the premises are false or irrelevant. They are false if taken as a claim of equal ability: people differ greatly in strength, health, skill and intelligence, and the weakest do not in fact stand a fair chance against an armed band. They are irrelevant if taken as a claim of equal vulnerability: everyone can be killed, but this does not give everyone equal hope of attaining their ends, because the strong and the well-allied can still expect to win most contests. A sharper version says that equality of vulnerability actually cuts against Hobbes's later argument. If people were very unequal, the weak would submit to the strong and there would be peace by domination, as in a family or a conquered province; Hobbes's war needs enough equality to make everyone a threat, but a world in which individuals band into confederacies is already a world of groups, not equals. The SEP article on Hobbes's moral and political philosophy (Lloyd and Sreedhar) notes the related point that families and other groups ordered by internal obligations, and not only isolated individuals, would be the primitive units of a state of nature.

**Reply.** Hobbes's own, from the text, with the literature's gloss. First, the argument needs only rough equality, and Hobbes says so: the differences are not "so considerable" that anyone can claim a benefit others may not claim. No one is so strong that they cannot be killed asleep, so no one can count on security by strength alone; that is the reading the SEP article gives, which says that no one is invulnerable nor can expect to dominate the others. Second, the claim about groups strengthens Hobbes more than it weakens him. The SEP article observes that competition, diffidence and glory are just as dangerous among small groups as among individuals, and Hobbes himself extends the war to sovereigns (Argument 6). Third, the ninth law changes the status of the claim. "If Nature therefore have made men equall, that equalitie is to be acknowledged; or if Nature have made men unequall; yet because men that think themselves equall, will not enter into conditions of Peace, but upon Equall termes, such equalitie must be admitted" (Lev. 15). The argument is thus partly descriptive (rough equality of vulnerability) and partly practical: even a naturally superior few could not get the many to accept peace on unequal terms. Aristotle's natural rulers are dismissed on both counts, as "not only against reason; but also against experience" (Lev. 15).

**Where scholars disagree.** (a) What kind of equality? Most readers, including Gregory Kavka (*Hobbesian Moral and Political Theory*, 1986) and the SEP article, take it as rough equality of vulnerability, enough to make domination uncertain. Kinch Hoekstra ("Hobbesian Equality", in S. A. Lloyd, ed., *Hobbes Today*, 2012; cited from memory, unchecked) has argued that the claim is as much a political recommendation as an observation: Hobbes asserts equality because the assertion serves peace, which is the logic of the ninth law. (b) Equality and women. The SEP article stresses that Hobbes was among the first Western philosophers to count women as persons in a social contract: people are equal because all are subject to domination and all can dominate, so women are naturally equal to men and their consent is needed for anyone's authority over them. Gabriella Slomp ("Hobbes and the Equality of Women", 1994, listed in SEP) and feminist critics, also reported in SEP, ask how this squares with Hobbes's later acceptance of fathers' dominion in most commonwealths (Lev. 20); that question belongs to Argument 13. (c) Is equality a premise or a conclusion? On a reading that draws on Leo Strauss's stress on vanity (1936; see Argument 4), the real premise of chapter 13 is the vain overestimation of one's own wisdom, so that equality matters chiefly because people refuse to admit it. On the more common reading the vanity is one cause of quarrel among others (Argument 6), and the equality premise does its work without it.

**Argument 6. The state of nature is a war of all against all: the three causes of quarrel (Lev. 13)**

*What it proves:* that wherever people live without a common power able to overawe them all, they are in a condition of war of every one against every one, in which there is no industry, no society and no justice, and life is miserable.

**Text and setting.** This is the argument the whole of *Leviathan* exists to answer, and the study guide reconstructs it in its own section (a). The version here follows chapter 13 paragraph by paragraph, so that each step can be located. Hobbes calls it an "Inference, made from the Passions", and then offers to confirm it "by Experience" (Lev. 13.10). The famous Latin tag for its conclusion, *bellum omnium contra omnes*, does not occur in the English *Leviathan*; the 1668 Latin *Leviathan* uses it in chapters 13 and 14, and the 1651 English *De Cive* has the English form, "a mere war of all against all" (*De Cive* Preface).

1. From equality of ability comes equality of hope; so if two people desire the same thing which both cannot enjoy, "they become enemies", and each endeavors to destroy or subdue the other (Lev. 13.3).
2. Since anyone who has something worth taking may expect others "to come prepared with forces united, to dispossesse, and deprive him, not only of the fruit of his labour, but also of his life, or liberty", there is general diffidence, that is, distrust (Lev. 13.3).
3. Given diffidence, "there is no way for any man to secure himselfe, so reasonable, as Anticipation", mastering by force or wiles all the people one can until no other power is great enough to endanger one (Lev. 13.4).
4. Because some take pleasure in conquest beyond what security needs, even those content with moderate means must increase their power or perish (Lev. 13.4; see Argument 4).
5. Each wants others to value them as they value themselves, and responds to signs of contempt by trying to extort a greater value "by dommage" (Lev. 13.5).
6. "So that in the nature of man, we find three principall causes of quarrel. First, Competition; Secondly, Diffidence; Thirdly, Glory" (Lev. 13.6). The first makes people invade for gain, the second for safety, the third for reputation (Lev. 13.7).
7. War is not fighting but a disposition to fight: it consists "in a tract of time, wherein the Will to contend by Battell is sufficiently known", as foul weather consists not in a shower or two but "in an inclination thereto of many dayes together" (Lev. 13.8).

Therefore "during the time men live without a common Power to keep them all in awe, they are in that condition which is called Warre; and such a warre, as is of every man, against every man" (Lev. 13.8). In that condition "there is no place for Industry; because the fruit thereof is uncertain", nor agriculture, navigation, building, knowledge, arts, letters or society, and, worst of all, "continuall feare, and danger of violent death; And the life of man, solitary, poore, nasty, brutish, and short" (Lev. 13.9). Nor is anything unjust: "Where there is no common Power, there is no Law: where no Law, no Injustice" (Lev. 13.13).

**What each premise rests on.** Premises 1 and 2 rest on Argument 5 and on scarcity, which Hobbes assumes rather than states: the things people want are not enough for all. Premise 3 is the decisive one, and it is a claim about rational choice under uncertainty. One cannot tell the peaceful from the predatory, so the safe policy is to treat everyone as a possible attacker and strike first. Premise 4 brings in Argument 4's restless desire of power, and premise 5 the passion for esteem that chapter 10 analyzed as part of power. Premise 7 is a definition, and it does a great deal of work: it means the war of all against all needs no constant fighting, only the absence of assurance. Hobbes then gives the evidence of experience. A traveler arms himself, a householder locks his doors and his chests, even where there are laws and armed officers, and so "accuse mankind by his actions, as I do by my words" (Lev. 13.10). He offers three examples of the condition itself: "the savage people in many places of America", the way people under a peaceful government "degenerate into, in a civill Warre" (Lev. 13.11), and sovereigns, who are always "in the state and posture of Gladiators" toward one another (Lev. 13.12).

**Objection.** Three objections are standard. The historical one, pressed by Locke and many after him, says that there never was such a condition, and that families and tribes have always had internal government; the SEP article reports Locke's view that the state of nature is preferable to an absolute sovereign. The psychological one, pressed by Rousseau and later by Butler and Hume (from memory; unchecked), says that Hobbes has read into natural man the passions of men already formed by society, such as vanity and the desire for precedence. The sharpest is Jean Hampton's dilemma (*Hobbes and the Social Contract Tradition*, 1986, as summarized in SEP's article on contractarianism). Either the war comes from the passions, greed and fear in particular, or from rationality, as in a prisoner's dilemma in which each rationally breaks agreements. If from the passions, the same passions will survive the social contract and break it. If from rationality, rational agents will not comply with the contract that sets up the sovereign any more than with other agreements. Either way the argument for war is too strong to allow the escape the rest of the book describes.

**Reply.** Hobbes's own answer to the first objection is in the text: he grants that the condition was "never generally so, over all the world" (Lev. 13.11), and argues that it does not need to have been. The state of nature is what follows wherever the common power is absent or fails, and civil war shows it. The second objection Hobbes partly accepts. He does not say all people are vain, only that "some" are (Lev. 13.4), and he insists that the passions themselves are "no Sin" (Lev. 13.10). The argument needs only that the vain and the predatory are numerous enough and hard enough to identify that everyone must guard against them. Hampton's dilemma has no reply in Hobbes. The literature's best answer, developed in different forms by Kavka (1986) and other game-theoretic readers (characterization from memory), is that the war arises from the combination of rational anticipation with partly irrational passions and with disagreement in judgment, and that what the sovereign changes is not human nature but the situation. Once there is a common power, anticipation is no longer rational, because "the bonds of words" are backed by "the feare of some coerceive Power" (Lev. 14.19). The passions remain but lose their occasion. Whether the people who set up the sovereign can trust each other long enough to do it is the remaining difficulty, taken up in Arguments 8 and 9.

**Where scholars disagree.** What, exactly, makes the state of nature a war? The SEP article sets out three answers, and each has its defenders. (a) Rationality: people are fully rational but trapped in a situation, often modeled as a prisoner's dilemma, where what is rational for each is bad for all. David Gauthier (*The Logic of Leviathan*, 1969) and Kavka (1986) are the leading game-theoretic readers (both listed in SEP's bibliography; the characterization of Gauthier is from memory). (b) Passions: the war arises from those whose passions overrule their judgment, the vainglorious above all. This line runs back to Strauss (1936) and is the one Hampton's dilemma calls the passions account. (c) Judgment: the IEP article on Hobbes's moral and political philosophy emphasizes that people in the state of nature are not without moral ideas but disagree in their judgments, and each is judge in their own cause. On this reading, which fits S. A. Lloyd's emphasis on religious and other transcendent interests (*Ideals as Interests*, 1992; see Argument 4), Hobbes has the English civil war in mind throughout, and the problem is not the absence of morality but the absence of a common judge. The study guide's reconstruction of this argument lists the three causes as separate premises; the readings above differ over which of them carries the weight.

**Argument 7. The right of nature and the first law of nature (Lev. 14)**

*What it proves:* that in the state of nature each person has a right to do whatever they judge necessary to preserve their life, so that each has a right to every thing; that this unlimited right makes everyone insecure; and that reason therefore delivers a general rule, the first law of nature, to seek peace where it can be had and to defend oneself by any means where it cannot.

**Text and setting.** Chapter 13 ended with the claim that the passions that incline people to peace are fear of death, desire of commodious living and hope to obtain it by industry, and that "Reason suggesteth convenient Articles of Peace" (Lev. 13.14). Chapter 14 opens with three definitions and two derivations, all in five paragraphs. The study guide reconstructs the passage from the laws of nature to covenant as one argument (its section b); here the first step is taken by itself, because the distinction between right and law it relies on is the hinge of Hobbes's moral theory.

1. "The RIGHT OF NATURE, which Writers commonly call Jus Naturale, is the Liberty each man hath, to use his own power, as he will himselfe, for the preservation of his own Nature; that is to say, of his own Life", and so of doing anything which "in his own Judgement, and Reason" he conceives to be the aptest means to it (Lev. 14.1).
2. Liberty is "the absence of externall Impediments" (Lev. 14.2).
3. "A LAW OF NATURE, (Lex Naturalis,) is a Precept, or generall Rule, found out by Reason, by which a man is forbidden to do, that, which is destructive of his life, or taketh away the means of preserving the same" (Lev. 14.3).
4. Right and law must be distinguished, "because RIGHT, consisteth in liberty to do, or to forbeare; Whereas LAW, determineth, and bindeth to one of them", so that they differ "as Obligation, and Liberty; which in one and the same matter are inconsistent" (Lev. 14.3).
5. In the condition of war, each is governed by their own reason, and "there is nothing he can make use of, that may not be a help unto him, in preserving his life against his enemyes" (Lev. 14.4).
6. Therefore "every man has a Right to every thing; even to one anothers body", and while that right lasts "there can be no security to any man, (how strong or wise soever he be,)" of living out his natural span (Lev. 14.4).

Therefore it is a precept or general rule of reason "That every man, ought to endeavour Peace, as farre as he has hope of obtaining it; and when he cannot obtain it, that he may seek, and use, all helps, and advantages of Warre" (Lev. 14.5). The first branch is the first and fundamental law of nature, "To seek Peace, and follow it"; the second is "the summe of the Right of Nature", "By all means we can, to defend our selves" (Lev. 14.5).

**What each premise rests on.** Premise 1 rests on the claim of chapters 6 and 11 that every voluntary act aims at some good to the agent, and on the claim of chapter 13 that death is the greatest of natural evils; the right of nature is the permission reason gives to the one end no one can be expected to forgo. Premise 2 is Hobbes's mechanical definition of liberty, which returns in chapter 21 and in the Bramhall debate (Argument 16). Premises 3 and 4 make the key move. A right is a liberty, the absence of an obligation, not a claim that others must respect, so two people can each have a right to the same thing. Premises 5 and 6 then follow from the state of war. The *Elements of Law* draws the consequence more bluntly: "that right of all men to all things, is in effect no better than if no man had right to any thing" (EL I.14.10, printed in Molesworth vol. IV as *De Corpore Politico* I.1.10). The first law of nature combines the two halves: reason forbids what destroys life, the state of war destroys life, so reason directs us to peace, while still permitting war where peace is not to be had.

**Objection.** Two objections are standard. The first is that the right to everything is incoherent or empty. A right to everything that everyone else equally has, and that imposes no duty on anyone, is not a right in any useful sense; and the clause "in his own Judgement" seems to make the right limitless, since anything at all can be judged necessary for preservation. The second, and deeper, is about the word "law". A precept "found out by Reason" that forbids what is destructive of one's life looks like a rule of prudence, a hypothetical imperative of the form *if you want to live, seek peace*. Such a rule does not oblige in the sense in which law obliges, and it seems to make morality a matter of self-interest. If the first law is only prudence, then the person who prefers glory or salvation to life, or who simply miscalculates, has broken no law, only made a mistake.

**Reply.** To the first objection, Hobbes's own reply is that he agrees. The emptiness of the right is his point: since the right to everything gives no security, it gives reason its motive to seek peace. The judgment clause is also limited. The right covers what one sincerely judges necessary for preservation, and the SEP article notes that this right, limited in theory, becomes in practice a right to all things only because almost anything might be so judged in a condition of war. To the second objection, Hobbes's reply is at the end of chapter 15 and is the subject of Argument 10. These dictates of reason, he says there, are called laws "improperly", since they are theorems about what conduces to self-preservation, and are properly laws only when considered as the word of God, who commands all things by right (Lev. 15.41). Hobbes thus concedes that, considered by themselves, they are precepts of reason, and holds that they are also, and properly, God's laws. Whether the first consideration or the second does the obliging is the dispute below.

**Where scholars disagree.** What kind of "ought" is the first law of nature? The SEP article says that commentators do not agree whether the laws of nature are mere precepts of prudence, divine commands, or moral imperatives of some other sort; the IEP article makes the same division. (a) The divine-command reading: A. E. Taylor ("The Ethical Doctrine of Hobbes", 1938, reprinted in Brown, ed., *Hobbes Studies*, 1965, which SEP lists; the date is from memory) and Howard Warrender (*The Political Philosophy of Hobbes: His Theory of Obligation*, 1957) argued that the laws oblige because they are God's commands, so that Hobbes's ethics is not egoistic at all; F. C. Hood (*The Divine Politics of Thomas Hobbes*, 1964) and A. P. Martinich (*The Two Gods of Leviathan*, 1992) give theological readings of the same kind. (b) The prudential reading: Thomas Nagel ("Hobbes's Concept of Obligation", 1959), J. W. N. Watkins (*Hobbes's System of Ideas*, 1965) and David Gauthier (1969) read the laws as rational counsels of self-preservation, and treat the talk of God as a supplementary sanction or a concession to readers. (c) A third reading, taken by S. A. Lloyd and stated in the SEP article she co-wrote, finds in the laws of nature a principle of reciprocity, summed up in the rule not to treat others in ways we would not have them treat us, which binds reasoners as such and not only the prudent (Lloyd's own book on the point, *Morality in the Philosophy of Thomas Hobbes*, 2009, is cited from memory). The question also divides readers on the right of nature: Leo Strauss (1936) argued that Hobbes made right, not law or duty, the basis of morality and so founded modern natural-rights theory, and many later historians, Richard Tuck among them (*Natural Rights Theories*, 1979, listed in SEP; the summary of his reading is from memory), place Hobbes in a line of rights theorists running back through Grotius.

**Argument 8. Laying down rights, covenants, and the rights no one can give up (Lev. 14)**

*What it proves:* that reason requires each person to lay down the right to all things on condition that others do the same; that a right is laid down by a voluntary sign, after which hindering its beneficiary is injustice; that a covenant of mutual trust is void in the state of nature on reasonable suspicion but binding under a power that enforces it; and that some rights, above all the right to resist force against one's life, cannot be laid down by any sign.

**Text and setting.** The rest of chapter 14, from the second law of nature onward, is Hobbes's theory of contract. Most of it reads like a law manual: transfer and delivery, express and inferred signs, gifts, promises, merit, oaths. The argument underneath it is the bridge from the first law of nature to the commonwealth. It shows how a right can be given up, what obligation is, why the obligation is fragile without a sovereign, and what the sovereign can never be given. Two later arguments depend on it: the reply to the Fool (Argument 9) and the rights subjects keep against the sovereign (Argument 14).

1. From the first law follows the second: "That a man be willing, when others are so too, as farre-forth, as for Peace, and defence of himselfe he shall think it necessary, to lay down this right to all things; and be contented with so much liberty against other men, as he would allow other men against himselfe" (Lev. 14.6).
2. The willingness is conditional, because if others will not lay down their right, to lay down one's own would be "to expose himselfe to Prey, (which no man is bound to)" (Lev. 14.6).
3. To lay down a right is to divest oneself of the liberty of hindering another's use of his own right to the same thing; it gives the other no new right, but only removes an impediment (Lev. 14.7).
4. A right is laid down by renouncing or by transferring it, through "some voluntary and sufficient signe, or signes", and the one who has done so is "OBLIGED, or BOUND" not to hinder the beneficiary. To do so is "INJUSTICE, and INJURY", which is like the absurdity of contradicting what one maintained at the beginning (Lev. 14.8).
5. Transferring a right is a voluntary act, and "of the voluntary acts of every man, the object is some Good To Himselfe"; so "a man cannot lay down the right of resisting them, that assault him by force, to take away his life", nor of resisting wounds, chains and imprisonment (Lev. 14.9).
6. A contract is a mutual transfer of right; a covenant is a contract in which at least one party is trusted to perform later (Lev. 14.10, 14.12).
7. In the condition of mere nature, a covenant in which neither party performs at once is void "upon any reasonable suspition", because "the bonds of words are too weak to bridle mens ambition, avarice, anger, and other Passions, without the feare of some coerceive Power"; whoever performs first "does but betray himselfe to his enemy" (Lev. 14.19). Under a power set up to constrain covenant-breakers, the fear is no longer reasonable, and the first performer is obliged (Lev. 14.20).

Therefore people can bind themselves by covenant, but in the state of nature such bonds hold only where a party has already performed; a common power is needed to make covenants of mutual trust safe to keep; and whatever covenant sets up that power cannot include the right to resist force against one's own life. Hence "A Covenant not to defend my selfe from force, by force, is alwayes voyd" (Lev. 14.30), and the same is true of a covenant to accuse oneself without assurance of pardon (Lev. 14.31).

**What each premise rests on.** Premise 1 is presented as derived from the first law: if peace is to be sought, and peace is impossible while each keeps the right to everything, then the right must be laid down so far as peace requires. The condition "when others are so too" comes from the right of nature, which the first law preserves in its second branch. Premise 3 is a consequence of Argument 7's definition of right as liberty: since everyone already had a right to everything, laying down a right cannot give anyone more right, only fewer obstacles. Premise 4 contains Hobbes's most original definition, that injustice is a practical self-contradiction, "voluntarily to undo that, which from the beginning he had voluntarily done" (Lev. 14.8). It also contains the admission that words alone do not hold: the bonds "have their strength, not from their own Nature, (for nothing is more easily broken then a mans word,) but from Feare of some evill consequence upon the rupture" (Lev. 14.8). Premise 5 rests on chapter 6's psychology of the will (Argument 4) and on a rule of interpretation: a sign that seems to give up the right to life is to be read as ignorance of what the words mean, not as an act of will (Lev. 14.9). Hobbes adds the evidence of practice: criminals are led to execution "with armed men, notwithstanding that such Criminals have consented to the Law, by which they are condemned" (Lev. 14.30).

**Objection.** Three objections go to the core. (a) The assurance problem. If no covenant of mutual trust is safe to keep in the state of nature, how can the covenant that sets up the sovereign be made? It is itself a covenant among people none of whom has yet performed, so by premise 7 it should be void on the first reasonable suspicion. (b) Injustice as absurdity. A logical contradiction is between propositions; breaking a promise is an action. To call it absurd either confuses the two or makes obligation depend on nothing more than consistency, so that one who openly repudiated a past promise would do no wrong. (c) The inalienable right. If no one can give up the right to resist force against their life, every subject keeps a private judgment about when their life is threatened, and the absolute sovereign of chapter 18 rests on a covenant that has a hole in it. Bishop Bramhall, as the SEP article reports, called *Leviathan* a "Rebell’s Catechism" on this ground, and later critics have called the limits of obligation the Achilles' heel of the theory.

**Reply.** Partly Hobbes's, partly the literature's. To (a), Hobbes's answer is the structure of the covenant that institutes the sovereign: all covenant at once with all, and what each gives up is a right that the sovereign then has the power to make others give up as well. Hobbes also says that the fear that voids a covenant "must be alwayes something arising after the Covenant made", some new fact or sign of the will not to perform (Lev. 14.21); the general suspicion of the state of nature, which everyone knew when they covenanted, does not void the founding covenant. That answer is the subject of Arguments 9 and 11. To (b), the reply is that Hobbes offers the comparison with absurdity as an analogy ("somewhat like to that"), not as a definition, and that the obligation itself comes from the laws of nature (Argument 7) and, in a commonwealth, from the sovereign's enforcement. To (c), Hobbes accepts the consequence and tries to limit it. The retained right is narrow and defensive: it permits resisting the officers who come to kill or imprison one, not judging the sovereign's laws, and the sovereign keeps the right to punish. Susanne Sreedhar (*Hobbes on Resistance*, 2010, and "Defending the Hobbesian Right of Self-Defense", 2008, both listed in SEP) has argued that the inalienable rights are consistent with the absolute sovereign and are in fact required by Hobbes's account of what a covenant can contain.

**Where scholars disagree.** (a) What is given up in the founding covenant? Jean Hampton (1986), as SEP's contractarianism article reports, calls Hobbes's contract an *alienation contract*, in which people alienate their rights to judge their own disputes and to defend themselves; she contrasts it with an *agency* model in which the sovereign is only lent power, and argues that Hobbes's premises, including the inalienable right of self-defense, push him toward the agency model he rejects (the second half of this summary is from memory). (b) Does the right to resist extend beyond the moment of attack? The study guide and the library entry treat it as narrow; the SEP article notes that Hobbes also gives subjects seemingly broad resistance rights where family or honor are at stake, and that if each judges the adequacy of protection for herself, it can seem that people never really leave the state of nature. (c) Are covenants extorted by fear really covenants? Hobbes says they are (Lev. 14.28), and the IEP article counts this among the weak steps in his case for obedience; Argument 13 takes up the dispute.

**Argument 9. The reply to the Fool (Lev. 15)**

*What it proves:* that keeping a valid covenant is never against reason, even for someone who cares only for his own preservation and contentment, and so that justice is a law of nature and not just a useful name.

The setting is the start of chapter 15. The third law of nature says "That Men Performe Their Covenants Made", and justice is defined as the keeping of covenant (Lev. 15.1–2). Hobbes then lets an objector speak. "The Foole hath sayd in his heart, there is no such thing as Justice" (Lev. 15.4): the echo is Psalm 14, whose fool says there is no God. Hobbes's Fool does not deny that covenants exist, or that breaking them is called injustice. He denies that breaking them is always irrational. Since each man's preservation is left to his own care, making or keeping covenants "was not against Reason, when it conduced to ones benefit" (Lev. 15.4). His showcase is the usurper: a crown won by breach of faith seems to pay. Hobbes answers: "This specious reasoning is nevertheless false" (Lev. 15.4).

Hobbes first narrows the case. He is not talking about bare mutual promises in the state of nature, which chapter 14 has already declared void "upon any reasonable suspition" (Lev. 14.19), because "he which performeth first, does but betray himselfe to his enemy" (Lev. 14.19). The question is the second performer's: the case where "one of the parties has performed already; or where there is a Power to make him performe" (Lev. 15.5). Should he now perform too?

1. An action is reasonable or not by what can be foreseen and reckoned on when it is chosen, not by how it happens to turn out. If an act tends to one's destruction, a lucky accident that makes it pay does not make it "reasonably or wisely done" (Lev. 15.5).
2. In the condition of war no one can defend himself by his own strength or wit "without the help of Confederates" (Lev. 15.5).
3. Whoever breaks a covenant declares that he thinks breach reasonable, and so declares that those who help him cannot rely on him.
4. Such a man "cannot be received into any Society, that unite themselves for Peace and defence, but by the errour of them that receive him" (Lev. 15.5); and once received, he is kept only until they see their mistake.
5. Other men's errors are something "a man cannot reasonably reckon upon as the means of his security" (Lev. 15.5). So the breaker's safety rests on what he can neither foresee nor count on.
6. By premise 1, acting on such a policy is against the reason of his own preservation.
Therefore keeping covenant, once the other side has performed or a power stands over both, is not against reason: "Justice therefore, that is to say, Keeping of Covenant, is a Rule of Reason, by which we are forbidden to do any thing destructive to our life; and consequently a Law of Nature" (Lev. 15.7).

Two short paragraphs close off the Fool's examples. Heaven cannot be won by breach, "there being but one way imaginable; and that is not breaking, but keeping of Covenant" (Lev. 15.6). The successful rebel is still irrational, because success "cannot reasonably be expected", and because by gaining a throne that way "others are taught to gain the same in like manner" (Lev. 15.7). The second point matters: the usurper sets a precedent that will be used against him.

**What each premise rests on.** Premise 1 is Hobbes's account of deliberation as reckoning consequences (Lev. 5–6; see Argument 2): reason judges by expectation, not outcome. Premise 2 is the natural equality of Lev. 13 (Argument 5): no one is strong enough to go it alone. Premises 3 and 4 depend on breach being visible, or at least discoverable, and on breach being read as a declaration of policy, not a one-off act. Premise 5 is a claim about the limits of prudent calculation.

**Textual basis.** Lev. 15.1–7; Lev. 14.19 on covenants of mutual trust and the first performer; compare *De Cive* ch. 3, the parallel chapter on the other laws of nature. The study guide's chapter-15 section gives the same reply in summary and calls its success "a live question" ([study guide](../../study/hobbes-leviathan.md)).

**Objection.** The standard objection is that the reply works only against the careless knave. Hobbes needs breach to be detected, or at least to be expected to be detected. A clever Fool breaks faith only when breach will stay secret, or when he is so placed that the confederacy cannot cast him out. Against him, premises 3 and 4 fail: a secret breach declares nothing. This is the shape of Hume's later "sensible knave" (*Enquiry concerning the Principles of Morals*, Conclusion), who keeps the general rule and takes the exceptions. A second objection comes from Jean Hampton (*Hobbes and the Social Contract Tradition*, 1986). If Hobbes's agents are rational enough to see that keeping covenants pays, they could keep covenants in the state of nature too, and the war of all against all would not follow. If they are not, they cannot make the covenant that sets up the sovereign. Either the reply to the Fool succeeds too well, or the escape from the state of nature fails.

**Reply.** Partly Hobbes's, partly the literature's. Hobbes's own resources are premise 1 and the stakes. The Fool's gamble risks expulsion from the only thing that keeps him alive, and Hobbes holds that it is irrational to stake one's life on a bet one cannot assess, however good the odds look. Gregory Kavka (*Hobbesian Moral and Political Theory*, 1986) rebuilt the reply in decision-theoretic terms: when the possible loss is catastrophic, the gain modest, and the probability of detection hard to estimate, a prudent agent adopts the rule of keeping covenants and does not reopen it case by case. David Gauthier went further. In "Three against Justice: The Foole, the Sensible Knave, and the Lydian Shepherd" (collected in *Moral Dealing*, 1990, pp. 129–149, checked at the De Gruyter catalogue record) he takes the Fool, Hume's knave and Plato's Gyges as one challenge, and answers it with the theory of his *Morals by Agreement* (1986): a constrained maximizer, disposed to keep agreements with others so disposed, does better over a life than a straightforward maximizer, because only he is admitted to cooperation. Against Hampton, a Kavka-style answer is that the reply covers only the second performer, as Hobbes says, so the first-performer problem of the state of nature still stands. The sovereign's job is to turn every covenant into a second-performer case.

**Where scholars disagree.** The dispute is over what the reply is. One reading, Kavka's and Gauthier's in different forms, takes it as an argument about rational policy under uncertainty, and asks whether it is good decision theory. Kinch Hoekstra ("Hobbes and the Foole", *Political Theory* 25, 1997, pp. 620–654, checked at PhilPapers) challenges what he calls the usual interpretation, on which Hobbes holds that it is never reasonable to think injustice will pay. On his reading the reply is narrower and turns on what it is reasonable to count on, and the Fool's claim about reason, not his prospects, is the target. S. A. Lloyd (*Morality in the Philosophy of Thomas Hobbes*, 2009, checked in the SEP bibliography) reads the laws of nature through a reciprocity principle, so that the Fool's fault is that he claims an exception he would not grant to others; on that reading the reply is a point about justifying oneself to others, not only about self-interest. Patricia Springborg ("Hobbes's Fool the Insipiens, and the Tyrant-King", *Political Theory* 39, 2011, checked in the SEP bibliography) reads the Fool historically, as the psalmist's *insipiens* and as a figure for the tyrant who seizes a throne. Whether the reply needs the fear of God is a further question. Hobbes brackets it: the Fool takes away "the feare of God" (Lev. 15.4), and the reply is meant to work without it. Whether Hobbes's laws of nature can bind at all without God is the subject of Argument 10.

**Argument 10. Laws of nature as theorems: are they laws, and whose? (Lev. 15 end; Lev. 26)**

*What it proves:* that the "laws of nature" are, in themselves, conclusions of reason about what preserves us, and that they become laws in the proper sense only when considered as commanded: by God, who has right to command all things, or by the civil sovereign, once a commonwealth exists. What the argument leaves open is which of these two sources the obligation finally rests on, and that is the question this section is about.

The setting is the last paragraph of chapter 15. Hobbes has listed nineteen laws of nature, from seeking peace to the rule that judges must be impartial, and has said that their science "is the true and onely Moral Philosophy" (Lev. 15.40). Then he qualifies the name:

> These dictates of Reason, men use to call by the name of Lawes; but improperly: for they are but Conclusions, or Theoremes concerning what conduceth to the conservation and defence of themselves; whereas Law, properly is the word of him, that by right hath command over others. But yet if we consider the same Theoremes, as delivered in the word of God, that by right commandeth all things; then are they properly called Lawes. (Lev. 15.41)

The argument implicit in the passage, with the support Hobbes gives it elsewhere, runs:

1. Law, properly, "is not Counsell, but Command" (Lev. 26), the word of one who has a right to command those already obliged to obey him.
2. The laws of nature, as reason finds them, are theorems: general truths of the form *peace, and the means to it, preserve those who seek it*. A theorem, as such, is no one's command.
3. So, considered only as dictates of reason, the laws of nature are called laws "improperly" (Lev. 15.41).
4. God "by right commandeth all things", and he declares his laws three ways: "by the Dictates of Naturall Reason, By Revelation, and by the Voyce of some Man" (Lev. 31). His right to reign rests not on creation or gratitude but on "his Irresistible Power" (Lev. 31).
5. The same theorems, taken as God's word delivered through natural reason, are therefore laws properly so called (Lev. 15.41).
6. In a commonwealth the sovereign commands them too. In the state of nature, "the Lawes of Nature, which consist in Equity, Justice, Gratitude, and other morall Vertues" are "not properly Lawes, but qualities that dispose men to peace, and to obedience"; once a commonwealth is settled, "then are they actually Lawes, and not before" (Lev. 26).
Therefore "The Law of Nature, and the Civill Law, contain each other, and are of equall extent" (Lev. 26): the laws of nature become binding law through a commander, and obedience to the civil law is itself a dictate of the law of nature.

Alongside this sits the definition at the start of chapter 14: "A LAW OF NATURE, (Lex Naturalis,) is a Precept, or generall Rule, found out by Reason" forbidding what is destructive of one's life (Lev. 14.3). Hobbes insists there that law and right "differ as much, as Obligation, and Liberty" (Lev. 14.3). And chapter 15 limits how the laws bind: "The Lawes of Nature oblige In Foro Interno; that is to say, they bind to a desire they should take place: but In Foro Externo; that is, to the putting them in act, not alwayes" (Lev. 15.36). Keeping them when no one else does would make one "a prey to others" (Lev. 15.36).

**What each premise rests on.** Premise 1 is the command theory of law that Hobbes states in Lev. 26 and that legal positivists later took up. Premise 2 rests on his idea of science as reasoning from definitions to consequences (Lev. 5; Argument 2). Premise 4 rests on the theology of Lev. 31, which grounds God's kingdom by nature in power, not in benefits. Premise 6 rests on the theory of the sovereign as sole interpreter and enforcer.

**Textual basis.** Lev. 14.3 (definition of a law of nature; law versus right); 15.36–41 (*in foro interno*; the laws eternal, easy, and the true moral philosophy; the theorems passage); 26 (law as command; the fourth of the chapter's numbered points, on the mutual containment of natural and civil law); 31 (God's natural kingdom, the threefold word of God). *De Cive* ch. 3 ends with a parallel remark that the laws of nature, as delivered by God, are properly laws. The library entry states the question this argument raises: are laws of nature "mere prudential advice, or also God's commands that genuinely oblige?" ([Thomas Hobbes](../../eras/03-early-modern/thomas-hobbes.md)).

**Objection.** The objection comes from both sides at once. From the natural-law side, Richard Cumberland (*De Legibus Naturae*, 1672) answered Hobbes that the laws of nature are genuine laws because God promulgates them through the nature of things and attaches natural rewards and punishments to them, and that their content is the common good of all rational beings, not each one's preservation. On that view Hobbes has reduced law to advice and then tried to buy it back with a sentence about God. From the secular side the complaint is the reverse. If the laws bind only as God's commands, and God's right rests only on irresistible power, then obligation is just the prudence of the weak toward the strong, and the "theorems" were all there was to Hobbes's ethics. Either way, premise 5 looks like an afterthought: one sentence of Lev. 15.41 against a whole chapter that treats the laws as rules of self-preservation.

**Reply.** The literature's; Hobbes gives no direct answer. Defenders of the prudential reading accept the second horn and deny that it is a defect. Hobbes's moral philosophy, they say, is a set of hypothetical imperatives: if you want to live, seek peace. The reference to God marks how the same rules look to those who believe in a lawgiver, and adds no further obligation. Defenders of the theological reading reply that Hobbes makes the point in *De Cive* as well, and builds a whole chapter on God's natural kingdom and its laws (Lev. 31), so it is not a stray sentence, and that a reading on which "law of nature" never means law makes nonsense of Hobbes's own vocabulary of obligation, sin and justice. A third group argues that one can keep the laws' normative force without settling the theological question, because the laws state what any reasoner must accept if he is to justify his conduct to others.

**Where scholars disagree.** This is the oldest dispute in Hobbes scholarship. A. E. Taylor ("The Ethical Doctrine of Hobbes", 1938, reprinted in K. C. Brown's *Hobbes Studies*, 1965) and Howard Warrender (*The Political Philosophy of Hobbes: His Theory of Obligation*, 1957) argued that the laws of nature oblige as God's commands, and that Hobbes's ethics is a theory of duty, not of interest; F. C. Hood (*The Divine Politics of Thomas Hobbes*, 1964) and A. P. Martinich (*The Two Gods of Leviathan*, 1992) extended the theological reading. On the other side, Thomas Nagel ("Hobbes's Concept of Obligation", *Philosophical Review*, 1959), J. W. N. Watkins (*Hobbes's System of Ideas*, 1965) and David Gauthier (*The Logic of Leviathan*, 1969) read the laws as precepts of prudence. Nagel concluded that Hobbes has no notion of moral obligation at all, only of rational self-interest. Stephen Darwall (*The British Moralists and the Internal 'Ought'*, 1995) reads Hobbes as an early internalist about reasons. S. A. Lloyd (*Morality in the Philosophy of Thomas Hobbes*, 2009) takes the third line: she reads the laws as derived from a reciprocity theorem, roughly that one may not condemn in others what one would do oneself, which binds anyone who offers reasons to others, believer or not. The SEP article on Hobbes notes the division and points to Warrender and Martinich on one side and Nagel and Darwall on the other (all checked in the SEP bibliographies and text). The dispute matters for every argument after this one. If the laws are only theorems, the obligation to obey the sovereign is prudential and lapses when prudence does; if they are God's commands, a subject owes obedience even when calculation points the other way.

**Argument 11. Authorization: persons, actors and the representative (Lev. 16–17)**

*What it proves:* that a multitude of individuals can become one person, the commonwealth, only by each authorizing a single representative, so that the representative's acts count as the acts of each of them; and so that the sovereign's actions are the subjects' own.

Chapter 16 closes Part I with a short theory of persons that Part II depends on. It begins with a definition. A person is he "whose words or actions are considered, either as his own, or as representing the words or actions of an other man, or of any other thing to whom they are attributed, whether Truly or by Fiction" (Lev. 16). Hobbes then distinguishes the natural person, whose words are his own, from the "Feigned or Artificiall person", whose words represent another (Lev. 16). The machinery is borrowed from the theatre and the law court. *Persona* meant a mask, and "to Personate, is to Act, or Represent himselfe, or an other" (Lev. 16). Chapter 17 then uses it to make the state.

1. An artificial person is one whose words and actions represent another. Where the one represented owns them, "the Person is the Actor; and he that owneth his words and actions, is the AUTHOR: In which case the Actor acteth by Authority" (Lev. 16).
2. Authority is "a Right of doing any act", and to act by authority is to act "by Commission, or Licence from him whose right it is" (Lev. 16). What an actor does within his authority binds the author as if the author had done it.
3. A multitude is by nature many, not one. "A Multitude of men, are made One Person, when they are by one man, or one Person, Represented; so that it be done with the consent of every one of that Multitude in particular. For it is the Unity of the Representer, not the Unity of the Represented, that maketh the Person One" (Lev. 16).
4. Each member is then an author of what the representative does, and if the authority is given "without stint", each owns "all the actions the Representer doth" (Lev. 16).
5. The way to erect a common power is for every man to covenant with every man: "I Authorise and give up my Right of Governing my selfe, to this Man, or to this Assembly of men, on this condition, that thou give up thy Right to him, and Authorise all his Actions in like manner" (Lev. 17).
Therefore the multitude is "united in one Person", the commonwealth, whose definition is "One Person, of whose Acts a great Multitude, by mutuall Covenants one with another, have made themselves every one the Author" (Lev. 17). Hobbes stresses that this "is more than Consent, or Concord; it is a reall Unitie of them all, in one and the same Person" (Lev. 17).

The payoff comes in chapter 18. Because "every Subject is by this Institution Author of all the Actions, and Judgements of the Soveraigne", a subject who "complaineth of injury from his Soveraigne, complaineth of that whereof he himselfe is Author" (Lev. 18). The sovereign may commit iniquity, but not injury in the proper sense, since no one can injure himself.

**What each premise rests on.** Premises 1 and 2 rest on the legal ideas of agency and ownership: Hobbes says "author" is to actions what "owner" is to goods (Lev. 16). Premise 3 is the decisive claim. It locates the unity of a people in its representative and not in any prior collective will. This is Hobbes's answer to the parliamentarians of the 1640s, who claimed to speak for "the people" against the king. On Hobbes's view there is no people apart from its representative. Premise 4 rests on the voluntariness of authorization, and premise 5 on the second law of nature (Argument 7).

**Textual basis.** Lev. 16 (whole); 17 (the covenant formula, the definition of a commonwealth, "Mortall God"); 18 (consequences: no injury by the sovereign); 42 (actions done in obedience are the sovereign's). The study guide calls authorization "the book's most original idea" ([study guide](../../study/hobbes-leviathan.md)). The SEP article on Hobbes's moral and political philosophy notes that the *Elements of Law* and *De Cive* have subjects lay down or transfer their right to the sovereign, and that only *Leviathan* has them own and authorize its actions (checked in the SEP text).

**Objection.** Two, one internal and one external. The internal one is stated in the SEP article. If subjects own and authorize all the sovereign's actions, they seem to share responsibility for everything it does. Yet Hobbes insists in Lev. 42 that "the externall actions done in obedience to them, without the inward approbation, are the actions of the Soveraign, and not of the Subject" (Lev. 42), "them" being the sovereign's laws. He cannot have it both ways. The external objection was put by Sir Robert Filmer, *Observations concerning the Originall of Government* (1652). Filmer agreed with Hobbes about the rights of sovereignty but rejected his way of acquiring them. A multitude that is not yet one person cannot covenant as a body, and real men never assembled to authorize anyone. So the covenant is a fiction, and a fiction cannot oblige (Filmer's argument is paraphrased here). Jean Hampton (1986) added a modern version: if authorization means each subject keeps the right to judge whether the sovereign acts within the authority given, then the subjects have only lent their power, and the absolutism of Argument 12 fails.

**Reply.** Hobbes's own, in part. Against Filmer, Hobbes needs no historical assembly: chapter 20 argues that the same rights arise when people submit to a conqueror (Argument 13), and the Review and Conclusion treats tacit submission as enough. What matters is that each subject can be understood to have authorized the sovereign, not that a meeting took place. On responsibility the literature offers a reply. S. A. Lloyd ("Authorization and Moral Responsibility in the Philosophy of Hobbes", *Hobbes Studies* 29, 2016, checked in the SEP bibliography) and the SEP article argue that Hobbesian authorization means owning an act as a matter of right, so that the author cannot complain of injury, without carrying moral blame for it. On that reading Lev. 18 and Lev. 42 are consistent: the subject has no grievance in justice, and the sovereign carries the sin. Against Hampton the reply is the text. Authority given "without stint" leaves no reserved judgment about scope, except the rights no one can give up (Argument 14).

**Where scholars disagree.** First, about whether authorization changes the theory. David Gauthier (*The Logic of Leviathan*, 1969) took authorization as the key to *Leviathan* and as a real advance on the earlier works. Hampton (1986) argued that Hobbes's premises support only an agency relation, in which the people keep the right to recall their agent, and not the alienation of right he wanted. Second, about whose person the state is. Quentin Skinner ("Hobbes and the Purely Artificial Person of the State", *Journal of Political Philosophy* 7, 1999, checked at PhilPapers) argues that for Hobbes the commonwealth is itself a person, distinct from both the people and the sovereign, with the sovereign as its representative; this is his reading of Hobbes as a founder of the modern idea of the state. Critics of that reading disagree about where the person lies. Christine Chwaszcza ("The Seat of Sovereignty: Hobbes on the Artificial Person of the Commonwealth or State", *Hobbes Studies* 25, 2012, checked at PhilPapers) argues, against Skinner, that what Hobbes calls the sovereign is not a person but the office of government; A. P. Martinich ("Authorization and Representation in Hobbes's *Leviathan*", in *The Oxford Handbook of Hobbes*, 2016, title checked at PhilPapers) and Laurens van Apeldoorn ("On the Person and Office of the Sovereign in Hobbes' *Leviathan*", *BJHP* 28, 2020, checked in the SEP bibliography) continue the debate. Hanna Pitkin's *The Concept of Representation* (1967) treated Hobbes as the source of "authorization" theories of representation and criticized them for licensing anything the representative does. The question underneath is whether Hobbes has explained how a people can bind itself, or only renamed its submission.

**Argument 12. Sovereignty must be absolute and undivided (Lev. 18, 29)**

*What it proves:* that whoever holds sovereignty, one man or an assembly, must hold all its essential rights together and be subject to no lawful check by subjects, courts or rival bodies; and that the doctrine of divided or limited sovereignty is a cause of civil war.

Chapter 18 derives twelve rights of the sovereign by institution: subjects cannot change the form of government, the sovereign cannot forfeit his power, the majority binds dissenters, the sovereign does no injury and cannot justly be punished, and he holds the rights of judging doctrine, making law, judging controversies, making war and peace, choosing ministers, rewarding and punishing, and granting honours. The argument here is the one that ties them together. Chapter 29 then lists the opinions that weaken a commonwealth, and one is "That the Soveraign Power may be divided" (Lev. 29). The study guide reconstructs the argument for absolute sovereignty in its section (c) ([study guide](../../study/hobbes-leviathan.md)); the version below adds the text on indivisibility and the disputes.

1. The sovereign is made by a covenant "of one to another, and not of him to any of them", so "there can happen no breach of Covenant on the part of the Soveraigne" (Lev. 18).
2. Any condition laid on the sovereign would need a judge. If subjects claim a breach and others deny it, "there is in this case, no Judge to decide the controversie: it returns therefore to the Sword again" (Lev. 18).
3. Promises without a power behind them bind nothing: "Covenants being but words, and breath, have no force to oblige, contain, constrain, or protect any man, but what it has from the publique Sword" (Lev. 18).
4. The essential rights depend on one another. "if he transferre the Militia, he retains the Judicature in vain, for want of execution of the Lawes; Or if he grant away the Power of raising Mony; the Militia is in vain" (Lev. 18). So they are "incommunicable, and inseparable" (Lev. 18).
5. Where the rights are split between bodies that can disagree, each is a rival representative, and there is no one person: "they are not one Person, nor one Soveraign, but three Persons, and three Soveraigns" (Lev. 29, on mixed monarchy).
6. Rival sovereigns over the same subjects are the state of war inside the commonwealth: "what is it to divide the Power of a Common-wealth, but to Dissolve it; for Powers divided mutually destroy each other" (Lev. 29).
Therefore sovereignty must be undivided, and it must be unlimited by any lawful superior, since a limiting body would itself be sovereign or would reopen the question of who judges.

Hobbes applies the argument to England directly. Had there not first been an opinion "that these Powers were divided between the King, and the Lords, and the House of Commons, the people had never been divided, and fallen into this Civill Warre" (Lev. 18). The target is the theory of mixed monarchy that parliamentarian writers drew from Charles I's *Answer to the XIX Propositions* (1642) and that Philip Hunton set out in *A Treatise of Monarchie* (1643) (both unchecked, from the standard historical literature). Hobbes adds a grotesque image: a mixed commonwealth is like a man he had seen with "another man growing out of his side" (Lev. 29). The same reasoning rules out a church with its own jurisdiction, since "where one is Soveraign, another Supreme; where one can make Lawes, and another make Canons; there must needs be two Common-wealths" (Lev. 29; Argument 15).

**What each premise rests on.** Premise 1 rests on the authorization structure of Argument 11. Premise 2 is the regress at the heart of the argument: a judge of the sovereign is either sovereign or not final. The SEP article on Hobbes's moral and political philosophy states it as the danger of "an infinite regress of non-authoritative 'authorities'" (checked in the SEP text). Premise 3 is the lesson of Lev. 14 (Argument 8). Premise 4 is a claim about how government works, and premise 5 applies the unity-of-the-representer thesis from Lev. 16.

**Textual basis.** Lev. 18 (the rights of sovereigns by institution, their indivisibility, the reply to the "miserable" condition of subjects); 19 (forms of commonwealth; tyranny as a name of dislike); 26 (the sovereign not subject to civil laws); 29 (the seditious doctrines, dividing sovereignty, mixed government); *De Cive* chs. 6–7; EL II.1. The library entry puts the point in one line: divided sovereignty "is civil war by institutional means" ([Thomas Hobbes](../../eras/03-early-modern/thomas-hobbes.md)).

**Objection.** The classic objection is John Locke's (*Second Treatise*, 1689, §93; paraphrased). It is odd to think that people would take care to protect themselves against polecats and foxes, the harms other subjects might do them, and then be content to be devoured by lions, a ruler with unchecked power. Locke's conclusion is that rational people would never authorize an absolute power, and that a government which threatens its subjects puts itself into a state of war with them. A second objection comes from the history Hobbes appeals to. Divided constitutions have been stable: the later British constitution and federal systems divide powers and yet do not dissolve. Gregory Kavka (*Hobbesian Moral and Political Theory*, 1986) argued that Hobbes's own premises support only a strong, not an absolute, sovereign, and that a limited, divided government can supply the security that the state of nature lacks.

**Reply.** Hobbes's own first. He anticipates the "miserable condition" objection in Lev. 18. Subjects under any form of government suffer some harm, because "the estate of Man can never be without some incommodity or other", but the worst a government can do "is scarce sensible, in respect of the miseries, and horrible calamities, that accompany a Civill Warre" (Lev. 18). Sovereigns also have reason not to ruin their subjects, since the sovereign's own strength lies in theirs (Lev. 18, paraphrased). Against Locke, then, the lion is a better bet than the war of polecats and foxes. Against the divided-constitution objection the literature's reply is that Hobbes's claim is about final authority, not about the division of labour. Courts, parliaments and ministers can exercise powers, so long as there is a final arbiter whose word ends disputes, and Hobbes allows that the sovereign may delegate everything except sovereignty itself (Lev. 18: grants of the essential rights are void unless sovereignty is "in direct termes renounced"). On this reading a modern constitution is Hobbesian if some body, a constituent power or the people at the ballot box, has the last word.

**Where scholars disagree.** The dispute is over whether the regress argument works. Jean Hampton (*Hobbes and the Social Contract Tradition*, 1986) gave it its strongest form and then argued that it fails, because the self-defence rights Hobbes keeps (Argument 14) reintroduce private judgment at the base of the system. Kavka argued the opposite way: drop absolutism, keep the rest. The SEP article, by S. A. Lloyd and Susanne Sreedhar, presents absolutism as a claim about authority to settle disputes: the powers of government are connected, so that losing one may defeat the rest, and any limit invites disputes about whether it has been crossed (checked in the SEP text). Historians read the argument as an intervention. Johann Sommerville (*Thomas Hobbes: Political Ideas in Historical Context*, 1992) and Quentin Skinner place it against the mixed-monarchy writings of the early 1640s. Richard Tuck (*The Sleeping Sovereign*, 2016; unchecked here) has argued that Hobbes's distinction between sovereignty and government made room for a democratic sovereign who rarely acts, and Kinch Hoekstra ("A Lion in the House: Hobbes and Democracy", 2007, checked in the SEP bibliography) examines Hobbes's treatment of democracy as the first form of sovereignty. Whether Hobbes's absolutism describes any workable state or only marks a limit no state can reach is still argued.

**Argument 13. Commonwealth by acquisition: covenants made in fear bind (Lev. 14, 20)**

*What it proves:* that a conqueror who spares the lives of the vanquished, in exchange for their submission, acquires exactly the rights of a sovereign set up by mutual agreement; because covenants made out of fear are valid, and every commonwealth rests on fear anyway.

The premise is laid down early, in chapter 14: "Covenants entred into by fear, in the condition of meer Nature, are obligatory" (Lev. 14.28). Hobbes's examples are ransom and peace treaties. If I covenant to pay a ransom for my life, "I am bound by it", for it is a contract in which one side receives life and the other money or service (Lev. 14.28). The principle is general: "whatsoever I may lawfully do without Obligation, the same I may lawfully Covenant to do through feare: and what I lawfully Covenant, I cannot lawfully break" (Lev. 14.28). Chapter 20 applies it to political power, and the Review and Conclusion of 1651 applies it to the England of the Engagement controversy, when former royalists were asked to swear loyalty to the new Commonwealth.

1. A covenant is valid if it is a voluntary transfer of right, and an act done from fear is still voluntary. (Chapter 21 makes the general point: "Feare and Liberty are consistent", as when a man throws his goods overboard to save the ship (Lev. 21.3).)
2. So "Covenants entred into by fear" bind, where no law forbids what is promised (Lev. 14.28).
3. A commonwealth by acquisition arises "when men singly, or many together by plurality of voyces, for fear of death, or bonds, do authorise all the actions of that Man, or Assembly, that hath their lives and liberty in his Power" (Lev. 20).
4. It differs from a commonwealth by institution "onely in this, That men who choose their Soveraign, do it for fear of one another, and not of him whom they Institute: But in this case, they subject themselves, to him they are afraid of. In both cases they do it for fear" (Lev. 20).
5. Whoever holds that covenants from fear are void must say that "no man, in any kind of Common-wealth, could be obliged to Obedience" (Lev. 20).
6. The right of the conqueror comes not from his victory but from the covenant: "It is not therefore the Victory, that giveth the right of Dominion over the Vanquished, but his own Covenant" (Lev. 20).
Therefore "the Rights, and Consequences of Soveraignty, are the same in both" (Lev. 20), and the conquered subject owes the conqueror what an instituting subject owes the sovereign he chose.

Two limits come with the argument. A captive kept in chains has made no covenant and so has "no obligation at all", and may "break their bonds, or the prison; and kill, or carry away captive their Master, justly" (Lev. 20). The obligation begins only when the vanquished is trusted with bodily liberty in return for his promise. And the timing is precise. In the Review and Conclusion Hobbes says the point at which a man becomes subject of a conqueror "is that point, wherein having liberty to submit to him, he consenteth, either by expresse words, or by other sufficient sign, to be his Subject". That liberty arrives when his former sovereign can no longer protect him and "the means of his life is within the Guards and Garrisons of the Enemy" (Review and Conclusion). He closes the book by describing its design as "to set before mens eyes the mutuall Relation between Protection and Obedience" (Review and Conclusion).

**What each premise rests on.** Premise 1 rests on Hobbes's definition of liberty as absence of external impediment (Argument 14) and of the will as the last appetite in deliberation (Argument 16): fear is one more appetite or aversion, not a force that cancels choice. Premise 2 needs the absence of a forbidding law. In a commonwealth a promise extorted by a thief binds "till the Civill Law discharge me" (Lev. 14.28). Premise 4 rests on the account of motives in Lev. 13 and 17: what drives the institution of a commonwealth is fear of each other.

**Textual basis.** Lev. 14.28 (covenants extorted by fear); 20 (commonwealth by acquisition; paternal and despotical dominion); 21 (fear and liberty; the end of obligation when protection fails); the Review and Conclusion (the added law of nature to protect in war the authority that protects in peace; the point of submission to a conqueror). The study guide notes that scholars debate whether acquisition carries the same weight as institution ([study guide](../../study/hobbes-leviathan.md)).

**Objection.** The standard objection is that this makes might into right. Locke's chapter "Of Conquest" in the *Second Treatise* (1689) argues that an unjust conqueror gains no right by force, and that a promise extorted by a robber with a pistol does not bind, so that consent under duress is no consent (paraphrased). Royalist critics made the political version of the charge at once. Edward Hyde, Earl of Clarendon, in his *Brief View and Survey* of *Leviathan* (1676), accused Hobbes of writing to justify submission to Cromwell; the SEP article notes that Clarendon thought Hobbes supported Cromwell (checked in the SEP text). The IEP article on Hobbes's moral and political philosophy states the general complaint: Hobbes's critics saw from the start that his theory "makes turncoats into moral heroes" (checked in the IEP text). There is also an internal objection. If fear validates covenants, what is left of the distinction between a subject and a slave, apart from the chains?

**Reply.** Hobbes's own, for the most part. First, he does not say that victory confers right; premise 6 says the opposite. The conqueror's right comes from the subject's own act, and the chained captive owes nothing. Second, the robber case is not a counterexample in the state of nature, where no law forbids the payment; within a commonwealth the civil law can and does discharge such promises (Lev. 14.28; Lev. 20). Third, Hobbes turns the objection round. A theory on which covenants made from fear are void condemns every commonwealth, since all rest on fear, and in practice "there is scarce a Common-wealth in the world, whose beginnings can in conscience be justified" (Review and Conclusion). A theory of obligation that waits for a clean origin leaves everyone unobliged. The cost is real, and Hobbes accepts it: obligation tracks protection, and the subject of a defeated sovereign is free to submit to the victor.

**Where scholars disagree.** The historical dispute is over what Hobbes was doing in 1651. Quentin Skinner ("Conquest and Consent: Thomas Hobbes and the Engagement Controversy", 1972; unchecked here) placed the Review and Conclusion among the de facto theorists, such as Anthony Ascham, who argued that obedience is owed to whatever power protects. Kinch Hoekstra ("The de facto Turn in Hobbes's Political Philosophy", 2004; unchecked here) traced how far that turn goes and how it fits Hobbes's earlier works. Jeffrey Collins (*The Allegiance of Thomas Hobbes*, 2005; unchecked here) read the book's religious politics as an alignment with the Independents and Cromwell. Against such readings, interpreters who stress the continuity with *De Cive* argue that the theory of acquisition was already there in 1642, so *Leviathan* does not change sides; it only applies an old principle to new facts. The philosophical dispute is whether fear and consent can coexist. Jean Hampton (1986) and the author of the IEP article judge that the argument strains at every step, because it needs coerced promises to carry full moral weight. The SEP article by S. A. Lloyd and Susanne Sreedhar takes the other side: it treats institution and acquisition as "equally legitimate" ways of establishing sovereignty, with the same underlying motive, fear, and states the upshot plainly: on Hobbes's view legitimacy "depends not on how a government came to power, but only on whether it can effectively protect those who have consented to obey it" (both checked in the SEP text).

**Argument 14. The liberty of subjects and the right to resist (Lev. 21)**

*What it proves:* two things that pull against each other. First, that the liberty of subjects is not a limit on sovereign power: subjects are free where the law is silent, and nothing the sovereign does to them is an injury. Second, that every subject keeps certain liberties no covenant can transfer, above all the liberty to resist those who would kill or wound him, even when they act for the sovereign.

Chapter 21 opens with a definition from natural philosophy: "Liberty, or FREEDOME, signifieth (properly) the absence of Opposition; (by Opposition, I mean externall Impediments of motion;)" (Lev. 21.1). Water is free to run down its channel; a man in chains is not free to move. Civil laws are "Artificiall Chains", fastened at one end "to the lips of that Man, or Assembly, to whom they have given the Soveraigne Power; and at the other end to their own Ears" (Lev. 21.5). These chains hold by the danger, not the difficulty, of breaking them. Hobbes then sets out the two halves of his answer.

1. Liberty in the proper sense is absence of external impediment (Lev. 21.1). Laws are impediments of a special kind, which bind only through fear of punishment.
2. No commonwealth regulates every action, so "in all kinds of actions, by the laws praetermitted, men have the Liberty, of doing what their own reasons shall suggest, for the most profitable to themselves" (Lev. 21.6).
3. This liberty does not limit the sovereign. Since "every Subject is Author of every act the Soveraign doth", nothing the sovereign does "can properly be called Injustice, or Injury" (Lev. 21.7).
4. But a right can pass only by covenant, and some rights cannot be covenanted away. "Covenants, not to defend a mans own body, are voyd" (Lev. 21.11, recalling Lev. 14.30), because the end of every transfer of right is the security of the person who makes it.
5. So "every Subject has Liberty in all those things, the right whereof cannot by Covenant be transferred" (Lev. 21.11).
6. In particular, if the sovereign commands a man, "though justly condemned", to kill or wound himself, not to resist those who assault him, or to go without "food, ayre, medicine", "yet hath that man the Liberty to disobey" (Lev. 21.12). He need not accuse himself without assurance of pardon (Lev. 21.13).
7. The words of submission, "I Authorise, or take upon me, all his actions", contain no promise to kill oneself or another: "by allowing him to Kill Me, I am not bound to Kill my selfe when he commands me" (Lev. 21.14).
Therefore subjects keep a set of true liberties that the sovereign cannot take away by any command, and "The Obligation of Subjects to the Soveraign is understood to last as long, and no longer, than the power lasteth, by which he is able to protect them" (Lev. 21.21).

The cases Hobbes draws from this go further than many readers expect. A conscripted soldier may sometimes refuse "without Injustice", for instance by finding a substitute, and Hobbes makes "allowance to be made for naturall timorousnesse" (Lev. 21.16). The limit is the end of sovereignty: where refusal "frustrates the End for which the Soveraignty was ordained; then there is no Liberty to refuse" (Lev. 21.15), and when the commonwealth needs every man under arms, every one is obliged. No one may resist the sword of the commonwealth to defend another person (Lev. 21.17). But rebels who have already resisted and expect death may join together to defend themselves: "Certainly they have", for "they but defend their lives, which the guilty man may as well do, as the Innocent" (Lev. 21.17).

**What each premise rests on.** Premise 1 is the mechanical definition of liberty that Argument 16 defends against Bramhall. Premise 3 rests on authorization (Argument 11). Premise 4 rests on Lev. 14: the motive of every transfer of right "is nothing else but the security of a mans person" (Lev. 14.9), so no one can be understood to have given up the means of that security (Argument 8). Premise 6 adds a psychological claim. No one can be expected to choose death, so a covenant to submit to it would be an empty sign.

**Textual basis.** Lev. 21.1–25 (whole chapter), especially 21.1–7 (definitions and the compatibility of liberty with absolute power), 21.10–17 (the true liberties), 21.18 (liberty in the silence of the laws) and 21.21 (obligation lasts as long as protection); Lev. 14.9, 14.30 (rights that cannot be laid down); 27 (fear as an excuse); the Review and Conclusion (when a subject may submit to a conqueror; Argument 13). The library entry's "residual right of self-defense" and the study guide's ch. 21 summary state the same doctrine ([Thomas Hobbes](../../eras/03-early-modern/thomas-hobbes.md); [study guide](../../study/hobbes-leviathan.md)).

**Objection.** The objection began with Hobbes's contemporaries. John Bramhall, in *The Catching of Leviathan* (1658), called the book a "Rebell's Catechism" (as quoted in the SEP article, checked). If each subject may judge when his life is threatened, and obligation ends whenever protection fails, then each subject is again the judge of his own safety, and the state of nature has never been left. The SEP article puts the modern form: "if it is left to each subject to judge for herself the adequacy of that protection, it seems that people have never really exited the fearsome state of nature" (checked in the SEP text). Jean Hampton (*Hobbes and the Social Contract Tradition*, 1986) pressed the point as a dilemma. Either the right of self-preservation is surrendered, in which case Hobbes's psychology makes the surrender impossible, or it is retained, in which case the sovereign's power is held only on each subject's continuing judgment, which is not absolute sovereignty at all. Some commentators, the SEP reports, call the limits of obligation the "Achilles' heel" of the theory.

**Reply.** Hobbes's own, in part, and the literature's. Hobbes separates a liberty from a right to judge for others. The condemned man may struggle with his executioner, but no one else may help him (Lev. 21.17), and the sovereign may lawfully punish him. The retained liberty is a liberty of the individual body, not a public right of resistance, so it creates no rival sovereign and no institution that can claim to judge the sovereign. Susanne Sreedhar (*Hobbes on Resistance: Defying the Leviathan*, 2010, checked in the SEP bibliography) argues that once the retained rights are read this way they are consistent with absolutism: they are rights to resist, held by individuals, which do not give anyone authority over the sovereign. She argues too that they are broader than they first appear, covering one's family and, in some cases, one's honour, as the SEP article notes. A sovereign who protects his subjects is seldom resisted, Hobbes adds, and a sovereign who cannot protect them has already lost his title (Lev. 21.21).

**Where scholars disagree.** Readers split three ways. The first group, Hampton among them, holds that the retained rights wreck absolutism, so that Hobbes must choose between his psychology and his politics. A second, with Sreedhar as its main exponent, holds that they are consistent with it and are in fact what makes the theory acceptable to self-interested agents. Eleanor Curran ("Can Rights Curb the Hobbesian Sovereign?", *Law and Philosophy* 25, 2006, checked in the SEP bibliography) argues that the right of self-preservation in the commonwealth is more than a bare liberty and places some constraints on the sovereign. A third line, historical, reads the chapter against the republican writers whom Hobbes attacks in Lev. 21.8–9, the classical authors who taught that only citizens of free states are free: Hobbes replies that although "LIBERTAS" is written on the turrets of Lucca, a citizen there has no more liberty than a subject of Constantinople (Lev. 21.8, paraphrased). Quentin Skinner (*Hobbes and Republican Liberty*, 2008; unchecked here) argues that Hobbes changed his definition of liberty in *Leviathan* precisely to defeat that republican claim. Whether the right of resistance is a loophole, a foundation or a polemical move against the republicans remains open.

**Argument 15. The civil sovereign governs religion (Lev. 12, 32–33, 39, 42)**

*What it proves:* that in a Christian commonwealth there is no church with authority of its own over against the state. The sovereign decides which books are Scripture, who may preach, and what is professed in public, and the subject who obeys a command to profess what he does not believe has done no wrong. Only inward belief lies outside the sovereign's reach, and that is because no one, sovereign or priest, can command it.

The argument starts in natural history. Chapter 6 defines religion with a rule of public authority already built in: "Feare of power invisible, feigned by the mind, or imagined from tales publiquely allowed, RELIGION; not allowed, superstition" (Lev. 6). Chapter 12 then explains where such fear comes from. Its four natural seeds are "Opinion of Ghosts, Ignorance of second causes, Devotion towards what men fear, and Taking of things Casuall for Prognostiques" (Lev. 12), and founders of states cultivated them to make men obedient, so that "the Religion of the former sort, is a part of humane Politiques" (Lev. 12). Religion is a source of power over men, and a power in the commonwealth that the sovereign does not control is a rival sovereign (Argument 12). Parts III and IV turn that political point into a reading of Scripture.

1. Sovereignty must be undivided (Argument 12). Of the diseases of a commonwealth, the most dangerous is a "Ghostly Authority" set up against the civil one (Lev. 29), because men fear the invisible punishments of the priest more than the visible ones of the magistrate.
2. A church that can command, judge or absolve must be one person, and a body of men is one person only through a representative (Argument 11).
3. There is no representative on earth over all Christians, since every Christian is the subject of some particular commonwealth. So a church "such as one as is capable to Command" "is the same thing with a Civil Common-wealth, consisting of Christian men" (Lev. 39). "Temporall and Spirituall Government, are but two words brought into the world, to make men see double, and mistake their Lawfull Soveraign" (Lev. 39).
4. Scripture binds as law only where someone with legislative power has made it law. Since sovereigns are the sole legislators in their dominions, "those Books only are Canonicall, that is, Law, in every nation, which are established for such by the Soveraign Authority" (Lev. 33).
5. Private claims to revelation cannot oblige anyone else, because no one can verify them. A subject may be obliged not to contradict his sovereign's revelation "by act or word", "but not to think any otherwise then my reason perswades me" (Lev. 32).
6. Belief cannot be commanded at all: "Beleef, and Unbeleef never follow mens Commands" (Lev. 42). But "Profession with the tongue is but an externall thing" (Lev. 42), like any other gesture of obedience.
7. When a subject is compelled to profess or perform what the sovereign commands, as Naaman bowed in the house of Rimmon, "that action is not his, but his Soveraigns" (Lev. 42), by the logic of authorization in Argument 11.
8. Nothing necessary to salvation is put at risk, since the only necessary article is "that JESUS IS THE CHRIST" (Lev. 43), together with obedience to the laws of God, which are the laws of nature and include obedience to the sovereign.
Therefore "the Civill Soveraign is the Supreme Pastor, to whose charge the whole flock of his Subjects is committed" (Lev. 42): all other pastors teach by his authority, the public worship of the commonwealth is his to set, and no church may excommunicate, depose or judge him.

**What each premise rests on.** Premises 1–3 apply the theory of sovereignty and of the representative to the church; nothing in them is specifically theological. Premise 4 uses the definition of law as the command of one who has authority (Lev. 26; Argument 10). Premises 5 and 6 rest on Hobbes's epistemology of testimony in Lev. 7 and 32: faith is belief in a person who speaks, and a claim to immediate revelation gives the hearer no grounds he could check. Premise 8 is a reading of Scripture and carries the most weight. If more than one article were necessary, a sovereign's command could put salvation in danger, and chapter 43's answer to the Christian's dilemma would fail. Chapter 32 sets the rule for that reading: "we are not to renounce our Senses, and Experience; nor (that which is the undoubted Word of God) our naturall Reason" (Lev. 32).

**Textual basis.** Lev. 6 (definition of religion); 12 (the natural seeds of religion and their cultivation); 29 (the "Ghostly Authority" as a disease of the commonwealth); 32 (prophecy and natural reason); 33 (the canon of Scripture); 39 (the word "church"); 42 (ecclesiastical power, the Naaman passage and the long reply to Bellarmine); 43 (the one necessary article, and what a Christian owes an infidel sovereign); 46–47 (the "Kingdome of Darknesse"). The library entry's section "Religion under state control" and the study guide's note on chapters 32–47 give the summary and the warning not to skip these chapters ([Thomas Hobbes](../../eras/03-early-modern/thomas-hobbes.md); [study guide](../../study/hobbes-leviathan.md)).

**Objection.** Two, one about the doctrine and one about its author. The first is the Christian's: God must be obeyed rather than men, and a church whose doctrine is fixed by the magistrate is no longer the church Christ founded. Bellarmine's case for papal power over princes, which chapter 42 answers point by point, is one form of this, and Presbyterian claims to a church discipline independent of the magistrate are another. Premise 7 makes the objection sharper. If a compelled profession is the sovereign's act and not mine, then the martyrs who refused to sacrifice to the emperor died for nothing, and Christ's warning against those who deny him before men loses its force. The second objection is that the argument is not sincere. Its reduction of the creed to one article, its account of religion as fear cultivated by founders, and its treatment of prophecy and miracles add up, on this reading, to a covert attack on revealed religion. Hobbes's contemporaries said so, and Edwin Curley ("'I durst not write so boldly'", 1992, checked in the SEP bibliography) finds a deliberate circle in chs. 32 and 37: prophets are tested by miracles and miracles by doctrine, a "fairly obvious problem of circularity" that Curley suggests Hobbes may have left the reader to find (Curley as quoted in SEP "Thomas Hobbes", checked).

**Reply.** To the first objection, Hobbes's own, in chapter 43. Under a Christian sovereign the conflict cannot arise over anything necessary, because such a sovereign professes the one necessary article. Under an infidel sovereign, Christians keep their faith in their hearts with "the licence that Naaman had" (Lev. 43), or, if they choose to bear witness, "they ought to expect their reward in Heaven, and not complain of their Lawfull Soveraign; much lesse make warre upon him" (Lev. 43). Martyrdom remains open; rebellion does not. Martyrs in the strict sense, chapter 42 adds, are witnesses of the resurrection, not anyone who dies for an opinion. To the second objection, the SEP article "Thomas Hobbes" (Stewart Duncan's, checked) notes that someone hiding his atheism would hardly have defended so many other dangerous views so openly, and that Hobbes's late claim that God is corporeal, defended at length against Bramhall, is "on the surface an odd theism rather than atheism".

**Where scholars disagree.** The SEP's moral and political article states the position plainly: "There is no settled consensus on how Hobbes understands the significance of religion within his political theory" (SEP, checked). On sincerity, A. P. Martinich (*The Two Gods of Leviathan*, 1992) reads Hobbes as a sincere, if anti-clerical, Christian who wanted to show that Christianity and absolute sovereignty are compatible (the SEP describes this reading without naming its holders; Martinich's place in it is from memory). Leo Strauss (1936) and Curley read the theology as cover or irony, and Douglas Jesseph ("Hobbes's Atheism", 2002) argues that a material God is incoherent (all three checked in the SEP bibliographies). On toleration, readers split again. Chapter 47 welcomes "the Independency of the Primitive Christians" as "perhaps the best", "because there ought to be no Power over the Consciences of men, but of the Word it selfe" (Lev. 47). Alan Ryan ("A More Tolerant Hobbes?", in Susan Mendus (ed.), *Justifying Toleration*) and Jeffrey Collins (*The Allegiance of Thomas Hobbes*, 2005) take this as a real case for toleration, in Collins's version tied to support for Cromwellian Independency. Johann Sommerville (*Thomas Hobbes: Political Ideas in Historical Context*, 1992) argues that Hobbes did not approve of Independency, and S. A. Lloyd (*Hobbes Studies*, 2022) supports Sommerville from Hobbes's theory of responsibility; Teresa Bejan ("Difference without Disagreement: Rethinking Hobbes on 'Independency' and Toleration", 2016) reopens the question (titles checked in the SEP bibliography; the positions summarized from memory and unchecked except where a title states them). Whether chapters 32–47 are the theological foundation of *Leviathan*, a shield for it or a solvent of religion is still open.

**Argument 16. Liberty and necessity: Hobbes's compatibilism against Bramhall (Lev. 21; *Of Liberty and Necessity*)**

*What it proves:* that every human action, voluntary ones included, is necessitated by prior causes, and that this takes nothing away from human liberty, rightly defined, or from the justice of laws, praise and punishment. The "freedom from necessity" that the schools and Bishop Bramhall defend is not a lost good but an incoherent notion.

The debate began in 1645 in the Paris household of the Marquess of Newcastle, where Hobbes and John Bramhall, the exiled Bishop of Derry, argued the question and then set out their views in writing. Hobbes's reply was printed without his consent in 1654 as *Of Liberty and Necessity*; Bramhall answered with *A Defence of True Liberty* (1655), and Hobbes with *The Questions concerning Liberty, Necessity, and Chance* (1656), which prints the bishop's text, his own earlier reply, Bramhall's rejoinder and fresh Animadversions side by side (the Paris meeting "in the presence of my Lord of Newcastle" is Hobbes's own account in the epistle to the *Questions*, EW V; the 1654 first edition is noted by Molesworth, EW IV; 1656 for the *Questions* is in the SEP; the year 1645, the 1655 date of Bramhall's *Defence* and the unauthorized printing are from memory, unchecked). Molesworth prints the 1654 treatise in modernized spelling, which the quotations below keep. Hobbes first states the question in a way that changes it: "I acknowledge this liberty, that I can do if I will; but to say, I can will if I will, I take to be an absurd speech" (LN, EW IV 240). The question is not whether we act as we will, which no one denies, but whether the will itself is in our power.

1. Liberty is "the absence of all the impediments to action that are not contained in the nature and intrinsical quality of the agent" (LN, EW IV 273). Water is free to run down its channel; a man in chains is not free to walk, though a man too sick to walk does not lack liberty but power. *Leviathan* gives the same definition: the liberty of a man "consisteth in this, that he finds no stop, in doing what he has the will, desire, or inclination to doe" (Lev. 21.2).
2. Deliberation "is nothing else but alternate imagination of the good and evil sequels of an action" (LN, EW IV 273), that is, alternating appetite and aversion, and the will is the last appetite in that series: "the last Appetite in Deliberation is called the Will" (Lev. 7.2).
3. Nothing begins from itself. So when a man comes to will what he did not will before, "the cause of his will, is not the will itself, but something else not in his own disposing" (LN, EW IV 274).
4. A sufficient cause is one that lacks nothing needed to produce the effect, and a sufficient cause is a necessary cause, since if it could fail to produce the effect, something needed would be missing. So "whatsoever is produced, is produced necessarily" (LN, EW IV 275), down to the throw of the dice.
5. A free agent, then, is not one that, with all requisites present, can still not act. That definition "implies a contradiction, and is nonsense" (LN, EW IV 275). A free agent "is he that can do if he will, and forbear if he will" (LN, EW IV 275).
Therefore "Liberty and Necessity are Consistent" (Lev. 21.4). Our voluntary actions proceed from liberty because they proceed from our will, and from necessity because "every act of mans will, and every desire, and inclination proceedeth from some cause, which causes in a continuall chaine (whose first link in the hand of God the first of all causes) proceed from Necessity" (Lev. 21.4). The phrase *free will* is a confusion: "from the use of the word Freewill, no liberty can be inferred to the will, desire, or inclination, but the liberty of the man" (Lev. 21.2).

**What each premise rests on.** Premise 1 is the mechanical definition of liberty that also does the political work in Argument 14: laws are external impediments, and fear does not take away liberty (Lev. 21.3). Premise 2 is the psychology of Lev. 6, which itself depends on the account of appetite as motion (Argument 1). Premises 3 and 4 are the causal principles of Hobbes's natural philosophy. Hobbes says plainly that he can prove premise 3 only by asking the reader to try to imagine a thing beginning without a cause. Premise 5 follows from 4. The theological rider in Lev. 21.4, that God's will is the cause of every human appetite, is Hobbes's extra charge against Bramhall: liberty from necessity would make man "a contradiction, and impediment to the omnipotence and Liberty of God" (Lev. 21.4).

**Textual basis.** Lev. 6 (deliberation and will); Lev. 21.1–4 (liberty, fear and necessity); *Of Liberty and Necessity* (EW IV 229–278), especially Hobbes's eight-point statement of his opinion (EW IV 272–275), the section headed MY REASONS (EW IV 275–278) and his answers to Bramhall's "inconveniences" (EW IV 252–256); *The Questions concerning Liberty, Necessity, and Chance* (EW V), which reprints Bramhall's arguments in full. Read the exchange in Chappell's Cambridge edition, *Hobbes and Bramhall on Liberty and Necessity* (1999).

**Objection.** Bramhall's, in two parts. First, the liberty Hobbes leaves us is not human liberty. Necessity and spontaneity may meet, the bishop grants, "but real necessity and true liberty can never meet together" (Questions, No. III, EW V). True liberty is rooted in reason, which can weigh the options and hold the will back, and a liberty that water and bees share is no liberty of a rational agent: "Is not this a childish liberty", Bramhall asks; "This is a brutish liberty" (Questions, No. III, EW V). Second, necessity destroys morality. If every act is necessitated, laws that forbid it are unjust, deliberation and advice are in vain, and praise, blame, reward and punishment are all pointless, since the agent could not have done otherwise. And if God is the first link in the chain, God is the author of sin. The modern form of the first part is the incompatibilist's: freedom requires the ability to do otherwise, or that the agent be the ultimate source of the act, and a determined agent has neither.

**Reply.** Hobbes's own, point by point (LN, EW IV 253–256). Laws are not made unjust by necessity. A man is punished not because his theft proceeded from an uncaused choice, but because it harmed others, and to punish voluntary harm "frameth and maketh men’s wills such as men would have them" (LN, EW IV 254). Deliberation is not in vain, since "it is the consultation that causeth a man, and necessitateth him to choose to do one thing rather than another" (LN, EW IV 254). To think otherwise is like reasoning, "If I shall live till to-morrow, I shall live till to-morrow, though I run myself through with a sword to-day" (LN, EW IV 255). Praise and blame say that an act is good or bad for someone, and with reward and punishment they "do by example make and conform the will to good and evil" (LN, EW IV 256). Necessity, on this view, works through deliberation and sanctions; it does not bypass them. To the charge of "brutish" liberty, Hobbes answers that rational and irrational agents differ in how much they deliberate, not in having a will exempt from causes.

**Where scholars disagree.** That Hobbes is a compatibilist, and an early one, is not disputed. The SEP article "Compatibilism" (Michael McKenna's, checked) treats his "no stop" definition as "an exemplary expression of classical compatibilism", and Hume's account in the *Enquiry*, section VIII, follows the same line (from memory). The disputes are about how good the theory is and what it was for. On the first, McKenna's article states the standard objection to classical compatibilism: a person in the grip of a compulsion or a hallucination may do as she wants without impediment and still not act freely, so ability to do what one wills is not enough (SEP, checked). Defenders of Hobbes reply that Hobbes never meant to give an account of moral responsibility in the modern sense, only of liberty, and that he grounds desert in the social uses of punishment. On the second, Nicholas Jackson (*Hobbes, Bramhall, and the Politics of Liberty and Necessity*, 2007, checked in the SEP bibliography) reads the quarrel as a political dispute of the Civil War, in which a royalist bishop and a royalist philosopher fought over obedience and religion as much as over metaphysics. Quentin Skinner (*Hobbes and Republican Liberty*, 2008, unchecked here) argues that Hobbes's definition of liberty as absence of external impediment was sharpened against republican theories of freedom (Argument 14). Whether Hobbes solved the problem of free will, dissolved it as a confusion of words, or changed the subject remains a live question in the free-will literature.
