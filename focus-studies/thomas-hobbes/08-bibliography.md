# Bibliography — Thomas Hobbes

Annotated and tiered: read downward only as far as your questions require. No prices are given; none was read on a publisher page for this list. Publishers and years come from a library catalogue (Open Library records, searched November 2026), from the bibliographies of the two SEP entries on Hobbes, or from the copyright page of a saved preview, and entries say which where the sources disagree. Page counts are given only where the catalogue record states them. One fact governs the whole list: *Leviathan* is cited by **chapter and paragraph** (Lev. 13.9), the *Elements of Law* by part, chapter and section (EL I.14.11), *De Cive* by chapter and section (DCv 1.12). Curley numbers *Leviathan*'s paragraphs, Tuck prints the 1651 page numbers in the margin, and Gaskin and Tuck & Silverthorne keep the section numbers, so any reading edition below finds every reference in this dossier. [02-works-and-texts.md](02-works-and-texts.md) explains the corpus and gives the edition rulings this list follows. This file quotes no Hobbes; the rest of the dossier quotes him in the spelling of the saved texts (the 1651 spelling for *Leviathan*), and the free texts in §1 are the ones it quotes.

## 1. Primary texts — the editions the plan uses

**Richard Tuck (ed.), *Leviathan*, revised student edition (Cambridge Texts in the History of Political Thought; Cambridge University Press, 1996; 519 pp.).** The dossier's first ruling for *Leviathan*, Weeks 1–7. It keeps Hobbes's spelling and punctuation, so its page looks like the text the dossier quotes, and its margins carry the 1651 page numbers that older scholarship cites.

**Edwin Curley (ed.), *Leviathan, with selected variants from the Latin edition of 1668* (Hackett, 1994; 584 pp.).** The alternative ruling, and the better one for Weeks 6–7. Spelling and punctuation are modernized, paragraphs are numbered, and the notes translate the passages where the 1668 Latin differs, which is where the arguments on religion in Week 6 are most often fought. The IEP calls it the best edition of the book. Curley is also a party to that fight: both SEP entries cite his essay "I durst not write so boldly" (§3).

**Noel Malcolm (ed.), *Leviathan* (Clarendon Edition of the Works of Thomas Hobbes, vols. 3–5; Oxford University Press).** The first critical edition and the first to print the English and Latin side by side. The catalogue record checked is a printing of 4 November 2014 (1,832 pp.); [02](02-works-and-texts.md) gives first publication as 2012. Malcolm's research toward it is the source of the dating of the Head, Bear and Ornaments printings in [02](02-works-and-texts.md). A library book, not a reading copy: open it when a variant matters.

**J. C. A. Gaskin (ed.), *The Elements of Law Natural and Politic: Part I, Human Nature; Part II, De Corpore Politico; with Three Lives* (Oxford World's Classics, Oxford University Press).** Week 1 (Epistle Dedicatory, ch. 1) and the parallels for Weeks 3–4. It keeps Tönnies's numbering under the 1650 titles. The "Three Lives" of the subtitle are early lives of Hobbes, useful for [01-life-and-times.md](01-life-and-times.md); which three was not checked here. The catalogue record checked is a 1999 printing (284 pp.); [02](02-works-and-texts.md) gives 1994, and the SEP's bibliography cites the edition as 1990 (see the log). For a disputed reading, Johann P. Sommerville (ed.), *The Elements of Law* (Clarendon Edition, vol. 1; Oxford University Press, 2024, per the copyright page of the saved preview), whose introduction is [02](02-works-and-texts.md)'s source for the 1640 circulation and the 1650 split.

**Richard Tuck & Michael Silverthorne (eds.), *On the Citizen* (Cambridge Texts in the History of Political Thought; Cambridge University Press, 1998; 250 pp.).** Week 3 (Preface to the Reader) and the shorter restatement of *Leviathan* 13–15 in chs. 1–3. A translation from the 1647 Latin, which the IEP calls the best. It matters because the 1651 English the dossier quotes is probably not Hobbes's own (Malcolm's attribution to Charles Cotton, in [02](02-works-and-texts.md)): check any argument that turns on one English word against this edition.

**Vere Chappell (ed.), *Hobbes and Bramhall on Liberty and Necessity* (Cambridge Texts in the History of Philosophy; Cambridge University Press, 1999; 104 pp.).** Week 7 and Argument 16. Per [02](02-works-and-texts.md), it prints the exchange of 1645–46 with selections from the later *Questions*, so you read Bramhall in his own words rather than through Hobbes's quotations of him.

**The Clarendon Edition volumes already out** (general editors Malcolm, Quentin Skinner and Keith Thomas; [02](02-works-and-texts.md) gives the plan). Catalogue records checked: Howard Warrender (ed.), *De Cive: The Latin Version* and *De Cive: The English Version* (Clarendon Press, 1983; the records number them vols. 2 and 3 of the series' earlier title, "The Clarendon Edition of the Philosophical Works of Thomas Hobbes"); Malcolm (ed.), *The Correspondence* (vols. 6–7; 1994); Alan Cromartie & Skinner (eds.), *Writings on Common Law and Hereditary Right* (vol. 11; 2005), the *Dialogue* of Week 5's optional reading; Paul Seaward (ed.), *Behemoth, or The Long Parliament* (vol. 10; 2010), Week 7. Use them to check, not to read.

**Free texts — the ones the dossier quotes,** so you can check any quotation word for word:

- ***Leviathan* (1651):** [Project Gutenberg #3207](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/3207). Its header does not name the printing it was made from, so for a quotation that matters, compare [Wikisource's transcription](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Leviathan) of the Head, the true first edition.
- ***Leviathan* (Latin, 1668):** a scan of the Blaeu edition on the [Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/leviathansivedem00hobb); the record says it was extracted from the second volume of Blaeu's 1668 collected works. Week 6, with Curley's notes.
- **Molesworth's *English Works* (London: John Bohn)** on the Internet Archive: [vol. II](https://archive.org/details/englishworksofth0002hobb), the 1651 English *De Cive* (*Philosophical Rudiments*); [vol. IV](https://archive.org/details/englishworksofth0004hobb), *Human Nature*, *De Corpore Politico* and *Of Liberty and Necessity*; [vol. V](https://archive.org/details/englishworksofth0005hobb), the *Questions concerning Liberty, Necessity, and Chance*. The dossier's saved vol. II is a different scan of the same volume.
- ***Behemoth*:** Ferdinand Tönnies (ed.), [*Behemoth; or, The Long Parliament*](https://archive.org/details/cu31924028063893) (London: Simpkin, Marshall, 1889). The dossier paraphrases it and does not quote it.
- **The Third Objections:** Elizabeth S. Haldane & G. R. T. Ross (trans.), *The Philosophical Works of Descartes*, [vol. II](https://archive.org/details/philosophicalwor02descuoft) (Cambridge University Press, 1911–12; corrected reprint 1934). Week 2. The dossier's saved scan is that reprint ("reprinted, with corrections, 1934"; see the log).
- **Aubrey's life of Hobbes:** Andrew Clark (ed.), *'Brief Lives'*, [vol. I](https://archive.org/details/briefliveschiefl01aubr) (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1898). Week 1.

**Avoid** abridged *Leviathan*s that drop Parts III and IV without saying so; any "1651" reprint whose copy-text is the Ornaments printing; citing *De Corpore Politico* by its 1650 chapter numbers without the EL equivalent; and reading the 1651 English *De Cive* as if every word were Hobbes's. The SEP's advice is the dossier's too: "Readers new to Hobbes should begin with Leviathan, being sure to read Parts Three and Four" (SEP, "Hobbes's Moral and Political Philosophy", §1).

## 2. First secondary books, in order

1. **Richard Tuck, *Hobbes* (Oxford University Press, 1989).** One or two evenings before Week 1. The catalogue lists printings from 1989 to 2022; it is now sold as a Very Short Introduction (from memory; see the log). Its catalogue description says it re-evaluates the reputation of a pessimistic atheist proposing a totalitarian state, and shows Hobbes's concern with refuting skepticism. That is Tuck's own, contested reading: a thesis to test in Weeks 2–3, not a neutral map.
2. **A. P. Martinich, *Hobbes: A Biography* (Cambridge University Press, 1999).** The life, for [01-life-and-times.md](01-life-and-times.md) and Week 1. The SEP's verdict: "The best modern biography is Martinich 1999" (SEP, "Thomas Hobbes", Bibliography). The catalogue's description says it draws on unpublished as well as published sources. Martinich has his own view of Hobbes's religion (§3, Religion), so read his chapters on it with Curley's notes beside them.
3. **Tom Sorell, *Hobbes* (Arguments of the Philosophers; Routledge & Kegan Paul, 1986).** Weeks 2–4, for one analytic book on the system rather than the politics alone. The IEP calls it a concise and well-judged account of the life and works; the SEP names it, with Martinich's *Hobbes* (Routledge, 2005), among the general books that cover the non-political philosophy.
4. **Tom Sorell (ed.), *The Cambridge Companion to Hobbes* (Cambridge University Press, 1996).** Dip in by topic from Week 2 on; the IEP calls it an excellent set of essays on every side of Hobbes's work. For the one book, Patricia Springborg (ed.), *The Cambridge Companion to Hobbes's Leviathan* (Cambridge University Press, 2007), Weeks 5–6.
5. **Johann P. Sommerville, *Thomas Hobbes: Political Ideas in Historical Context* (Macmillan, 1992).** Weeks 5–6; cited by the SEP and the IEP. A historian's book that sets the doctrines in the English quarrels of their time, the context [01](01-life-and-times.md) sketches (description from memory; unchecked).

## 3. Advanced and specialist, by area

Open these when a week's checkpoint question has narrowed to a debate. Most are academic books, best borrowed. A scholar's position not checked at the SEP, the IEP or the catalogue's description is marked unchecked.

- **The whole philosophy.** A. P. Martinich & Kinch Hoekstra (eds.), *The Oxford Handbook of Hobbes* (Oxford University Press, 2016), the place to start a literature search on one argument. S. A. Lloyd (ed.), *The Bloomsbury Companion to Hobbes* (Bloomsbury, 2013, per the SEP). Noel Malcolm, *Aspects of Hobbes* (Clarendon Press, 2002): his collected essays, among them the Cotton attribution and "Hobbes and the European Republic of Letters", which the SEP cites. Martinich, *A Hobbes Dictionary* (Blackwell, 1995), for terms.
- **Mind, language and method (Week 2; Arguments 1–3).** Philip Pettit, *Made with Words: Hobbes on Language, Mind, and Politics* (Princeton University Press, 2008). Its catalogue description gives the thesis: Hobbes, not Rousseau or later thinkers, first held that language is a cultural invention that transformed the human mind. The best book for Argument 2. Stewart Duncan (the SEP entry's author), *Materialism from Hobbes to Locke* (Oxford University Press, 2022, per the SEP), for Arguments 1 and 3. Douglas M. Jesseph, *Squaring the Circle* (University of Chicago Press, 1999), on the mathematics and the quarrel with Wallis. Steven Shapin & Simon Schaffer, *Leviathan and the Air-Pump: Hobbes, Boyle, and the Experimental Life* (Princeton University Press, 1985; the SEP cites a 1989 printing), on the Boyle controversy.
- **Rhetoric (Weeks 1, 3).** Quentin Skinner, *Reason and Rhetoric in the Philosophy of Hobbes* (Cambridge University Press; the SEP gives 1996, the earliest catalogue record 1997), on Hobbes's changing view of eloquence between the *Elements* and *Leviathan* (from memory; unchecked). His essays are collected in *Visions of Politics*, vol. 3, *Hobbes and Civil Science* (Cambridge University Press, 2002).
- **The state of nature, the laws of nature and the Fool (Weeks 3–4; Arguments 4–10).** The analytic literature is thickest here, and it divides. On one side, the rational-choice reconstructions: David Gauthier, *The Logic of Leviathan: The Moral and Political Theory of Thomas Hobbes* (Clarendon Press, 1969); Gregory S. Kavka, *Hobbesian Moral and Political Theory* (Princeton University Press, 1986), with his earlier *Ethics* article on the war of all against all (vol. 93, 1983, pp. 291–310, as the SEP's Prisoner's Dilemma entry cites it); and Jean Hampton, *Hobbes and the Social Contract Tradition* (Cambridge University Press, 1986), whose term "alienation contract" for Hobbes's contract the SEP's Contractarianism entry adopts. On the other, readings that refuse to reduce the laws of nature to prudence. Howard Warrender, *The Political Philosophy of Hobbes: His Theory of Obligation* (Clarendon, 1957, per the SEP; the catalogue record is a 1966 printing), reads them as obligating as God's commands (from memory; unchecked), the view Argument 10 weighs. S. A. Lloyd, *Ideals as Interests in Hobbes's Leviathan: The Power of Mind over Matter* (Cambridge University Press, 1992), argues, per its catalogue description, that religious and moral interests able to override the fear of death are central both to Hobbes's account of disorder and to his remedy; her *Morality in the Philosophy of Thomas Hobbes: Cases in the Law of Nature* (Cambridge University Press, 2009) takes the laws of nature case by case. Arash Abizadeh, *Hobbes and the Two Faces of Ethics* (Cambridge University Press, 2018), separates, per its description, prudential reasons of the good from contractual obligations owed to others. Read one book from each side before you write up Arguments 9 and 10.
- **Sovereignty, authorization and liberty (Week 5; Arguments 11–14).** Quentin Skinner, *Hobbes and Republican Liberty* (Cambridge University Press, 2008), on ch. 21's definition of liberty as a reply to the republican writers (from memory; unchecked), the essential reading for Argument 14. Deborah Baumgold, *Hobbes's Political Thought* (Cambridge University Press, 1988, per the SEP). The older classics, for [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md): Leo Strauss, *The Political Philosophy of Hobbes: Its Basis and Its Genesis* (Clarendon Press, 1936); C. B. Macpherson, *The Political Theory of Possessive Individualism: Hobbes to Locke* (Oxford University Press, 1962, per the SEP; not found in the catalogue search); Michael Oakeshott, *Hobbes on Civil Association* (1975; the catalogue gives Blackwell and the University of California Press, the SEP Oxford University Press).
- **Religion (Week 6; Argument 15).** Here readers who take Hobbes's Christianity at its word face readers who find a skeptic writing cautiously (sides as assigned below are from memory; unchecked). A. P. Martinich, *The Two Gods of Leviathan: Thomas Hobbes on Religion and Politics* (Cambridge University Press, 1992), is the main book on the first side; Edwin Curley's essay "'I durst not write so boldly' or, How to Read Hobbes's Theological-Political Treatise" (in D. Bostrenghi (ed.), *Hobbes e Spinoza*, Bibliopolis, 1992, per the SEP, which links a preprint) is the classic on the second. Also Jeffrey R. Collins, *The Allegiance of Thomas Hobbes* (Oxford University Press, 2005), and Tom Holden, *Hobbes's Philosophy of Religion* (Oxford University Press, 2023, per the SEP).
- **Liberty and necessity (Week 7; Argument 16).** Chappell (§1) first. Nicholas D. Jackson, *Hobbes, Bramhall, and the Politics of Liberty and Necessity: A Quarrel of the Civil Wars and Interregnum* (Cambridge University Press, 2007, per the SEP).
- **Context and reception.** Richard Tuck, *Philosophy and Government 1572–1651* (Cambridge University Press, 1993). Jon Parkin, *Taming the Leviathan: The Reception of the Political and Religious Ideas of Thomas Hobbes in England, 1640–1700* (Cambridge University Press, 2007), and Samuel I. Mintz, *The Hunting of Leviathan* (Cambridge University Press, 1962, per the SEP), for [06](06-reception-and-debates.md). Christopher Hill, *The Century of Revolution, 1603–1714* (2nd ed., Routledge, 1980), which the IEP calls the classic history of England's civil war and its repercussions, for [01](01-life-and-times.md).

## 4. Reference

- **[SEP, "Thomas Hobbes"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/hobbes/)** — by Stewart Duncan (first published 11 March 2009; substantive revision 1 March 2025). Everything but the politics: life and works, mind and language (with reasoning as computation), materialism, method, philosophy of religion, reception. Read it before Week 2.
- **[SEP, "Hobbes's Moral and Political Philosophy"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/hobbes-moral/)** — by Sharon A. Lloyd and Susanne Sreedhar (first published 12 February 2002; substantive revision 12 September 2022). The politics, with a long selected bibliography by topic. Read its opening sections before Week 3, the rest after Week 5. Satellite entries, by week:
  - ["Prisoner's Dilemma"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/prisoner-dilemma/) (Steven Kuhn; revised 24 October 2025), Weeks 3–4 and Argument 9. It reports Brian Skyrms's and Peter Vanderschraaf's claim that Hobbes and Hume both identified the strategy game theorists call GRIM as what underlies cooperation in important dilemma-like situations.
  - ["Contractarianism"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/contractarianism/) (Ann Cudd & Seena Eftekhari; revised 22 August 2025), Weeks 4–5: it separates contract theories of political authority from contract theories of morality.
  - ["Legal Positivism"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/legal-positivism/) (Leslie Green & Thomas Adams; revised 10 October 2025), Week 5 and Argument 10.
  - ["Compatibilism"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/compatibilism/) (Michael McKenna & D. Justin Coates; revised 16 April 2024), Week 7 and Argument 16; it calls Hobbes an exemplary expression of classical compatibilism.

  Free; budget an hour or two per entry (an estimate). There is no SEP entry on Bramhall at the obvious address.
- **[IEP, "Hobbes, Thomas: Moral and Political Philosophy"](https://iep.utm.edu/hobmoral/)** — by Garrath Williams: the plainer first pass, with a short annotated reading list (the source of the IEP verdicts in §§1–3). ["Hobbes, Thomas: Methodology"](https://iep.utm.edu/hobmeth/) (Stephen Finn), on the geometrical method and the histories, Weeks 1 and 7. ["Social Contract Theory"](https://iep.utm.edu/soc-cont/) (Celeste Friend), Week 4. The IEP pages show no publication date, and there is no general IEP "Hobbes" entry at the obvious address.
- **[PhilPapers, "Thomas Hobbes"](https://philpapers.org/browse/thomas-hobbes)** — for finding articles on one argument.
- ***Hobbes Studies*,** the annual journal the SEP names, and Perez Zagorin's critical bibliography of the scholarship to 1990, "Hobbes on Our Mind" (*Journal of the History of Ideas* 51, 1990), which the SEP recommends.

## 5. Media (verified November 2026; assignments in [07-study-plan.md](07-study-plan.md))

Each item was checked on its own page or in its show's feed; lengths are as stated there. The assigned audio comes to about 1 hour 45 minutes by those lengths (*Philosophize This!* eps. 26–27, 60 minutes together, and the 45-minute *In Our Time*), plus three Yale lectures whose pages state no running time. Listen after your own reconstruction, never instead of it.

- ***History of Philosophy Without Any Gaps*** (Peter Adamson; free). **The series has not reached Hobbes.** Its [Podbean feed](https://feed.podbean.com/hopwag/feed.xml), read 13 November 2026, ends at ep. 503, ["Free Market: Dutch Capitalism"](https://hopwag.podbean.com/e/hop-503-free-market-dutch-capitalism/) (8 November 2026), and no episode title or description in it mentions Hobbes, which matches the learning path's note that the series sits in the mid-seventeenth century. Useful background for Week 2 is the Descartes block from ep. 466, ["Well Hidden: Descartes' Life and Works"](https://hopwag.podbean.com/e/hop-466-well-hidden-descartes-life-and-works/) (30 March 2025; 20 min). Check the feed before you start; a Hobbes run, when it comes, belongs after Week 7.
- ***Philosophize This!*** (Stephen West; free). Checked on their own libsyn pages; lengths from the feed:
  - ep. 26, ["Thomas Hobbes pt. 1 - The Social Contract"](https://philosophizethis.libsyn.com/episode-026-thomas-hobbes-pt-1-the-social-contract) (17 June 2014; 30 min 31 s): life without laws, whether humans are selfish, why society needs a sovereign. Preview before Week 3.
  - ep. 27, ["Thomas Hobbes pt. 2"](https://philosophizethis.libsyn.com/episode-027-thomas-hobbes-pt-2) (1 July 2014; 29 min 36 s); its page says only that it continues the discussion. Before Week 5.
  - Optional: ep. 45, ["Rousseau Government pt. 1"](https://philosophizethis.libsyn.com/episode-045-rousseau-government-pt-1) (15 December 2014; 27 min 13 s), Hobbes's and Rousseau's states of nature side by side, after Week 4.

  West is a skilled popularizer, not an academic: preview and review with him, never reconstruct from him.
- **BBC Radio 4, *In Our Time*** — free on BBC Sounds; dates, guests and lengths from each programme page.
  - **Assigned:** ["Hobbes"](https://www.bbc.co.uk/programmes/p003k9l1) (1 December 2005; 45 minutes; Melvyn Bragg with Quentin Skinner, David Wootton and Annabel Brett), after Week 5. The podcast file in the show's feed is shorter (28 minutes by its stated length).
  - **Optional:** ["The Social Contract"](https://www.bbc.co.uk/programmes/b008w3xm) (7 February 2008; 45 minutes; with Melissa Lane, Susan James and Karen O'Brien), after Week 4; ["The Putney Debates"](https://www.bbc.co.uk/programmes/b01rw1k7) (18 April 2013; 42 minutes) and ["The Levellers"](https://www.bbc.co.uk/programmes/m002wkhq) (2026; 55 minutes; with Teresa Bejan, Ted Vallance and Clare Jackson), on the radicals of the 1640s, Weeks 1 and 5; ["Thucydides"](https://www.bbc.co.uk/programmes/b050bcf1) (29 January 2015; 43 minutes), Week 1; ["Robert Boyle"](https://www.bbc.co.uk/programmes/b0460p63) (12 June 2014; 47 minutes), Week 2; ["Free Will"](https://www.bbc.co.uk/programmes/b00z5y9z) (10 March 2011; 45 minutes; with Simon Blackburn, Helen Beebee and Galen Strawson), Week 7. The Levellers page and the feed give different dates (21 May and 18 June 2026), so only the year is given.
- **Yale, PLSC 114, *Introduction to Political Philosophy*** (Steven B. Smith; [Open Yale Courses](https://oyc.yale.edu/political-science/plsc-114), free, with transcripts and audio). The course page says it was videotaped in Fall 2006 and taught in 50-minute sessions. Three lectures, all titled "The Sovereign State: Hobbes, *Leviathan*":
  - [Lecture 12](https://oyc.yale.edu/political-science/plsc-114/lecture-12): who Hobbes was, Hobbes against Machiavelli and Aristotle, and the "Great Question" of what makes legitimate authority possible. Week 1.
  - [Lecture 13](https://oyc.yale.edu/political-science/plsc-114/lecture-13): the state of nature, pride and fear, the laws of nature. After Week 4.
  - [Lecture 14](https://oyc.yale.edu/political-science/plsc-114/lecture-14): sovereignty as an office, law as the sovereign's command, Hobbes and the modern state. After Week 5.

  The learning path's optional "PLSC 114 lectures 12–17 (Hobbes, Locke)" is these three and three on Locke. Smith says in Lecture 12 that he assigned only a few of the opening chapters, and no chapter list names religion or free will, so the course serves Weeks 1 and 3–5 only.
- **Wi-Phi (Wireless Philosophy)** — free animated videos by academics. A search of the site for Hobbes returns only the [*Democracy* module](https://www.wi-phi.com/modules/democracy/), led by Geoff Pynn, whose stated learning outcomes include considering Mozi's and Hobbes's arguments for the necessity of the state. The quiz after its first lesson, "The Anarchist's Challenge", asks about Hobbes's state of nature; the second lesson, "The Basis of State Authority", is on the social contract. Before Week 3. No running times are stated.
- No free academic lecture course on Hobbes alone was verified.
