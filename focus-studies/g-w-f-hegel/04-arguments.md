# Hegel: The Arguments
**English** | [简体中文](../../zh/focus-studies/g-w-f-hegel/04-arguments.md)

*Focus study · [Dossier home](README.md) · Library entry: [G. W. F. Hegel](../../eras/04-19th-century/g-w-f-hegel.md) · The system these arguments build: [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md) · Learning path: [Stage 4](../../learning-path.md)*

Each argument below is reconstructed in numbered premises in the house style: the text and its setting, the reconstruction, what each premise rests on, the strongest objection, and the best reply. The reply is Hegel's own where he gave one and the literature's where he did not, and it is labeled either way. Where scholars disagree about what the argument even is, the readings are set out with names on each side. Reconstruct each argument yourself, book closed, before reading the version here; the reconstructions are a check on your own, not a substitute for it.

Reconstructing Hegel in numbered premises needs a word of warning. Hegel thought that the form of a list of premises and a conclusion was itself one-sided, because it treats the steps as fixed counters that a reasoner moves around from outside, when for him the movement belongs to the subject matter. The Preface says so, and Argument 2 takes it up. The reconstructions here are a reader's tool, not a claim that Hegel argues like Hobbes. Where the premises seem to smooth over a transition that the text makes abruptly, say so in your own notes; most of the scholarly disputes below start at exactly such a transition. The popular formula "thesis, antithesis, synthesis" is not a key to these arguments. The three terms are Fichte's, they were spread as a summary of Hegel by Heinrich Moritz Chalybäus, and Hegel never used them together to name the steps of an argument of his own; the formula is set out as the misreading it is in [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md) and in the library entry.

The *Phenomenology of Spirit* (1807) argues in a series, and the series is the reason to read it in order. Each "shape of consciousness" takes a standard of knowledge for granted, tests itself against that standard, and fails by its own lights; the failure leaves a new standard, which the next shape takes up. Arguments 1 and 2 come from the Preface, where Hegel says what the series is for: the true is the whole, and the whole is a result, not a starting point; and the whole is not only substance but subject. Arguments 3 to 5 are the three chapters on consciousness, in which the knower looks for truth in an object over against it: the bare "this" of sense-certainty, the thing with many properties, and the forces and laws of the understanding. Each fails, and the failure of the third turns consciousness back on itself. Arguments 6 to 8 are the opening of the chapter on self-consciousness, the most read pages Hegel wrote: self-consciousness as desire, the struggle for recognition, and lordship and bondage. Arguments 9 to 16 carry the story through the freedom of self-consciousness, spirit, logic, right and history.

The learning path reads only the Preface in selection and the lordship and bondage section (¶¶178–196), and says plainly that the full *Phenomenology* is not a solo book. This file goes further into the text on purpose, in guided sections, never cover to cover, and the study plan's week limits are the guardrail. Arguments 1 and 2 belong to week 1 of the study plan, Arguments 3 to 5 to week 2, and Arguments 6 to 8 to week 3.

**Citations and spelling.** The *Phenomenology* is cited as PhG with the paragraph number of the standard Miller and Pinkard numbering: (PhG ¶178) means paragraph 178. Quotations come from J. B. Baillie's translation, *The Phenomenology of Mind* (2 vols., London: Swan Sonnenschein, 1910), because it is in the public domain and anyone can check it, and the Baillie page is added where the scan shows it: (PhG ¶178; Baillie p. 175). Baillie prints no paragraph numbers. The numbers here were matched to his paragraphs with the numbered transcription of Baillie's text at marxists.org, which follows the Miller numbering, and checked against the 1910 scan; where a paragraph could not be pinned, the section heading is cited instead. Baillie translates *Geist* as "mind" and *Begriff* as "notion", capitalizes freely, and spells the British way ("realise", "labour"); quotations keep his words, spelling and capitals exactly. The scan's text was produced by machine reading, and where it misreads a letter (most often "h" for "li", as in "reahty" for "reality") the quotation gives the printed word and the quote log records the correction. Nothing that is a paraphrase is put in quotation marks. Modern translations are in copyright and are not quoted, and later writers whose English is in copyright (Kojève, Marx's translators, the modern scholars) are paraphrased.

The reading editions are different from the quoted one. Read the *Phenomenology* in A. V. Miller's translation (Oxford, 1977), with J. N. Findlay's paragraph-by-paragraph analysis printed at the back, or in Terry Pinkard's (Cambridge, 2018), which is more accurate and more readable; both print the paragraph numbers used here. Baillie's English is a Victorian idealist's, and it is quoted here for its legal status, not as the best guide to the German. Where Baillie's choice of a word matters to an argument (his "mind" for *Geist*, his "acknowledged" and "recognised" for *anerkannt*), the argument says so. Baillie also opens many sections with a bracketed summary of his own; those summaries are the translator's, and nothing here quotes them as Hegel.

**Argument 1. The true is the whole: against beginning from the immediate (PhG Preface)**

*What it proves:* that philosophy cannot start from a first principle, an immediate certainty or an intuition of the Absolute and derive everything else from it; that the truth of a philosophical claim is the whole development that reaches it; and that a science of knowledge must therefore begin from the poorest standpoint consciousness has and earn every step after it.

**Text and setting.** The Preface stands in front of the book but argues about the book as a whole, and much of it is polemic. Its targets are three ways of starting philosophy that were current in Jena around 1806. The first is the method of first principles, in which philosophy sets out a single highest proposition and deduces the rest; Reinhold and Fichte had both built systems that way. The second is the philosophy of the Absolute as an undifferentiated identity, reached by intellectual intuition, which Schelling and his followers taught; the SEP article on Hegel records that Schelling took a barbed remark in the Preface as aimed at him and that their friendship ended over it. The third, set out in the Introduction that follows (PhG ¶73–74), is Kant's procedure of examining the faculty of knowledge before using it. Against all three the Preface makes one claim: a philosophy that begins by having its truth in hand has not got the truth at all. Read ¶¶1–5, 14–17, 20 and 24–27 for this argument, and the opening of the Introduction (¶¶73–76) for the attack on Kant.

1. A philosophical truth cannot be separated from the process that reaches it, although a preface that announces results invites the reader to think it can (PhG ¶¶1–3). The stages of a development "supplant one another as being incompatible with one another", as the blossom refutes the bud and the fruit the blossom, yet they are "moments of an organic unity", and "this equal necessity of all moments constitutes from the outset the life of the whole" (PhG ¶2; Baillie pp. 2–3).
2. Any starting point stated immediately is "merely the universal", an empty word that has not yet been developed: "If we say 'all animals,' that does not pass for zoology" (PhG ¶20; Baillie p. 18).
3. So a first principle of philosophy, "even if it is true, is yet none the less false just because and in so far as it is merely a fundamental proposition, merely a first principle" (PhG ¶24; Baillie p. 21).
4. Nor can philosophy begin with an intuition of the Absolute in which all differences vanish. To set the bare assertion that in the Absolute all is one against the whole of determinate knowledge, and "to give out its Absolute as the night in which, as we say, all cows are black—that is the very naivete of vacuous knowledge" (PhG ¶16; Baillie p. 15). Such enthusiasm "starts straight off with absolute knowledge, as if shot out of a pistol" (PhG ¶27; Baillie p. 25).
5. Nor can it begin, as Kant did, by testing knowledge as an instrument or medium before using it, since that procedure already takes for granted that knowledge stands on one side and the Absolute on the other; we should "take care lest the fear of error is not just the initial error" (PhG ¶74; Baillie p. 75).

Therefore "The truth is the whole. The whole, however, is merely the essential nature reaching its completeness through the process of its own development" (PhG ¶20; Baillie p. 17). Of the Absolute it must be said that it is "essentially a result", that "only at the end is it what it is in very truth" (PhG ¶20; Baillie p. 17). A science of knowledge has to begin where consciousness actually is, with "sense-consciousness", and for that consciousness "a long and laborious journey must be undertaken" (PhG ¶27; Baillie p. 25). That journey is the *Phenomenology*, and Argument 3 is its first step.

**What each premise rests on.** Premise 1 rests on an organic model of truth taken from the theory of living things: Hegel cites Aristotle's account of nature as purposive activity a few paragraphs later (PhG ¶22), and the background is Kant's account of organisms in the *Critique of Judgment*, in which every part is both end and means. The model is offered, not proved; the Preface says outright that only the finished system can justify it (PhG ¶17). Premise 2 is a logical point about abstraction. A universal on its own leaves open everything that matters, so it cannot yet be either true or false of anything definite. Premise 3 applies premise 2 to the method of first principles. Its target is the idea that justification runs in one direction, from a self-evident ground to everything grounded on it. Premise 4 is the polemic against the philosophy of identity, and it shows what Hegel thinks is wrong with the Absolute of his former ally: it is reached by declaring differences unreal, not by working through them. Premise 5 is a dilemma against Kant's critical starting point. If knowledge is an instrument, it alters what it grasps; if it is a medium, it refracts it; either way the critique assumes an Absolute cut off from knowledge, which is the very thing in question (PhG ¶73–74). The Introduction's alternative is that consciousness carries its own standard with it and tests itself against it, so that the philosopher need only look on (PhG ¶¶84–85).

**Objection.** The strongest objection is that the argument leaves philosophy with no place to stand. If nothing is true until the whole is complete, then every claim made along the way, including the claims of the Preface, is provisional at best, and the beginning of the system is either arbitrary or secretly borrowed from the end. Schelling pressed a version of this in his Munich and Berlin lectures. As the SEP article reports, he criticized the rationalism of Hegel's philosophy, and his reading of Hegel shaped the later picture of a thinker who claimed to deduce reality from pure thought; the charge was that such a philosophy can only reconstruct what it already knows, and so gives no account of why anything exists at all. Kierkegaard, who heard Schelling in Berlin in 1841–42, gave the objection its existential form: a system may be possible for a logician, but no existing person ever stands at the end of it, so a truth that is only true of the whole is true for no one who lives. Feuerbach's 1839 critique added that the *Phenomenology*'s supposed presuppositionless start is a staged one, since the author already knows where it must come out.

**Reply.** Hegel's own, in part, and the literature's. Hegel accepts the circle and denies that it is vicious. The Preface calls true reality "the circle which presupposes its end or its purpose, and has its end for its beginning" (PhG ¶18; Baillie p. 16), and it says that the opening claims are anticipations that only the system can justify (PhG ¶17). The beginning is not arbitrary, because the *Phenomenology* does not begin with a principle of Hegel's choosing. It begins with the standpoint that every reader occupies already, the claim that the most certain knowledge is the plain awareness of what is here and now, and it tests that standpoint by its own standard. The reply to Kant in premise 5 is meant to work the same way: the examination of knowledge is not dropped, but carried out from inside, with no standard imported from outside (PhG ¶84). Against Kierkegaard, a holist can reply that being provisional is not the same as being false; the parts of the system are not empty before the end, only incomplete. The reply leaves a debt. Whether Hegel can show that each stage really does generate the next, rather than being arranged by an author who knows the destination, has to be judged argument by argument, starting with sense-certainty.

**Where scholars disagree.** Three points are open. (a) Is the holism metaphysical or epistemic? On the metaphysical readings, the traditional one that Beiser defends and the revised one of Houlgate and Stern, "the whole" is reality itself, the Absolute as a self-developing totality, and the Preface states a metaphysical thesis about it. On the post-Kantian reading of Pippin and Pinkard, the whole is the space of justification: no claim is warranted in isolation, and every standard of knowledge is answerable to others, so that Hegel radicalizes Kant's critique instead of returning to pre-critical metaphysics. SEP notes that the older label "non-metaphysical" for the second camp was dropped because both camps grant Hegel some metaphysics; the dispute is over which kind. (b) What becomes of the beginning? Houlgate holds that Hegel's logic, at least, begins without presuppositions, with thought as pure being (SEP, citing Houlgate 2022), while Pippin holds that it begins from thought's own discursive form, its asserting that something is the case (SEP, citing Pippin 2019). (c) How much of the Preface is about Schelling? Older readers took the "night in which ... all cows are black" as a direct attack on him. Others, following Hegel's own later letter to Schelling, read it as aimed at his imitators; the Preface itself speaks of a formalism that has "arisen once more in philosophy itself" (PhG ¶16; Baillie p. 15), which fits either reading.

**Argument 2. Substance as subject (PhG Preface ¶¶17–25)**

*What it proves:* that what is ultimately real has to be understood not only as substance, a self-sufficient being that simply is what it is, but also as subject, an activity that differentiates itself, relates to itself in its differences and comes to know itself; and that the name for reality understood this way is spirit.

**Text and setting.** This is the Preface's programmatic thesis, announced as a view "which the developed exposition of the system itself can alone justify" (PhG ¶17; Baillie p. 15). It answers a question every post-Kantian was asking. Spinoza had made God the one substance and everything else its modes; Kant and Fichte had made the thinking subject the source of the order of experience; Schelling had tried to unite the two in an Absolute that is neither subject nor object. Hegel takes each in turn in ¶17, and Baillie's footnotes name the targets: Spinoza, then Kant and Fichte, then Schelling. The paragraphs that follow (¶¶18–25) say what being subject means, and ¶32, on the "power of the negative", is the best gloss on them. The argument is the bridge from Argument 1 to everything after it: if the whole is a result, something has to produce the result, and Hegel's answer is that the whole produces itself.

1. Spinoza's God, conceived as "the One Substance", shocked his contemporaries for a reason: "in such a conception self-consciousness was simply submerged, and not preserved" (PhG ¶17; Baillie pp. 15–16).
2. The opposite view, which takes thinking to be merely subjective, "is exactly the same bare uniformity", and an intellectual intuition that unites thought and being risks falling "back into that inert, abstract simplicity" (PhG ¶17; Baillie p. 16). So neither substance alone, nor the subject alone, nor their immediate identity will do.
3. "The living substance, further, is that being which is truly subject", truly actual "solely in the process of positing itself", and "As subject it is pure and simple negativity": it splits what is simple into opposed terms and then negates that opposition (PhG ¶18; Baillie p. 16).
4. A unity that has never gone through difference is empty. The life of God can be spoken of as "love disporting with itself", but that idea becomes insipid "if it lacks the seriousness, the suffering, the patience, and the labour of the negative" (PhG ¶19; Baillie p. 17).
5. This is what Aristotle meant in calling nature purposive: "reason is purposive activity", and the result is the same as the beginning because the beginning was a purpose (PhG ¶22; Baillie p. 19).
6. A proposition such as "God is the eternal" states that the Absolute is subject but cannot show it, because in such a proposition "The subject is taken to be a fixed point", and the predicates are attached to it by the knower from outside (PhG ¶23; Baillie p. 20).

Therefore truth is only actual "in the form of system", and "that substance is essentially subject, is expressed in the idea which represents the Absolute as Spirit (Geist)—the grandest conception of all, and one which is due to modern times and its religion" (PhG ¶25; Baillie p. 22). "Spirit is the only Reality" (PhG ¶25; Baillie p. 22), and it is real as the process of making itself its own object and knowing itself in that object.

**What each premise rests on.** Premise 1 is an interpretation of Spinoza that most of Hegel's generation shared after the pantheism controversy of the 1780s: a monism of substance leaves no room for freedom or for the self as a source of anything. Premise 2 is Hegel's verdict on the idealism of Kant and Fichte and on Schelling's philosophy of identity; it repeats the charge of Argument 1 that an undifferentiated Absolute is empty. Premise 3 is the heart of the argument and the least argued part of it. "Negativity" is Hegel's name for the activity of determining: to be something definite is to exclude, so a being that determines itself is one that sets itself against itself and then recognizes itself in what it has set against itself. The model is the self-conscious I, which knows itself only by distinguishing itself, as knower, from itself, as known. Premise 4 adds the Christian story of a God who becomes other than himself in the incarnation and returns to himself in spirit, a story Hegel reads as philosophy in pictorial form. Premise 5 rests on Aristotle's account of natural ends. Premise 6 is the first statement of Hegel's critique of the ordinary judgment, developed later in the Preface into an account of the speculative proposition (PhG ¶¶60–66), in which the subject passes into the predicate instead of standing still under it.

**Objection.** Feuerbach made the objection that the Young Hegelians and Marx took over. In his "Provisional Theses for the Reform of Philosophy" (1842) he argued that Hegel inverts the true relation of subject and predicate. Real subjects are finite beings, human beings and nature; thought and spirit are their predicates, things they do. Hegel makes thought the subject and real beings its predicates, and so turns the activity of human minds into the life of a divine mind that exists nowhere. On this reading "substance as subject" is theology in logical dress, and the way to read Hegel truly is to turn him the right way up. Marx applied the same inversion to the *Philosophy of Right* in his 1843 critique. The objection has an analytic version too. Talk of a "substance" that "posits itself" either attributes mental acts to the universe, which is mysticism, or is a metaphor whose literal content has not been given, and Russell, as SEP reports, dismissed Hegel's metaphysics as resting on a logic that the new logic of Frege and Peano had overthrown.

**Reply.** The literature's, in two forms that compete. The first reply accepts that the thesis is metaphysical and defends the metaphysics. Hegel does not attribute a mind to the cosmos as a person; he claims that reality has the structure of a self-developing whole whose highest form is human self-knowledge, so that the Absolute knows itself in and through finite knowers, not apart from them. That is not Feuerbach's mystified God but something close to his own humanism with a teleology added, which is why, on this reply, Feuerbach's "inversion" is less a refutation than a change of emphasis. The second reply denies that the thesis is a claim about a cosmic subject at all. "Subject" here means a self-determining activity whose standards are its own, and the claim that substance is subject says that the norms by which we know and act are not given to us by a world independent of us, nor imposed by individual minds, but are instituted and revised by a community that comes to understand them as its own. On this reply the thesis is the program for the rest of the *Phenomenology*, in which "substance" becomes the ethical world of a people (Argument 10 and the chapter on spirit) and "subject" the self-conscious agents who live in it.

**Where scholars disagree.** The dispute between those two replies is the main division in Hegel scholarship, and SEP presents it as live. (a) On the metaphysical side, Charles Taylor's *Hegel* (1975) reads spirit as a cosmic subject that posits the world as the condition of its own self-expression, and Frederick Beiser (*Hegel*, 2005) reads the thesis as an organic monism: Spinoza's one substance reconceived as a living, purposive whole. Stephen Houlgate and Robert Stern defend a revised metaphysical reading in which the structures of thought are the structures of being, without a cosmic person. (b) On the post-Kantian side, Robert Pippin (*Hegel's Idealism*, 1989) reads "substance as subject" as Hegel's development of Kant's claim that the unity of experience is the work of a self-conscious subject, made social and historical; Terry Pinkard (*Hegel's Phenomenology: The Sociality of Reason*, 1994) reads it as the claim that reason is a social practice of giving and asking for reasons; and Robert Brandom (*A Spirit of Trust*, 2019) reads it as an account of how conceptual norms are instituted by reciprocal recognition. SEP notes that the older name "non-metaphysical" for this camp has been given up, since Pippin too grants Hegel a metaphysics, in a new sense. (c) What the thesis owes to theology is a third question. Readers such as Taylor and Beiser treat the Christian background as essential; others treat it as Hegel's illustration, and Kojève made the thesis openly atheist, reading spirit as humanity making itself through history.

**Argument 3. Sense-certainty refutes itself (PhG ¶¶90–110)**

*What it proves:* that the claim to know a bare particular, a "this", immediately and without concepts, cannot be made good on its own terms; that whatever sense-certainty can say or point to is a universal; and so that even the most immediate knowledge is mediated.

**Text and setting.** This is the first shape of consciousness, and the *Phenomenology*'s first full example of its method. Sense-certainty is the view that the most certain and the richest knowledge is the plain awareness of what is here and now, before any concept has been applied to it. It is not one philosopher's doctrine but a standing temptation of empiricism; the SEP article on Hegel compares its objects to the sense-data of early twentieth-century analytic epistemology, and its lesson to Sellars's later attack on the "myth of the given". Hegel lets sense-certainty state its own standard and then tests it by that standard, which is what the Introduction promised (Argument 1). The chapter falls into three rounds: truth located in the object (¶¶95–99), in the I (¶¶100–102), and in the whole act of pointing (¶¶103–108), followed by a reflection on what the rounds show (¶¶109–110).

1. Sense-certainty takes itself to be "knowledge of the immediate, of what is", and so seems "the richest kind of knowledge" (PhG ¶¶90–91; Baillie p. 90).
2. To be knowledge at all, it must be able to say what it knows. Ask it: "What is the Now?" The answer is, for example, "the Now is night-time". But if we "write that truth down", since "A truth cannot lose anything by being written down", and look again at noon, "it has turned stale and become out of date" (PhG ¶95; Baillie p. 93).
3. What survives is a Now that is indifferent to being night or day, "not something immediate but something mediated" (PhG ¶96; Baillie p. 93). The same holds for the Here, which is a tree when I face one way and a house when I turn round.
4. In saying "this", "now" or "it is", we utter a universal and fail to say what we mean. "Language, however, as we see, is the more truthful", and "universality is the real truth of sense-certainty" (PhG ¶97; Baillie p. 94).
5. Locating the truth in the I fares no better. I see the tree and another sees the house, and "the one certainty disappears in the other"; the I that remains, "I is merely universal", like Now and Here (PhG ¶¶101–102; Baillie p. 96).
6. Pointing, the last resort, is itself a process: the Now pointed out has already been, and the Here pointed out is a Here of many Heres (PhG ¶¶105–108).

Therefore sense-certainty's object is a universal, its claim to know bare particulars fails by its own test, and the thing it hoped to grasp outside language is "nothing else than what is untrue, irrational, something barely and simply 'meant'" (PhG ¶110; Baillie p. 102; Baillie prints "what is called unspeakable" before this phrase). What sense-certainty learns is to take its object as a this-with-properties, a thing, which is the next shape, perception.

**What each premise rests on.** Premise 1 is sense-certainty's own claim, stated sympathetically; Hegel adds at once that this richest knowledge is also "the abstractest and the poorest kind of truth" (PhG ¶91; Baillie p. 90), because all it can say of its object is that it is. Premise 2 rests on a principle sense-certainty must accept if it is knowledge and not a feeling: a truth does not become false by being kept. Premise 3 rests on the structure of indexical words. "Now" and "here" pick out different times and places on different occasions, so what they share is a general rule, not a particular. Premise 4 is the claim that there is no saying without universals, and Hegel adds a joke at the expense of Wilhelm Traugott Krug, who had challenged the idealists to deduce his pen: anyone who asks philosophy to deduce "a so-called this thing" must first say which thing he means, and that "is quite impossible" (PhG ¶102; Baillie p. 96; Baillie's note points to the *Encyclopaedia*, §250; the SEP article tells the story of Krug's pen). Premise 5 repeats the test on the subject side. Premise 6 shows that even a gesture, the most immediate way of indicating something, has a structure of before and after.

**Objection.** Feuerbach's 1839 critique of Hegel made the objection that has lasted. Hegel refutes the word "this", not the thing. The sensible particular is not refuted by the fact that the word "this" applies to every particular; it is simply not something that language exists to capture. Sensuous being is real for a sensuous being, the man who sees and eats, and the dialectic of the Now shows only that thought cannot do what sense does. Hegel had anticipated part of this. He says that people who insist on the reality of sense objects should be "sent back to the most elementary school of wisdom", the mysteries of eating bread and drinking wine, and that "Even animals are not shut off from this wisdom", since they do not stand in awe of sensible things but "fall-to without more ado and eat them up" (PhG ¶109; Baillie p. 101). Feuerbach's point survives the joke: eating a thing presupposes that it is there, and that is what sense-certainty claimed. Philosophy of language supplies a second, more technical objection. A demonstrative sentence such as "now it is night" expresses a different proposition on each occasion of use, so writing it down and reading it at noon does not test the original claim; it produces a new and false one. On this objection Hegel confuses the sentence with what an utterance of it says.

**Reply.** The literature's, on Hegel's behalf. Both objections concede the point Hegel needs. Sense-certainty did not claim that particulars exist, which Hegel never denies; it claimed to *know* them, fully and without any general concept, and to know them better than any later shape could. Feuerbach's reply gives up that claim: if the particular is had in sensation and eating but cannot be said, then the certainty of sense is not knowledge. The technical objection gives it up too. To say which proposition "now it is night" expresses on a given occasion one needs a rule that takes contexts to times, and grasping the time thus picked out requires locating it in an order of earlier and later. That rule and that order are exactly the universal and the mediation Hegel says sense-certainty cannot do without. The argument is therefore best read not as a refutation of particulars, nor as a thesis about indexicals, but as a demonstration that singular reference depends on a framework of general concepts, which is the Kantian lesson that intuitions without concepts are blind, reached without Kant's division of the mind into separate faculties. That is how the SEP article reads it.

**Where scholars disagree.** (a) Is the chapter epistemology or ontology? Charles Taylor's essay "The Opening Arguments of the *Phenomenology*" (1972) reads it as a transcendental argument about the conditions of any knowledge of particulars. Robert Pippin and Robert Brandom read it as the first step of a theory of concepts: Brandom treats the failure of sense-certainty as showing that determinate content requires relations of incompatibility, the "determinate negation" that drives the rest of the book. Stephen Houlgate (*Hegel's Phenomenology of Spirit: A Reader's Guide*, 2013) insists that the chapter is also about what there is: the object, not only our talk of it, proves to be universal. (b) How close is Hegel to Sellars? SEP reports the comparison with the "myth of the given"; John McDowell has developed it, and others, including Kenneth Westphal, have argued that Hegel's point is a realist one that Sellarsian readings underplay. (c) Does the argument beg the question by making language the judge? Feuerbach said yes, and the existentialist line that runs through Kierkegaard holds more generally that an existing individual's certainty is not settled by what can be said. Defenders reply that sense-certainty itself appealed to the standard of truth, and a truth is something said.

**Argument 4. Perception: the thing and its many properties (PhG ¶¶111–131)**

*What it proves:* that the commonsense picture of a thing as a single unit that has many independent properties cannot be held together on its own terms, because the thing's unity and its many properties keep displacing one another; and that a thing is what it is only through its relations to other things, which points beyond perception to an account of objects in terms of forces and laws.

**Text and setting.** Perception (*Wahrnehmung*, literally "taking-true") begins with the result of Argument 3: it "takes what exists for it to be a universal" (PhG ¶111; Baillie p. 104), and its object is "the thing with many properties" (PhG ¶112; Baillie p. 106). This is the everyday metaphysics of medium-sized objects, and the philosophical problem in it is very old: is a thing a bundle of qualities, or a substrate in which qualities inhere? The SEP article on Hegel notes that the chapter takes up both pictures, the first associated with Plato and the second with Aristotle, and with them the early modern versions in Locke and Hume. The chapter is also where Hegel defines his most famous word. The sensible "this" has been superseded, and that is not nothing but "a determinate nothing"; for "to supersede (aufheben) is at once to negate and to preserve" (PhG ¶113; Baillie pp. 106–107).

1. The object of perception is both a "one", a unit that excludes other things, and an "also", a medium in which many universal properties coexist without disturbing each other: "This salt is a simple Here and at the same time manifold: it is white, and also pungent, also cubical in shape, also of a specific weight, and so on" (PhG ¶113; Baillie p. 107).
2. Perception's standard of truth is the self-identity of the object. Anything in its experience that conflicts with that identity must therefore be laid to its own account, and consciousness becomes aware of "the possibility of deception" (PhG ¶116; Baillie p. 110).
3. First strategy: the thing is one, and the diversity is ours. "This thing, then, is, in point of fact, merely white to our eyes, also sharp to our tongue, and also cubical to our feeling, and so on" (PhG ¶119; Baillie p. 113). But each property is determinate only in opposition to others (white to black), and it is through its determinate properties that the thing excludes other things; so the properties belong to the thing after all (PhG ¶120).
4. Second strategy: the thing is the "also", a collection of independent properties or "matters", and the unity is ours. "Thus we say of the thing, 'it is white, and also cubical, and also sharp,' and so on. But so far as it is white it is not cubical" (PhG ¶121; Baillie p. 115). Consciousness ends up alternately making both itself and the thing "a pure atomic many-less 'one'" and a set of independent elements (PhG ¶122; Baillie p. 116).
5. Third strategy: the thing is one in itself and many for others, or has one essential property and many inessential ones. But the inessential properties are still necessary to the thing, and that is "a distinction that only exists in words" (PhG ¶127; Baillie p. 119). The thing is what it is "so far as" it relates to others, and its relating to others is part of what it is (PhG ¶¶125–128).

Therefore the unity of the thing and its being for others cannot be kept apart, and the thing as perception conceives it dissolves. The commonsense understanding that tries to hold the two together by "in so far as" clauses is "sound common sense", which "is always poorest where it pretends to be richest" (PhG ¶131; Baillie p. 121). What has emerged is a universal that is no longer tied to sense: the thing as a play of being-for-itself and being-for-another, which the understanding will call force (Argument 5).

**What each premise rests on.** Premise 1 rests on the result of sense-certainty and on ordinary talk: we do say that salt is white and cubical and tart, and we take these as features of one thing. Premise 2 states perception's criterion, which the Introduction says every shape has (PhG ¶84): perception holds that the object is true and its own apprehension may err. Premises 3 and 4 are the two classical theories of the thing, and the argument against each is that it relocates the problem without solving it. Putting the unity in the mind leaves the properties unrelated; putting the plurality in the mind leaves the thing a bare unit with no determinate character. Premise 4 depends on a particular reading of predication: if being white *is* the thing's being, then being cubical, which is not being white, must be another being. Premise 5 rests on the point that a thing's determinate character is relational: to be this definite thing is to differ from other things, and to differ is to be related.

**Objection.** The strongest objection is that the argument trades on a confusion about the word "is". Russell argued that much of Hegel's logic depends on failing to distinguish the "is" of predication ("the salt is white") from the "is" of identity ("the salt is this crystal"). If the salt *is white* in the predicative sense, nothing follows about its *not* being cubical; a thing can fall under many predicates at once without any of them being the thing. The apparent contradiction of premise 4, "so far as it is white it is not cubical", then disappears as soon as one has a logic of predicates and relations, which Frege and Russell supplied and Hegel lacked. The SEP article records Russell's general charge that the new logic had "destroyed" Hegel's metaphysics by overturning the Aristotelian logic on which it was based. A second objection, made by many readers of the chapter, is that the "contradictions" are only tensions within particular philosophical theories of the thing, which a better theory (say, a thing as a substance with modes, or a physical system) could avoid, so that nothing shows that perception as such must fail.

**Reply.** The literature's. Against Russell, Hegel's defenders point out that the argument does not deny that one subject can have many predicates; it asks what makes the many predicates the properties of *one* thing, rather than a heap of qualities that happen to be in the same place. A logic of predicates and relations takes the unity of the subject for granted as the value of a variable; Hegel's question is what that unity consists in, and the formal reply leaves it unanswered. Robert Stern (*Hegel, Kant and the Structure of the Object*, 1990) reads the chapter in this way, as an argument that neither bundle nor substratum explains the unity of an object, and that the solution Hegel later gives is a substance-kind, a universal that is itself the unity of the thing's properties. Against the second objection, the reply is that the argument is meant to show something about any view on which a thing's identity is fixed independently of its relations; better theories of the thing, including physical ones, avoid the contradictions by doing what the next chapter does, explaining properties through forces and laws. The reply leaves a debt. Hegel claims that the movement from perception to understanding is necessary, but premise 5's verdict on "in so far as" clauses is quick, and a reader who thinks qualified predication is harmless will not feel driven anywhere.

**Where scholars disagree.** (a) What is the chapter about? Stern and others treat it as metaphysics of the object, and Houlgate as a demonstration about what things are. Pippin and Pinkard treat it as an argument about the conditions of a knower's claims: what it takes for a consciousness to be entitled to say that the world contains things with properties. (b) Who are the targets? H. S. Harris (*Hegel's Ladder*, 1997) finds Locke, Hume and the natural science of matters (heat, magnetism) behind the "also" and the "matters"; others read the chapter as primarily systematic and see the historical allusions as illustrations. The SEP article links the two classical pictures to Plato and Aristotle. (c) Is Russell's objection fair? Russell's critics, including many analytic Hegelians, reply that Hegel's "contradiction" is not the logical contradiction of a sentence with its negation but the incoherence of a theory under strain. Brandom's inferentialist reading supports the reply. It distinguishes properties that are merely different, such as white and cubical, which one thing can have together, from properties that exclude one another, such as white and black, and reads the chapter as discovering that a thing's identity depends on the second kind of relation, which Hegel calls exclusive difference.

**Argument 5. Force, the understanding and the inverted world (PhG ¶¶132–165)**

*What it proves:* that the scientific understanding, which explains appearances by forces and laws in a world behind the appearances, finds in that inner world nothing but the structure of its own explaining; that its distinction between appearance and a supersensible reality collapses; and that consciousness of an object therefore turns out to be a form of self-consciousness.

**Text and setting.** The chapter is the longest and hardest of the three on consciousness, and the study plan gives it a full sitting on its own. Its subject is the shape of knowledge that the SEP article calls the type of scientific cognition that posits underlying forces to explain what we perceive. Its background is Newtonian physics, the debates over forces and "matters" in the chemistry and electrical theory of around 1800, and Kant's distinction between appearances and things in themselves. The chapter runs in four stages: force and its expression, which turns into a "play of forces" (¶¶132–141); the inner world behind appearance and the kingdom of laws (¶¶142–153); explanation, which Hegel calls tautological (¶¶154–156); and the "inverted world" and infinity (¶¶157–165). Read the first and last stages closely and the middle in outline.

1. The thing of perception has proved to be a unity that exists only in its relations to others. The understanding conceives such a unity as force: something that expresses itself in a multiplicity and withdraws into itself, and that is solicited to expression by another force (PhG ¶¶135–141).
2. Force exists only in this play of forces, so it is never itself perceived; the understanding "looks through the intervening play of forces into the real and true background of things" (PhG ¶143; Baillie p. 136). That background is the inner world, the supersensible beyond.
3. The inner world has no content but what it receives from appearance: "The supersensible is the established truth of the sensible and perceptual", and so "The supersensible is then appearance qua appearance" (PhG ¶147; Baillie p. 140). Its first stable form is the law, and the supersensible world becomes "a quiescent 'kingdom of laws'" (PhG ¶149; Baillie p. 143).
4. Explanation distinguishes a law from the force that grounds it, and then declares the force to have exactly the content of the law. The occurrence of lightning "is apprehended as universal", expressed as the law of electricity, and then explained by electrical force; this is "an explanation that not only explains nothing", but "merely repeats the same thing over again" (PhG ¶¶154–155; Baillie p. 149).
5. Explanation thus shows that the differences in the inner world are differences the understanding makes and then cancels. Taken as a second world, the inner world "has turned round into its opposite" (PhG ¶157; Baillie p. 151): an inverted world in which "what by the law of the first is sweet" is sour, north pole is south, and the "revenge on an enemy" that the first world calls satisfaction becomes punishment, and punishment "the pardoning grace which preserves his being and brings him to honour" (PhG ¶158; Baillie pp. 152–153).
6. The inverted world cannot be a second world beside the first; it is the first world's own inner difference. "We have to think pure flux, opposition within opposition itself, or Contradiction" (PhG ¶160; Baillie p. 155), a self-differentiating unity that Hegel calls infinity and describes as "the soul of the world, the universal life-blood" (PhG ¶162; Baillie p. 157).

Therefore in the inner world "understanding merely experiences itself" (PhG ¶165; Baillie p. 161). A consciousness whose object is a distinguishing that at once cancels its distinctions is "a distinguishing of what is undistinguished, it is Self-consciousness" (PhG ¶164; Baillie p. 161), and "behind the so-called curtain, which is to hide the inner world, there is nothing to be seen unless we ourselves go behind there" (PhG ¶165; Baillie p. 162).

**What each premise rests on.** Premise 1 rests on Argument 4 and on the dynamical theory of matter, which Kant had defended and Schelling made central: matter is constituted by forces of attraction and repulsion, not by inert stuff. Premise 2 rests on the fact that forces are posited, not observed. Premise 3 is the decisive move against Kant: a world of things in themselves of which we know nothing is "empty, for it is merely the nothingness of appearance" (PhG ¶146; Baillie p. 138), and those who say that "the inner being of things cannot be known" are right only about that empty beyond (PhG ¶146; Baillie p. 138). Premise 4 is a criticism of explanation by forces, which Hegel thinks restates the law in the form of a cause; Baillie's note sets beside it Helmholtz's later remark that a force is merely the law of action made into an object. Premise 5 is the hardest. It is partly a reductio of two-world views (if the inner world is a different world, it may as well be the inverse of this one), partly a set of illustrations from magnetism, electricity and punishment. Premise 6 introduces the concept of infinity, a unity that is its own differentiation, which will be the structure of self-consciousness, of life and of spirit.

**Objection.** Two objections are strongest. The first, from the philosophy of science, is that premise 4 is false. Newton's law of gravitation does not merely repeat the falling of this apple; it unifies terrestrial and celestial motion under one law and predicts what has not been observed, and to posit a force with the content of the law is not idle when the law holds across domains. If explanation is not tautological, the understanding has not discovered itself behind the curtain; it has discovered the world. The second objection is Popper's. Premise 6 tells us to "think ... Contradiction" (PhG ¶160). Popper argued in "What is Dialectic?" (1940) that to accept contradictions as true, as a theory of the world and not only of thought, "would mean a complete breakdown of science", since from a contradiction anything follows (SEP, 'Hegel's Dialectics', citing Popper 1940). On this objection the inverted world is not a deepening of science but the point at which Hegel leaves it.

**Reply.** The literature's. To the first objection, the reply concedes that Hegel underrates the unifying power of laws but denies that this touches the main argument. The tautology charge is aimed at a specific philosophical picture, in which forces are hidden things in a second world that make laws true. Unification across domains is a relation among laws, and it says nothing in favor of a supersensible realm of hidden powers; to that extent Hegel's conclusion that the "inner" is the order the understanding finds among appearances is close to a modern regularity or structural view of laws. To the second objection there are two replies, and the SEP entry on dialectics sets out both. Graham Priest accepts that Hegel rejects the law of non-contradiction and defends him: from a contradiction everything follows only if one presupposes that law, so Popper's argument begs the question, and in a paraconsistent logic the acceptance of some contradictions does not require the acceptance of all (SEP, citing Priest 1989). Other defenders deny that Hegel's "contradiction" is the joint truth of a sentence and its negation. It names the structure of a whole whose identity consists in relations to its own opposed moments, as a magnet is a unity of two poles that exist only together, and talk of such a structure licenses no inference to arbitrary conclusions; the SEP entry notes that some sympathetic readers, such as Dieter Wandschneider, grant Popper the logical point and treat dialectical contradiction as only apparent contradiction. The reply leaves a debt: the step from the understanding's self-relation to the claim that consciousness of objects *is* self-consciousness rests on the infinity of premise 6, and readers who do not accept that concept get no further reason here.

**Where scholars disagree.** (a) What is the inverted world? Hans-Georg Gadamer's essay on it (in *Hegel's Dialectic*, 1971) reads it as more than a joke, as Hegel's criticism of a Platonic two-world picture that also contains a moral truth about the reversal of appearances in punishment and grace; Pinkard reads it chiefly as a reductio of the idea that a second, "real" world could explain this one, and H. S. Harris traces its many allusions, from Kant's things in themselves to the satirical tradition of the "world turned upside down". (b) How good is the critique of science? Readers who emphasize Hegel's engagement with the science of his day, such as the essays collected by Houlgate (1998) that SEP cites, defend the chapter as a serious philosophy of laws and forces; others treat its examples as dated and its point as purely conceptual. (c) Why does consciousness become self-consciousness? On Pippin's reading the chapter's result is Kant's thesis that all consciousness of objects is implicitly self-conscious, which Hegel will make social in the next chapter; on more metaphysical readings, such as Houlgate's, the result is that the structure of the object, infinity, is the same as the structure of the self, so that the world is not foreign to thought.

**Argument 6. Self-consciousness is desire, and desire needs another self-consciousness (PhG ¶¶166–177)**

*What it proves:* that self-consciousness is not a quiet inner awareness of oneself but a practical relation to the world, desire, in which the self assures itself of itself by negating what is other; that desire can never achieve this by consuming things; and that self-consciousness can be satisfied only by an object that is itself a self-consciousness, so that the self is social from the start.

**Text and setting.** The chapter opening, headed in Baillie "The Truth which Conscious Certainty of Self Realises", is the hinge of the book. In the three chapters on consciousness "the truth for consciousness is something other than consciousness itself" (PhG ¶166; Baillie p. 164); now there is "a certainty which is on a par with its truth" (PhG ¶166; Baillie p. 164), and we have "passed into the native land of truth" (PhG ¶167; Baillie p. 165). The paragraphs are compressed and abstract, and they are often skipped by readers in a hurry to reach the master and the slave. They should not be: they contain the argument that makes the struggle for recognition necessary. Read them with Fichte in mind, for whom the I posits itself only by opposing a not-I and needs another free being's summons to become aware of its own freedom (SEP, 'Recognition').

1. Self-consciousness is the result of Argument 5: consciousness of the object has proved to be a form of self-relation, a self that "is essentially the return out of otherness" (PhG ¶167; Baillie p. 166).
2. But the world of sense and perception has not vanished; it remains as something that self-consciousness takes to be negative, not truly independent. A self that relates to its object as negative, and seeks to prove its own independence by negating it, "is primarily desire" (PhG ¶168; Baillie p. 167).
3. The object, meanwhile, has itself become self-related: "the object has become Life" (PhG ¶168; Baillie p. 167), a self-maintaining process of living things and their kinds (PhG ¶¶169–173).
4. Desire gets its certainty by consuming its object: "self-consciousness is thus only assured of itself through sublating this other ... self-consciousness is Desire" (PhG ¶174; Baillie p. 172).
5. But "In this state of satisfaction, however, it has experience of the independence of its object". The certainty exists only through cancelling an other, so there must always be another other; self-consciousness "rather produces it again, as well as the desire" (PhG ¶175; Baillie p. 172).
6. So self-consciousness "can only attain satisfaction when this object itself effectually brings about negation within itself" (PhG ¶175; Baillie p. 173), that is, when the object negates itself while remaining independent. An object that can do that is a consciousness.

Therefore "Self-consciousness attains its satisfaction only in another self-consciousness" (PhG ¶175; Baillie p. 173). Its truth is "the reduplication of self-consciousness" (PhG ¶176; Baillie p. 173). "When a self-consciousness is the object, the object is just as much ego as object", and "With this we already have before us the notion of Mind or Spirit": "Ego that is 'we,' a plurality of Egos, and 'we' that is a single Ego" (PhG ¶177; Baillie p. 174). How such a unity is achieved is the subject of Arguments 7 and 8.

**What each premise rests on.** Premise 1 rests on the whole of chapters 1 to 3. Premise 2 rests on the idea, which Hegel takes from Fichte, that self-consciousness is an activity, not an inner perception: the self knows itself as what it does, and what it first does is set itself against what is not itself. Premise 3 brings in the concept of life, which the SEP article and recent work such as Karen Ng's *Hegel's Concept of Life* (2020) take to be central to Hegel's theory of mind; the self that desires is a living being among living beings. Premise 4 is an observation about appetite: eating confirms the eater's power over the eaten, and that is the self-certainty desire seeks. Premise 5 is the key observation: desire's satisfaction is self-defeating, since it depends on what it destroys and must start again. Premise 6 is the boldest step. Hegel claims that the only object that could give desire lasting satisfaction is one that carries out the negation itself, so that the self's independence is confirmed without the object's being destroyed; and the only such object is one that can relate to itself negatively, which is to say a self-conscious being who can yield, assent or acknowledge.

**Objection.** The strongest objection is that premise 6 is a non sequitur. From the fact that consuming things never gives lasting satisfaction, it follows at most that desire is endless, not that some special object must exist that could satisfy it. Even if such an object is needed, it is unclear why it must be another self-consciousness, and not, say, a tool or a domesticated animal, which also, in a sense, negates itself in serving its owner. Readers who study the transition closely, such as Frederick Neuhouser and Robert Pippin, have said that the argument here is among the most compressed in the book and that the step from desire to recognition is hard to reconstruct. A second objection is Marx's, from the 1844 manuscripts: Hegel treats the human being as self-consciousness, so that the object becomes a mere moment in the self's return to itself; the real human being is a natural, sensuous being with needs, whose objects are independent of it, not stand-ins for its own negativity.

**Reply.** The literature's. The best reply reads the argument as concerned with a specific kind of satisfaction, the self-certainty desire was seeking, not with pleasure. Desire wants proof that it is the independent one, that the world counts for nothing against it. No thing can supply that proof, because a thing that is consumed proves nothing about its consumer except that it once ate, and a thing that merely persists counts against it. What could supply it is an independent being that *takes* the self as the standard, an object whose own self-relation contains the judgment that the self counts. Only a being that can take something as a standard, that is, a being with a point of view of its own, can give that judgment; a tool or an animal cannot, since it does not acknowledge anything. On this reply premise 6 is not a leap but the analysis of what desire's aim requires. Against Marx, the reply is that premises 2 and 3 already make the self a living, desiring being, and that the next section makes it a working one; Marx is right that Hegel describes this abstractly, but the abstraction is a stage of the book, not its final word.

**Where scholars disagree.** (a) Is this a story about two people or about one? Kojève, whose Paris lectures made the section famous, read "desire" as specifically human desire, the desire for another's desire, and the whole chapter as a philosophical anthropology of how humans become human by risking life for recognition. John McDowell ("The Apperceptive I and the Empirical Self", 2003, reprinted in *Having the World in View*, 2009) reads the section as an allegory of a single self-consciousness coming to terms with its own embodied life, with the "other self-consciousness" standing for the empirical self; Pippin (*Hegel on Self-Consciousness*, 2011) replies that the sociality is meant literally. (b) What is the role of life? Pippin and Pinkard stress that the self that desires must also be a living being, and that its practical self-relation is a condition of any knowledge; Ng and others give life a metaphysical role as the first form of the concept. (c) How much of the argument is Fichte's? SEP's 'Recognition' entry stresses Fichte's summons as the source of the idea that self-awareness depends on another free subject; Ludwig Siep (*Anerkennung als Prinzip der praktischen Philosophie*, 1979) traced the concept through Hegel's Jena writings and argued that the *Phenomenology* version is narrower than the Jena one, a view Axel Honneth took up.

**Argument 7. The struggle for recognition (PhG ¶¶178–188)**

*What it proves:* that a self-consciousness exists as such only in being recognized by another; that recognition, in its pure concept, must be mutual; and that the first attempt to win it, by each self proving to the other that it is not bound to life, must take the form of a life-and-death struggle, which fails in its own aim if either party dies.

**Text and setting.** This is the opening of section A of chapter IV, which Baillie heads "Independence and Dependence of Self-Consciousness" with the subtitle "Lordship and Bondage" (Baillie p. 175); the learning path's excerpt (¶¶178–196) begins here, and the library entry's reconstruction covers this argument and the next together. The section has three parts. Paragraphs 178–184 set out the pure concept of recognition, as the philosopher sees it. Paragraphs 185–188 show how that concept first appears to the self-consciousnesses themselves, as a struggle. Paragraphs 189–196 (Argument 8) give the outcome. The SEP article on recognition traces the idea back to Fichte's "summons" and to Hegel's Jena writings of 1805–06, where a struggle over property in a state of nature is already a struggle for recognition, not for goods; the *Phenomenology* makes the struggle more abstract and more radical.

1. "Self-consciousness exists in itself and for itself, in that, and by the fact that it exists for another self-consciousness; that is to say, it is only by being acknowledged or 'recognised'" (PhG ¶178; Baillie p. 175). This is the result of Argument 6.
2. When one self-consciousness meets another, it "has come outside itself": it finds itself in the other, and so "it has lost its own self, since it finds itself as an other being", but it also does not yet regard the other as fully real (PhG ¶179; Baillie p. 176).
3. Since the other is just as independent, nothing can be achieved on one side alone: "Action from one side only would be useless" (PhG ¶182; Baillie p. 177). In its pure concept, recognition is reciprocal: "They recognise themselves as mutually recognising one another" (PhG ¶184; Baillie p. 178).
4. But as the two first appear to each other they are living individuals, "for each other in the manner of ordinary objects", each certain of itself but not of the other (PhG ¶186; Baillie p. 178). The concept first appears as its opposite, a relation in which "one is merely recognised, while the other only recognises" (PhG ¶185; Baillie p. 178).
5. To show itself as pure self-consciousness, each must show that it is not tied to life, by aiming at the other's death and risking its own. So "they prove themselves and each other through a life-and-death struggle" (PhG ¶187; Baillie p. 179), and "it is solely by risking life, that freedom is obtained" (PhG ¶187; Baillie p. 180).

Therefore the struggle is necessary for this stage of self-consciousness: "The individual, who has not staked his life, may, no doubt, be recognised as a Person; but he has not attained the truth of this recognition as an independent self-consciousness" (PhG ¶187; Baillie p. 180). But the struggle, carried to death, "cancels both the truth which was to result from it, and therewith the certainty of self altogether" (PhG ¶188; Baillie p. 180). If one party dies, there is no one left to recognize the survivor; the two "let one another go quite indifferently, like things", and "Their act is abstract negation", not the negation of consciousness that preserves what it overcomes (PhG ¶188; Baillie p. 181). The way out, which Argument 8 follows, is that one party yields.

**What each premise rests on.** Premise 1 rests on Argument 6: a self that seeks proof of its independence can get it only from another being that can grant it. Premise 2 describes the ambivalence of that dependence: I need the other to confirm me, so I find myself in the other, and I resent the other as a rival to my independence. Premise 3 is a conceptual point about recognition as Hegel understands it. To recognize is to take another as having authority, and a recognition I extract from someone I do not recognize in turn is worth nothing to me, since that someone's judgment does not count. Premise 4 introduces the gap between the concept and its first realization, a pattern the *Phenomenology* repeats throughout. Premise 5 rests on the idea that life is the most basic attachment a self-conscious being has, so that to show oneself indifferent to life is to show oneself free of every particular attachment. Hegel uses "Person" in premise 5's conclusion for the bearer of abstract legal standing, a term he takes up again in the *Philosophy of Right* (Argument 13); the claim is that such standing is not yet self-conscious independence.

**Objection.** The strongest objection is that the struggle is unmotivated, and that by making it necessary Hegel builds domination into the foundation of social life. Even granting that self-consciousness needs recognition, nothing in premise 3 requires a fight. If recognition is reciprocal in its concept, the obvious way to get it is to give it. Why would two beings who need each other's confirmation set out to kill each other? Axel Honneth (*The Struggle for Recognition*, 1992) argues that the *Phenomenology* narrows Hegel's richer Jena account, in which recognition develops through love, law and social esteem, to a single confrontation between abstract self-consciousnesses, and so loses the forms of recognition that make social life possible. Jessica Benjamin's psychoanalytic work (*The Bonds of Love*, 1988), which the SEP entry on recognition cites with Honneth, holds that the first recognition human beings experience is the care of a parent, not a struggle. On these views Hegel's story is Hobbes's state of nature with prestige substituted for gain.

**Reply.** The literature's, with Hegel's text. First, the struggle is not Hegel's account of how recognition is actually achieved; it is the first, failed attempt of a self-consciousness that has only the concept of its own independence and knows nothing yet of mutuality. Hegel says outright that the struggle defeats itself (PhG ¶188), and the section's lesson, as the SEP entry on recognition puts it, is that such fighting reaches an impasse: either one subject dies or one submits, and neither outcome gives adequate recognition. Second, the struggle has a point that Honneth's richer model keeps. To be recognized as a free self, and not merely as a useful or lovable living thing, is to be recognized as someone whose commitments can outweigh any natural desire, including the desire to live; the risk of life dramatizes that capacity. Third, the reply to Benjamin is that Hegel is not writing developmental psychology. Love does appear in the *Phenomenology* and the *Philosophy of Right* as a form of recognition, in the family, but it is a recognition of a self as a member of a natural unit, not as an independent agent. The library entry gives the standard defense in brief: the argument's force is conceptual, not historical.

**Where scholars disagree.** (a) Is the struggle history, anthropology or logic? Kojève, in the 1930s lectures published as *Introduction to the Reading of Hegel* (1947), read it as the origin of human history: humanity begins when a being risks its animal life for prestige, and history is the working out of the resulting relation of master and slave until mutual recognition is achieved. Jean Hyppolite's *Genesis and Structure of Hegel's* Phenomenology of Spirit (1946) gave a less anthropological reading. Most recent interpreters, including Pippin and Pinkard, read the section as a conceptual reconstruction of the conditions of self-conscious agency, with no claim about prehistory. (b) How many selves are there? Against McDowell's one-self reading (Argument 6), Pippin and Brandom read the struggle as a genuine relation between agents, and Brandom makes reciprocal recognition the basis of normative authority itself: to recognize someone is to take her to have commitments and entitlements (SEP, 'Recognition', citing Brandom). (c) Jena or *Phenomenology*? Ludwig Siep and Honneth hold that the Jena writings contain the better theory; Robert Williams (*Hegel's Ethics of Recognition*, 1997) argues that the *Phenomenology*'s concept of reciprocal recognition, set out in ¶¶178–184 before the struggle starts, is itself the full theory, which the struggle only begins to realize. The SEP entry cites Siep (1979) and Honneth on the Jena material.

**Argument 8. Lordship and bondage: the reversal through fear and work (PhG ¶¶189–196)**

*What it proves:* that the relation of lord and bondsman, which the struggle produces when one party yields, defeats the lord's aim, since he is recognized only by a consciousness he does not recognize; and that the bondsman, through the fear of death, service and formative work on things, comes to a truer independence than the lord, though not yet to freedom.

**Text and setting.** This is the passage the learning path and the library entry treat as the center of the excerpt, and the one most read outside philosophy. The struggle of Argument 7 has taught that "life is as essential to it as pure self-consciousness" (PhG ¶189; Baillie p. 181): a self that kills its rival gains nothing, and a self that dies gains nothing either. So one party gives way and lives. Baillie translates *Herr* as "Master, or Lord" and *Knecht* as "Bondsman" (PhG ¶189; Baillie p. 182). "Bondsman" is closer to the German than "slave", which Kojève's French made standard; the word covers servant and serf as well, and the library entry warns against reading the passage as a literal theory of historical slavery. Read ¶¶189–193 for the lord and ¶¶194–196 for the bondsman, and then ¶197, which turns the result into stoicism (Argument 9).

1. The outcome of the struggle is two opposed shapes of consciousness: "The one is independent whose essential nature is to be for itself, the other is dependent whose essence is life or existence for another. The former is the Master, or Lord, the latter the Bondsman" (PhG ¶189; Baillie p. 182).
2. The lord relates to the thing through the bondsman, who works on it, and to the bondsman through the thing, the life and existence the bondsman would not give up: "it is his chain" (PhG ¶190; Baillie p. 182). The lord enjoys; the bondsman labors.
3. The lord is recognized, but for recognition proper each would have to do to himself what he does to the other, and so "a form of recognition has arisen that is one-sided and unequal" (PhG ¶191; Baillie p. 184).
4. The lord's truth is therefore the reverse of what he wanted: "It is not an independent, but rather a dependent consciousness that he has achieved" (PhG ¶192; Baillie p. 184). His recognition comes from a being whose judgment he holds worthless, and his satisfaction depends on another's work.
5. The bondsman, for his part, has known absolute fear. He "felt the fear of death, it was in mortal terror of its sovereign master", and in that experience was "melted to its inmost soul" (PhG ¶194; Baillie p. 185). That dissolution of every fixed attachment is the pure negativity the lord only claimed to have.
6. Fear alone is not enough: "albeit the fear of his lord is the beginning of wisdom, consciousness is not therein aware of being self-existent" (PhG ¶195; Baillie p. 186). Work supplies what fear lacks. Desire's satisfaction vanishes in consumption; "Labour, on the other hand, is desire restrained and checked, evanescence delayed and postponed; in other words, labour shapes and fashions the thing" (PhG ¶195; Baillie p. 186). In the shaped thing the bondsman's own activity becomes permanent and visible to him.

Therefore "The truth of the independent consciousness is accordingly the consciousness of the bondsman" (PhG ¶193; Baillie p. 184), and bondage will "change round into real and true independence" (PhG ¶193; Baillie p. 184). In fashioning the thing the bondsman "becomes aware, through this re-discovery of himself by himself, of having and being a 'mind of his own'" (PhG ¶196; Baillie p. 187). Hegel adds the condition at once. Both fear and formative work are needed, and needed in a universal form; work without the absolute fear yields only a "mind of its own" that "is simply stubbornness (Eigensinn), a type of freedom which does not get beyond the attitude of bondage" (PhG ¶196; Baillie p. 188).

**What each premise rests on.** Premise 1 is the result of Argument 7: the yielding party chose life over recognition and so made himself dependent. Premise 2 describes the structure of the relation; the bondsman is bound by his attachment to life, not by force alone. Premise 3 applies the concept of recognition from ¶¶178–184: recognition counts only if it is given by someone the recognized party also recognizes. Premise 4 draws the consequence for the lord, and it is the passage's best-known insight: domination defeats itself as a source of self-worth. Premise 5 reinterprets fear as an experience of freedom: in fearing for its whole being, and not for this or that, the bondsman's consciousness has been loosened from every particular attachment. The phrase about the fear of the lord echoes the biblical proverb that the fear of the Lord is the beginning of wisdom (Psalm 111:10; Proverbs 9:10), which Hegel turns to his purpose. Premise 6 is the theory of work. Work is desire held back, and what it produces lasts; in the product the worker sees his own activity as something independent of him, which is what the lord's consumption could never give.

**Objection.** Marx's criticism in the Paris manuscripts of 1844 is the most influential. Marx credits Hegel with grasping labor as the process by which human beings make themselves, and with seeing the self as the result of its own work. But he charges that the only labor Hegel really knows is abstract mental labor; the bondsman's work becomes a stage in the self-education of consciousness, and the real conditions of labor (who owns the product, who commands the work, what the work does to the worker) are passed over. Read this way the reversal is a consolation: the bondsman is told that he has a "mind of his own" while he still works for another and owns nothing. Hegel's own text seems to concede the point, since the next shape is stoicism, a freedom of thought whose essence is "to be free, on the throne as well as in fetters" (PhG ¶199; Baillie p. 193; Argument 9). On this objection the passage describes an inner liberation and calls it independence.

**Reply.** Partly Hegel's own, partly the literature's. Hegel does not claim that the bondsman is free at the end of ¶196. He claims that the bondsman has reached a truer form of self-consciousness than the lord: a self that knows itself in what it does and makes, not in what it consumes or commands. That self is still in bondage, and the *Phenomenology* says so; the next three shapes (Argument 9) are the bondsman's attempts to make his freedom real in thought alone, and they fail. The full answer to the question of what mutual recognition requires in the world comes in the chapter on spirit and in the *Philosophy of Right* (Arguments 13 to 15), where freedom is embodied in property, contract, family, civil society and the state. Marx's objection is therefore best read as a demand that the argument be carried further into economic life, which is what Marx himself did with it, not as a refutation of what the passage shows. The reply leaves a debt. The claim that work in the service of another can be formative depends on the condition of ¶196, that the worker has passed through absolute fear and works in a universal manner; Hegel does not explain how one would tell formative work from mere drudgery.

**Where scholars disagree.** (a) What is the passage for? Kojève made it the motor of history: the slave, by working, transforms nature and himself, and history ends when the slave's work and the master's recognition are united in a state of universal mutual recognition. Georg Lukács (*The Young Hegel*, 1948) read it as Hegel's discovery of labor under the influence of Adam Smith and the political economists. Gadamer's essay on the dialectic of self-consciousness (1973) argued against Kojève that the passage is about the formation of consciousness, not about a social class. Pippin and Brandom read the master as a model of a one-sided conception of authority, one that claims authority without accepting any answerability to others, and the passage as a demonstration that such authority is incoherent. (b) Did history shape the passage? Susan Buck-Morss (*Hegel and Haiti*, 2009, first as an essay in 2000) argued that Hegel wrote it with the Haitian revolution in mind; others, including Pinkard, doubt that the text supports a specific historical reference. (c) Who has used it? The library entry notes the reception through Kojève, and Simone de Beauvoir and Frantz Fanon adapted the dialectic in *The Second Sex* (1949) and *Black Skin, White Masks* (1952); Fanon in particular argued that colonial domination does not follow Hegel's pattern, since the colonizer wants work from the colonized, not recognition. Whether those uses are readings of Hegel or transformations of him is itself disputed.

**Argument 9. Stoicism, scepticism and the unhappy consciousness (PhG ¶¶197–230)**

*What it proves:* that a freedom won by withdrawing into thought, whether it ignores the world (stoicism) or dissolves it (scepticism), cannot hold, and that the self divided between a changing self and an unchanging beyond it (the unhappy consciousness) is the honest result of both; the division ends only when the self gives up its private will and finds the universal in its own action, which is the step to Reason.

**Text and setting.** Lordship and bondage ends with the bondsman discovering, in work, that the form he gives to things is his own (Argument 8). Section IV.B of the *Phenomenology* draws the consequence. The bondsman's discovery is a discovery of thought: "a consciousness, which takes itself to be infinitude", and "It is one which thinks or is free self-consciousness" (PhG ¶197; Baillie p. 190). The section then runs through three shapes that Hegel names for historical movements. Stoicism is "this freedom of self-consciousness", "as is well known", in so far as it appeared in "the history of man's spirit" (PhG ¶198; Baillie p. 192). Scepticism is the ancient Pyrrhonism of Sextus, not modern doubt. The unhappy consciousness has no ready-made name, as Baillie's note says (p. 200), and the shapes Hegel describes under it, devotion to an unchangeable beyond, the search for it in a particular embodiment, gratitude, a mediator, asceticism, are recognizably those of medieval Christianity, which Baillie's notes identify (God as judge, Christ, the priesthood). Week 4 of the study plan reads the whole section.

1. The self that has learned in work that its form is its own takes thought as its essence: what counts as true and good for it is what it can think (PhG ¶¶197–199).
2. Stoicism makes that freedom independent of circumstance: "The essence of this consciousness is to be free, on the throne as well as in fetters" (PhG ¶199; Baillie p. 193). The master's power and the bondsman's service no longer touch it.
3. But this freedom has withdrawn from the world, not mastered it. Its thought has no content of its own; content is "a datum, is given" (PhG ¶200; Baillie p. 194). It is "merely the notion of freedom, not living freedom itself" (PhG ¶200; Baillie p. 193), and its watchwords, wisdom and virtue, "soon begin to get wearisome" (PhG ¶200; Baillie p. 194).
4. "Scepticism is the realisation of that of which Stoicism is merely the notion" (PhG ¶202; Baillie p. 195): where stoicism ignores the world, scepticism works on it, dissolving every claim to determinate truth.
5. Scepticism cannot live by its result. It "announces the nullity of seeing, hearing, and so on, yet itself sees and hears"; "Its deeds and its words belie each other continually" (PhG ¶205; Baillie p. 199). Its talk "is like a squabble among self-willed children" (PhG ¶205; Baillie p. 199).
6. So the sceptic is in fact two selves, one unchanging and self-identical, one contingent and confused. "In this way the duplication, which previously was divided between two individuals, the lord and the bondsman, is concentrated into one" (PhG ¶206; Baillie p. 200).
7. When that doubled self knows itself as doubled, it is the unhappy consciousness, "the consciousness of self as a divided nature, a doubled and merely contradictory being" (PhG ¶207; Baillie p. 200). It places its essence in an unchangeable beyond and itself in the changeable, so every effort to rise to the beyond is again its own particular effort: "a struggle against an enemy, victory over whom really means being worsted" (PhG ¶209; Baillie p. 201).
8. Its attempts to reach the unchangeable (devotion, then work and gratitude, then self-mortification) fail in the same way, until it hands its will to a mediator and renounces its property and enjoyment, so that it has "the certainty of having in truth stripped itself of its Ego" (PhG ¶229; Baillie p. 217).
9. Giving up one's own will, though, "is only in one aspect negative": it affirms the will "as not a particular, but universal" (PhG ¶230; Baillie pp. 217–218). What the unhappy consciousness took to be a beyond is present in its own renunciation.

Therefore the self is not a thinking point set against a world it ignores, dissolves or flees, but the unity of the particular and the universal. When it sees this, "there has arisen the idea of Reason, of the certainty that consciousness is, in its particularity, inherently and essentially absolute, or is all reality" (PhG ¶230; Baillie p. 219).

**What each premise rests on.** Premise 1 rests on Argument 8: the bondsman's work is the first time a self finds its own form outside it, and Hegel treats thinking as the internalized version of that discovery. Premises 2 and 3 rest on Hegel's standing complaint about abstract freedom, which comes back in the Terror (Argument 10) and in his case against Kant's moral law (Argument 12): a freedom defined only by what it refuses has no content, and gets its content from outside. Premise 3's point about the criterion of truth is a historical one: Hegel says stoicism "got embarrassed" when asked for the criterion of truth and answered with "reasonableness" (PhG ¶200; Baillie p. 194), which is the ancient sceptics' charge against the Stoics. Premises 4 and 5 rest on a reading of Pyrrhonism as a practice of opposing every claim with an equal one, which Hegel admired; the charge in premise 5 is the old one that a sceptic cannot live his scepticism. Premises 6 to 9 rest on the phenomenological method stated in the Introduction (PhG ¶¶84–87): each shape is tested by its own standard, and its failure by that standard produces the next shape. The religious content of premises 7 and 8 is Hegel's reading of Christian piety, which he treats more fully in chapter VII.

**Objection.** The objection with the longest pedigree is that the transitions are not necessary and the order is history in disguise. Why should the freedom of thought appear as stoicism, then scepticism, then Christian devotion, unless because that is the order in which they came in the Roman and medieval world? Rudolf Haym, whose *Hegel und seine Zeit* (1857) set the terms of the liberal critique of Hegel, put the charge against the whole *Phenomenology*: it mixes a psychology of consciousness with a history of culture so that each confuses the other (from memory; the SEP article on Hegel's social and political philosophy cites Haym only for the reading of the *Philosophy of Right*). If the sequence is historical, it proves nothing about self-consciousness as such; if it is logical, the historical names are decoration and the argument has to stand without them, which it does not obviously do. A second objection is Kierkegaard's in spirit. Hegel treats the unhappy consciousness as a shape to be passed through on the way to Reason, but the condition he describes, a self that knows its eternal essence and cannot reach it, is for Kierkegaard not a stage that thought overcomes but the permanent condition of the existing individual before God. *Either/Or* (1843) contains an address on "The Unhappiest One" that plays on Hegel's term (from memory). On this view the passage to Reason is the system explaining away the very thing it has described.

**Reply.** The literature's. On necessity, the argument does not claim that history had to produce Zeno, Sextus and the medieval church in that order. It claims that a self which takes thought as its whole essence must either hold the world at a distance or dissolve it, and that either policy, followed through, splits the self in two. The historical movements are the clearest cases of each policy. Hegel says in the Preface that the individual has to pass through, in shortened form, the stages of formation that the general spirit has already passed through (PhG ¶28, paraphrased), so the history is used as the record of experiments already run, not as the premise. Terry Pinkard (*Hegel's Phenomenology: The Sociality of Reason*, 1994, from memory) reads the shapes as attempts by a community to give an account of what is authoritative for it, which makes the transitions failures of justification, not historical accidents. On Kierkegaard, a defender can concede the description and deny the inference: Hegel agrees that the unhappy consciousness cannot be talked out of its unhappiness. What dissolves it is a change in what it does, the surrender of private will in action shared with others, and Hegel's word for the result is spirit, not a proof. Whether that answers Kierkegaard, who held that the individual's relation to God cannot be mediated by any community, is exactly the open question.

**Where scholars disagree.** (a) How historical is section IV.B? Jean Wahl's *Le malheur de la conscience dans la philosophie de Hegel* (1929) made the unhappy consciousness the key to the whole of Hegel, and read it through the young Hegel's theological manuscripts; Alexandre Kojève and Jean Hyppolite carried that reading into French philosophy (all from memory). Pinkard and Robert Pippin read it as a stage in the logic of normative authority, and H. S. Harris's commentary (*Hegel's Ladder*, 1997, from memory) tracks the historical references paragraph by paragraph while denying that they carry the argument. (b) Is the unhappy consciousness a critique of Christianity? Feuerbach read Hegel as having shown that God is the alienated essence of humanity, and the Left Hegelians took section IV.B that way; the Right Hegelians, and readers such as Houlgate, take Hegel to criticize only a form of religious consciousness that keeps God in a beyond, not Christianity, which chapter VII presents as the absolute religion (from memory). (c) Judith Butler (*Subjects of Desire*, 1987; *The Psychic Life of Power*, 1997, from memory) reads the ascetic moments of ¶¶225–229 as the first account of how a subject is formed by turning a prohibition against itself, and so as an ancestor of Nietzsche's and Freud's accounts of conscience. That reading takes premise 8, not premise 9, as the center of the argument.

**Argument 10. Absolute freedom and the Terror (PhG ¶¶582–595)**

*What it proves:* that a freedom which recognizes no will but the universal will of all, and no institution it has not made itself, can produce no law and no lasting work; it can only destroy, and its last object is the individual it claimed to free, so that the Terror of 1793–94 follows from the principle and not only from the circumstances of the Revolution.

**Text and setting.** This is the climax of chapter VI.B, "Spirit in Self-Estrangement", which traces the culture of the old regime, the Enlightenment's struggle with faith, and the Enlightenment's last word, utility. When everything is valued as useful for self-consciousness, the world has no standing of its own left, and "the actual revolution of concrete actuality, the new mode of conscious life — absolute freedom" comes on the scene (PhG ¶582; Baillie p. 592). The section is Hegel's account of the French Revolution, written thirteen years after the fall of Robespierre, and it names no names. The "universal will" is Rousseau's general will as the revolutionaries took it up, and Baillie's note identifies the regime described as the revolutionary government. Hegel came back to the same verdict in the *Philosophy of Right*, where Rousseau has "the merit of discovering and presenting a principle" that is thought itself, namely will (PhR §258 R; Dyde p. 241), and its application produced "the most tremendous spectacle which the human race has ever witnessed" (PhR §258 R; Dyde p. 242). Week 5 of the study plan reads the section. See [Jean-Jacques Rousseau](../../eras/03-early-modern/jean-jacques-rousseau.md) and, for the Kantian sequel in "Morality", Argument 12.

1. Absolute freedom is the self-consciousness for which "the world is for it absolutely its own will, and this will is universal will" (PhG ¶584; Baillie p. 594). It is not a will exercised by "giving a silent assent, or an assent through a representative", but "the will of all individuals as such" (PhG ¶584; Baillie p. 594).
2. Such a will can recognize nothing standing outside it. So "In this absolute freedom all social ranks or classes, which are the component spiritual factors into which the whole is differentiated, are effaced and annulled" (PhG ¶585; Baillie p. 595).
3. A lasting work of freedom would need distinct, stable spheres: powers "differentiated into legislative, judicial and executive", and the estates of social labor (PhG ¶588; Baillie p. 597). But every such sphere confines individuals to a part, and the universal will refuses to be represented by a part. So it cannot make laws or institutions.
4. Nor can it act. A deed needs a single will to carry it, and as soon as one person or body acts for the universal, "all other individuals are excluded from the entirety of this deed" (PhG ¶589; Baillie p. 598).
5. "Universal freedom can thus produce neither a positive achievement nor a deed; there is left for it only negative action; it is merely the rage and fury of disappearance and destruction" (PhG ¶589; Baillie p. 598).
6. When the old order is gone, the only thing left to negate is the individual self. "The sole and only work and deed accomplished by universal freedom is therefore death", a death "with no more significance than cleaving a head of cabbage or swallowing a draught of water" (PhG ¶590; Baillie p. 599).
7. The government that acts for the universal is in fact a particular will, so "The victorious faction only is called the government" (PhG ¶591; Baillie p. 599). Since what it opposes is only intention, it cannot prove guilt: "Being suspected, therefore, takes the place, or has the significance and effect, of being guilty" (PhG ¶591; Baillie p. 600).
8. In this experience absolute freedom learns what it is: "the terror of death is the direct apprehension (Anschauung) of this its negative nature" (PhG ¶592; Baillie p. 600).

Therefore freedom cannot be the bare identity of each will with the universal will. It needs a differentiated order that the self can recognize as its own without having made it from nothing. The *Phenomenology* draws this conclusion first as a move inward, to the "Moral Life of Spirit" (PhG ¶595; Baillie p. 605) and Kant; the *Philosophy of Right* draws it as the state of estates and powers (Argument 15).

**What each premise rests on.** Premise 1 rests on Rousseau's doctrine that sovereignty cannot be represented (*Social Contract* III.15), which Hegel echoes in the phrase about assent "through a representative". Premise 2 is the Revolution's own program, the abolition of orders and corporations, and it rests on the Enlightenment result of chapter VI that nothing has value except for self-consciousness. Premises 3 and 4 are the logical core, and they rest on Hegel's view that the universal is actual only as articulated into particulars: a universal that refuses all particular embodiment is abstract, the same charge made against stoicism (Argument 9). Premise 6 rests on the claim that the bare self, stripped of rank, property and role, is the only remaining "other" to a universal will; it is a premise about logic, illustrated by the guillotine. Premise 7 is the most historical: the Law of Suspects of September 1793 made suspicion a ground of arrest, and Hegel's sentence is a description of it.

**Objection.** Two objections pull in opposite directions. The first is the historian's. The Terror had causes, war on the frontiers, civil war in the Vendée, food shortages, the flight of the king, that Hegel's account ignores, and a historian such as Albert Soboul read it as a response to emergency, not as the working out of an idea (from memory). If the Terror was contingent, Hegel has turned an event into a proof. The second objection comes from the liberal side and is Isaiah Berlin's. In "Two Concepts of Liberty" (1958) Berlin argued that the "positive" conception of freedom, freedom as self-realization through identification with a rational will or a larger whole, is the conception that despotisms have used to coerce people in the name of their true selves, and he counted Hegel among its chief sources (from memory). On that view the argument indicts the wrong thing. The trouble with the Jacobins was not that their universal was abstract, but that they claimed to know what everyone really willed, and Hegel's remedy, a rational state in which the individual "has his truth" (PhR §258 R; Dyde p. 240), makes the same claim in a more durable form.

**Reply.** The literature's. Against the historian, the argument does not claim that every revolution must end in terror, only that a revolution which takes the bare general will as its principle has no way to stop, because it has no criterion for distinguishing legitimate opposition from betrayal. That is a claim about the structure of a political idea, and it is compatible with any number of causes for when and how far the idea was acted on. François Furet's later reading of the Revolution, in which the Terror follows from the revolutionary discourse of popular sovereignty, gave a historian's version of Hegel's thesis (from memory). Against Berlin, Hegel's remedy is not a single will. Premise 3 is a list of what absolute freedom cannot tolerate, and it reads as a description of liberal constitutionalism: separated powers, representation, a society of distinct occupations. The *Philosophy of Right* builds its state out of exactly these, with a sphere of subjective freedom in civil society that no Jacobin would allow (Arguments 14 and 15). Whether the Hegelian state then claims too much for itself is a fair question, but it is a question about Argument 15, not a reason to reject this argument's diagnosis.

**Where scholars disagree.** (a) What did Hegel think of the Revolution? Joachim Ritter (*Hegel und die französische Revolution*, 1957) argued that there is no philosophy that is a philosophy of revolution as deeply as Hegel's, since his whole account of modern freedom is an attempt to secure the Revolution's principle; Jürgen Habermas ("Hegel's Critique of the French Revolution", in *Theory and Practice*) replied that Hegel celebrated the revolution in principle in order to condemn the revolutionaries in fact (both from memory). The SEP article on Hegel's social and political philosophy records that Hegel kept toasting the fall of the Bastille all his life (checked). (b) Is the target Rousseau, Kant or Fichte? The universal will is Rousseau's, but the section's move from a will that knows only itself into "Morality" suggests that Kant's autonomy and Fichte's ego are also in view; Rebecca Comay (*Mourning Sickness*, 2011, from memory) reads the German reception of the Revolution, Kant's included, as a way of having the Revolution in thought instead of in fact, and the transition to Morality as Hegel's diagnosis of that habit. (c) How to read ¶595's "another land"? Pinkard takes it as the turn from French politics to German philosophy, the Revolution's principle reinterpreted as Kant's moral will (from memory); readers who stress the Berlin-era Hegel, Avineri among them, read the *Philosophy of Right* as the place where the principle is given institutions (Avineri's *Hegel's Theory of the Modern State*, 1972, is cited in SEP for the "liberal" reading, checked).

**Argument 11. Being, nothing, becoming (EL §§86–88)**

*What it proves:* that the most abstract thought, pure being, cannot be held fixed: thought through, it is indistinguishable from nothing, nothing in turn is the same as being, and the only stable thought of the two is their passing into each other, becoming, which is "the first concrete thought" of the *Logic*.

**Text and setting.** The *Logic* opens with the question the Preface to the *Phenomenology* had raised, where philosophy may begin (Argument 1). Hegel's answer is that a beginning must presuppose nothing, so it cannot be a principle, a definition, the certainty of the self or an intuition of the absolute, since each of these "contains a mediation, and hence cannot be the real first" (EL §86 R; Wallace p. 158). What is left is the thought of what is, with nothing further said about it. The *Encyclopaedia Logic* gives the argument in three short sections with Hegel's remarks and the Zusätze added from lecture notes by the editors of 1840; the *Science of Logic* gives a longer version. This is the passage that week 6 of the study plan reads, and it is the passage the textbook triad was built on. Heinrich Moritz Chalybäus, in lectures published in 1837, called being, nothing and becoming the first thesis, antithesis and synthesis, and a schema for all that follows; Hegel himself never used the three words as his method, and in the *Phenomenology* Preface dismissed the "triplicity" taken over from Kant as a lifeless schema (Gustav E. Mueller, "The Hegel Legend of 'Thesis-Antithesis-Synthesis'", *Journal of the History of Ideas* 19, 1958, checked; the SEP article "Hegel's Dialectics" cites Mueller as the classic criticism of the triad reading, checked). The terms are Fichte's. For the Kantian background, the antinomies in which reason contradicts itself, see the [Kant dossier's arguments](../immanuel-kant/04-arguments.md).

1. A science that presupposes nothing must begin with what is immediate and wholly indeterminate. "Pure Being makes the beginning: because it is on one hand pure thought, and on the other immediacy itself, simple and indeterminate" (EL §86; Wallace p. 158).
2. Pure being has no determination at all: to say anything more of it than that it is would be to add a determination, and so to stop beginning with being alone.
3. A thought with no determination whatever is the thought of nothing. "But this mere Being, as it is mere abstraction, is therefore the absolutely negative: which, in a similarly immediate aspect, is just Nothing" (EL §87; Wallace p. 161).
4. Nothing, thought in its turn, is the same empty, immediate, self-identical thought. "Nothing, if it be thus immediate and equal to itself, is also conversely the same as Being is" (EL §88; Wallace p. 163).
5. Yet being and nothing are also not the same: "It is as correct however to say that Being and Nothing are altogether different, as to assert their unity" (EL §88 R; Wallace p. 163). The difference cannot be stated, since neither has any content to state it with; it is "something unutterable, which we merely mean" (EL §88 R; Wallace p. 164).
6. So thought cannot rest in either. Each, held on its own, has already passed into the other. What thought actually has is that passing.
7. The passing of being into nothing and of nothing into being is becoming. "The truth of Being and of Nothing is accordingly the unity of the two: and this unity is Becoming" (EL §88; Wallace p. 163).

Therefore "Becoming is the first concrete thought, and therefore the first notion: whereas Being and Nought are empty abstractions" (EL §88 Z; Wallace p. 167). Being and nothing are kept in becoming as its moments, not discarded: this is the first case of what Hegel calls *Aufhebung*, sublation. Hegel offers familiar illustrations, for the reader who doubts we have any idea of a unity of being and nothing: "The readiest example of it is Becoming", and another is a beginning, in which the thing "is not yet, but it is more than merely nothing" (EL §88 R; Wallace pp. 165–166).

**What each premise rests on.** Premise 1 rests on the demand for a presuppositionless science, which Hegel inherits from Reinhold and Fichte and turns against them: their first principles (the I = I, the absolute indifference) are already mediated (EL §86 R). Premise 2 is analytic. Premise 3 is the decisive step, and it rests on taking "indeterminate" strictly: being and nothing differ, if at all, only in what we mean by them, not in any thought-content. Hegel's own gloss is that "Being, as Being, is nothing fixed or ultimate: it yields to dialectic and sinks into its opposite" (EL §86 Z; Wallace p. 161). Premises 5 and 6 rest on the view of contradiction that Hegel sets out in EL §§79–82: the "dialectical" moment, in which a one-sided determination passes into its opposite, is followed by the "speculative" moment, which grasps the unity of the two in their opposition. Premise 7 rests on the claim that a process can be the content of a thought, and not only a series of thoughts.

**Objection.** Friedrich Adolf Trendelenburg made the classic objection in his *Logische Untersuchungen* (1840) and in "Die logische Frage in Hegel's System" (1843) (from memory; no saved source). Pure being is at rest, and so is pure nothing. Two motionless abstractions, thought together, give an identity or a contradiction, not a movement. Becoming is motion, and motion cannot be extracted from concepts that lack it. So Hegel has borrowed it from intuition, from our experience of things coming to be and passing away in space and time, while claiming to derive it from pure thought. If that is so, the presuppositionless beginning fails at its first step, and the whole *Logic*, which proceeds by the same kind of transition, rests on smuggled intuition. Kierkegaard read Trendelenburg and pressed the point in the *Concluding Unscientific Postscript* (1846): in logic no movement can come about, since logic and everything logical simply is (from memory). A different objection, common in analytic philosophy after Russell, holds that premise 3 trades on an equivocation between the thought of nothing determinate and the thought of nothing at all (from memory).

**Reply.** The literature's, with Hegel's own resources. The becoming at the start of the *Logic* is not motion in space and time. Hegel says that the readiest example of it is becoming as we picture it, but that the example only illustrates the thought (EL §88 R). What the thought contains is that each term, held on its own, is not stable: being is already nothing, nothing already being. Stephen Houlgate (*The Opening of Hegel's Logic*, 2006, from memory) puts the reply this way: Trendelenburg assumes that thought is a fixed content to which movement must be added, while the *Logic* argues that an indeterminate thought is not fixed, so that the vanishing of being into nothing is what we find when we try to think being, not a picture we add. On the equivocation charge, Hegel's point is that there is no determinate content to equivocate between at this stage; the objection assumes a distinction between nothing determinate and nothing at all, which itself requires the category of determinacy that the *Logic* has not yet earned. The reply carries a cost. If the opening is not about motion, the word "becoming" is a name for a logical relation, and the claim that it is the first concrete thought has to be redeemed by the rest of the *Logic*, which is the hardest book Hegel wrote.

**Where scholars disagree.** (a) Is the *Logic* an ontology or a theory of thought? On the metaphysical reading, defended by Houlgate (2005, 2022), Robert Stern and Frederick Beiser, the categories are the structure of being itself, so that becoming is the first thing that can be truly said of what is. On the post-Kantian or non-metaphysical reading of Robert Pippin (*Hegel's Realm of Shadows*, 2019), Terry Pinkard and, in his inferentialist way, Robert Brandom, the *Logic* is a successor to Kant's deduction of the categories, an account of what any intelligible judgment requires (SEP "Hegel", sections on the logic, checked). The SEP article records Houlgate's complaint that Pippin misses the logic as a direct study of being, and Pippin's reply that Hegel's metaphysics is a metaphysics in a new sense. (b) Is there a single dialectical method? The SEP article "Hegel's Dialectics" (Maybee) argues that the triad fits being, nothing and becoming better than almost anything else in the system, and that the engine of the *Logic* is the one-sidedness of each determination, not a three-step pattern; W. T. Stace (1924), cited there, thought the triad was the method but admitted Hegel does not apply it throughout (checked). Michael Forster and Jon Stewart are among those cited there as still using versions of the triad reading (checked). (c) How strong is Trendelenburg's objection? Kierkegaard thought it decisive; Houlgate thinks it misunderstands the project; Michael Inwood (*Hegel*, 1983, from memory) concedes that the opening trades on the ambiguity of "nothing" but takes the trade to be instructive, not fatal.

**Argument 12. The empty formalism of Kant's moral law (PhR §135; PhG ¶¶419–437)**

*What it proves:* that a moral law whose only test is non-contradiction can neither generate duties nor rule out wrongs, because any content that is not already assumed to be valid is consistent with itself and with its opposite; so duties get their content from an ethical life (*Sittlichkeit*) of institutions that already exist, and Kant's achievement, the autonomy of the will, is kept only when it is placed inside such a life.

**Text and setting.** Hegel made the charge many times, first in the essay on natural law of 1802–03 and last in the Berlin lectures. Two statements are the standard ones. In the *Phenomenology*, at the end of the chapter on Reason, two sections called "Reason as Lawgiver" and "Reason as Testing Laws" (PhG ¶¶419–437) examine an ethical consciousness that tries to find its laws in reason alone; Baillie's note to the end of the first section says that "The above criticism applies to Kant's 'categorical imperative'" (Baillie p. 417). In the *Philosophy of Right* the charge sits at the center of "Morality", the second part of the book, which comes between "Abstract Right" (property, contract, wrong) and "Ethical Life" (family, civil society, state). Hegel is not attacking a straw man he despises. "It is the signal merit of the standpoint of the Kantian philosophy of action that it has made prominent this signification of duty", he says (PhR §133 A; Dyde p. 127), and the doctrine of autonomy "attained to a firm basis and starting-point first of all in the Kantian philosophy through the thought of the infinite autonomy of the will" (PhR §135 R; Dyde p. 128). The Kant dossier reconstructs the test Hegel attacks as [Argument 7, the universal-law test](../immanuel-kant/04-arguments.md), and the humanity formula as Argument 8. Week 7 of the study plan reads PhR §§105–141 in selection.

1. On Kant's view, as Hegel takes it, duty must be done for duty's sake, and the good will is the will determined by the form of law alone (PhR §133).
2. Duty so understood "is only an abstract universality, and has for its characteristic an identity without content" (PhR §135; Dyde p. 128).
3. The only test such a principle supplies is consistency: a maxim is permitted if it can be universalized without contradiction. But that test is "a tautology and indifferent to the content, it accepts one content just as readily as the opposite" (PhG ¶430; Baillie p. 419).
4. Contents are consistent with themselves, taken alone. "Property per se does not contradict itself", and "community of goods, contradicts itself just as little" (PhG ¶430; Baillie p. 420). Taken as a set of relations, each contradicts itself as much as the other: "Property therefore contradicts itself on all hands just as much as absence of property" (PhG ¶431; Baillie p. 421).
5. A contradiction appears only against a content already assumed. "The rule that there should be no private property contains of itself no contradiction"; "Only if it is really fixed and assumed that private property and human life should exist and be respected, is it a contradiction to commit theft or murder" (PhR §135 R; Dyde p. 129).
6. So from formal duty "no specific duties can be deduced"; materials "may be introduced from without", but then they are not derived from duty, and "all manner of wrong and immoral acts may be by such a method justified" (PhR §135 R; Dyde p. 128).
7. If the moral standpoint does not pass into ethical life, Kant's acquisition "is reduced to empty formalism, and moral science is converted into mere rhetoric about duty for duty's sake" (PhR §135 R; Dyde p. 128).

Therefore duties have content only within ethical life, the family, civil society and the state, where the institutions are already there and the self finds them its own. The *Phenomenology* draws the same conclusion in its own way: reason as lawgiver "is reduced to being reason as criterion; instead of laying down laws reason now only tests what is laid down" (PhG ¶428; Baillie p. 417), and what remains is the ethical consciousness for which the laws simply hold, as for Antigone, who "takes them for the unwritten and unerring law of the gods" (PhG ¶437; Baillie p. 425). The *Philosophy of Right*'s Addition puts the point in one line: "Where there is nothing, there can be no contradiction" (PhR §135 A; Dyde p. 129).

**What each premise rests on.** Premise 1 is a fair summary of the *Groundwork*'s first section. Premise 2 is Hegel's gloss: the moral law is universal, but its universality is that of a form without a content of its own. Premise 3 is the reading of the Formula of Universal Law as a test of logical consistency, which is the reading under dispute. Premise 4 is Hegel's own analysis of property, in which property has both a universal side (it is recognized by everyone) and a particular side (it is mine), so that the bare concept is neither consistent nor inconsistent until it is placed in a system of relations. Premise 5 carries the argument and is a general point about contradiction: a maxim can conflict only with something taken as fixed. Kant's deposit example, in which a maxim of keeping deposits whose owners cannot prove them would abolish deposits, is the case Hegel had in mind as early as the natural-law essay, where he replied that nothing in the moral law says there must be deposits (from memory). Premise 7 is the transition to ethical life, which is argued for in Arguments 13 to 15.

**Objection.** The objection is the Kantians', and it has three parts. First, Hegel tests the wrong thing. The universal-law formula tests maxims, a person's principles of action, not institutions such as property, and the contradiction it looks for is a contradiction in willing, not a logical contradiction in a proposition. Christine Korsgaard's practical-contradiction reading (1985) and Onora O'Neill's *Acting on Principle* (1975) show how a maxim of false promising defeats itself as a means to its own end, without assuming in advance that promising is good (from memory). Second, the formula of universal law is one of three, and the formula of humanity gives the moral law a content of its own: rational nature as an end in itself. Third, Kant never claimed to deduce duties from the bare form. The *Metaphysics of Morals* (1797) applies the principle to the general facts of human life, and that is not cheating. Allen Wood ("The Emptiness of the Moral Will", 1989) concludes that Hegel's criticism is unfair and misses its mark, and Fabian Freyenhagen ("The Empty Formalism Objection Revisited", 2012) and Robert Stern ("On Hegel's Critique of Kant's Ethics: Beyond the Empty Formalism Objection", 2012) have returned to the question (all checked in the SEP article on Hegel's social and political philosophy, Thom Brooks). Hegel's best-known ally made the same charge in a different spirit. John Stuart Mill, in *Utilitarianism* (1861), granted that Kant lays down a universal first principle but said that when Kant begins to deduce actual duties from it, "he fails, almost grotesquely, to show that there would be any contradiction, any logical (not to say physical) impossibility, in the adoption by all rational beings of the most outrageously immoral rules of conduct" (*Utilitarianism*, ch. 1). Mill's conclusion was that Kant was a consequentialist in disguise, not that the formula needs ethical life.

**Reply.** Partly Hegel's, partly the literature's. Against the first part, Hegel can grant that the test applies to maxims and still ask where the maxim's end comes from. The practical-contradiction reading works by assuming that the agent wants the end, the false promise's loan, that universal adoption would defeat; it then shows the maxim self-defeating relative to that end. Hegel's point survives in a weaker form: what counts as an agent's end, and which practices exist to be relied on, is supplied by a social world, not by the form of law. Against the second part, a defender of Hegel such as Robert Pippin (*Hegel's Practical Philosophy*, 2008, from memory) argues that the formula of humanity raises the same question one level up: what treating someone as an end requires depends on what the institutions of a society make possible, so it too needs ethical life to be determinate. Against the third, Hegel's claim is not that Kant cheated but that the content added in the *Metaphysics of Morals* is not shown to follow from autonomy, so that the system is in practice what Hegel says it is, a form supplemented from outside. The SEP article adds a qualification on Hegel's own terms: Hegel has redefined "morality" as reflection that is by itself "without content", so part of the force of the charge comes from his definition (checked).

**Where scholars disagree.** (a) Does the charge refute Kant? Wood, writing on the Kantian side, says no, and the SEP article agrees that the criticism misses its mark (checked). Freyenhagen's essay re-examines the Remark to §135 against recent Kantian responses, and Stern's argues that Hegel's real disagreement with Kant lies beyond the formalism objection, in how obligation itself is understood (both titles checked in the SEP bibliography; their arguments from memory). Sally Sedgwick holds that a reconstructed version of the charge, aimed at the claim that pure reason alone can determine duty, still has force (from memory). (b) What is the charge for? On one reading, Hegel's target is moral philosophy as such, any view that grounds duty in the individual's reflection alone (SEP, checked); on another, it is Fichte as much as Kant, and the romantic appeal to conscience that Hegel attacks in PhR §§136–140. (c) Does ethical life simply bless whatever exists? Ernst Tugendhat (*Self-Consciousness and Self-Determination*, 1979, from memory) argued that Hegel's move from morality to ethical life gives up the individual's right to criticize institutions; Frederick Neuhouser (*Foundations of Hegel's Social Theory*, 2000) and Wood (*Hegel's Ethical Thought*, 1990) answer that ethical life is rational only when it secures the subjective freedom that morality claims, so the right of criticism is kept, inside it (Neuhouser 2000 and Wood 1990 are checked in the SEP bibliography; their positions are from memory). That dispute is the subject of Argument 15.

**Argument 13. Property as the first embodiment of freedom (PhR §§41–51)**

*What it proves:* that property is justified not as a means to satisfying needs, nor as a reward for labor, but because a free will must have an external existence to be actual, and a thing is the first and simplest such existence; hence every person must have property, private property is more rational than common ownership, and how much each person owns is, at this level, no concern of right.

**Text and setting.** "Abstract Right", the first part of the *Philosophy of Right*, starts from the thinnest notion of a free agent, the person, who is a will related only to itself and capable of rights. Its first section, "Property", asks what such a will needs in order to be more than an inner capacity. The answer is the argument of §§41–51, and it is the first step in the book's long construction of freedom, which runs through contract, wrong, morality (Argument 12), the family, civil society (Argument 14) and the state (Argument 15). The SEP article on Hegel's social and political philosophy stresses that Hegel uses "Property", "Contract" and "Wrong" in uncommon ways: the aim is not an economics of ownership but the first ground for a free will's freedom, so that what and how much one owns is set aside (Brooks, checked). The rival theories are in the background. For [Locke](../john-locke/04-arguments.md) (Argument 14 there), property begins when a person mixes his labor with what is held in common; for [Hobbes](../thomas-hobbes/04-arguments.md), there is no property without a sovereign to define it (Lev. 24). Hegel's ground is neither labor nor the state, but the will. Week 7 of the study plan reads these sections.

1. "A person must give to his freedom an external sphere, in order that he may reach the completeness implied in the idea" (PhR §41; Dyde p. 48). A will that exists only inwardly is a capacity, not yet an actual freedom.
2. What is distinct from the free will is "an object, something not free, impersonal and without rights" (PhR §42; Dyde p. 49). A thing has no end of its own.
3. So a person may put his will into any thing. The thing "receives its meaning and soul from his will", and "Mankind has the absolute right to appropriate all that is a thing" (PhR §44; Dyde p. 51). Appropriating is "at bottom only to manifest the majesty of my will towards things" (PhR §44 A; Dyde p. 51).
4. In possession the will gets "a tangible existence", and "in this way first became an actual will" (PhR §45; Dyde p. 52). That is the rational point of property, not the satisfaction of wants: "it is really the first embodiment of freedom and an independent end" (PhR §45 R; Dyde p. 52).
5. Since the will embodied is a particular person's, property is in principle private: "Since property makes objective my personal individual will, it is rightly described as a private possession" (PhR §46; Dyde p. 52). Plato's *Republic* "does a wrong to the person, in regarding him as unable to hold property" (PhR §46 R; Dyde p. 53).
6. The will must be shown in the thing, not only meant: "it is not enough that I represent it as mine and internally will it to be mine; I must also take it over into my possession", so that "The embodiment of my will can then be recognized by others as mine" (PhR §51; Dyde p. 57).
7. The rational element in property is "that it is I who own property"; the rest, how much and of what, depends on wants, talents and circumstances. "Hence what and how much I possess is from the standpoint of right a matter of indifference" (PhR §49; Dyde p. 55).

Therefore each person has a right to property as such, and an equal one: "Men are equal, it is true, but only as persons, that is, only with reference to the source of possession. Accordingly every one must have property" (PhR §49 A; Dyde p. 56). Equal shares, by contrast, are no demand of right, for "justice demands merely that every one should have property" (PhR §49 A; Dyde p. 56).

**What each premise rests on.** Premise 1 is the central thesis of Hegel's practical philosophy, already present in the struggle for recognition and the bondsman's work (Arguments 7 and 8): freedom is actual only when it is embodied in something outside the self that others can see. Premise 2 is the post-Kantian distinction between persons and things, with a Hegelian twist in the Remark to §44: the free will's appropriation of things is a practical refutation of philosophies that ascribe to things an independent reality or declare them unknowable, so property has an idealist meaning. Premise 3 is a strong claim, and it includes animals, which Hegel treats as things in this respect (§44 A). Premise 5 rests on the particularity of the will: a will that is "this" will must have something that is "mine" (§46 A). Premise 6, the requirement of taking possession, is where recognition by others enters, and it points forward to contract, where two wills recognize each other as owners; the SEP article reads this as the stage at which property is determined "within the context of a common will" (PR §71, quoted there, checked). Premise 7 separates the right to property from questions of distribution, which Hegel assigns to civil society.

**Objection.** Two objections, one from the left and one from analytic political philosophy. Marx, in the *Critique of Hegel's Philosophy of Right* (1843), attacked the political use Hegel made of property, especially entailed landed estates, which in the *Philosophy of Right* qualify their holders for the upper house; on Marx's reading the state becomes the property of private property, and the abstract person of §41 is the bourgeois owner presented as the human being as such (from memory). The *Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts* of 1844 go further: private property is the product of alienated labor, not the embodiment of freedom (from memory). The second objection is Jeremy Waldron's (*The Right to Private Property*, 1988, from memory). Waldron reads Hegel's argument as the clearest case of a "general-right" argument: if owning property is necessary to a person's development as a free agent, then every person has a right to own something, and the argument supports a duty to see that the propertyless acquire property. Hegel says as much ("every one must have property"), and then in premise 7 declares distribution a matter of indifference to right. Either the argument justifies existing holdings, and then it does not show that each must have property, or it shows that each must have property, and then it condemns a society in which many have none.

**Reply.** The literature's, with Hegel's text. On Waldron's dilemma, Hegel takes the second horn, but locates it later in the book. In "Abstract Right" the question is only what a free will requires, and the answer is property as such; the abstract person cannot ask "How much do I possess?", since that question arises only for "particular persons" with wants and talents (§49 A). Civil society is where those persons live, and there Hegel admits that the market produces a class without property and treats it as a defect the system has not solved (Argument 14). On this reading the property argument is not an apology for existing distributions; it is the standard by which civil society's failure is measured. Peter Stillman ("Property, Freedom and Individuality in Hegel's and Marx's Political Thought", 1980) reads Hegel and Marx as sharing the view that property matters for individuality, and differing over whether the market can deliver it (title checked in the SEP bibliography; the reading from memory). On Marx's first point, defenders such as Wood and Neuhouser concede that the entailed estates are among the weakest parts of the book, an accommodation to the landed nobility that the property argument itself does not require (from memory). On Locke, a Hegelian can add that labor is one way of taking possession, forming a thing (§56), but not the ground of right; the ground is that a will has made itself visible in the thing and is recognized there.

**Where scholars disagree.** (a) What is property for? On the developmental reading, which Alan Patten (*Hegel's Idea of Freedom*, 1999) and Dudley Knowles have defended in different forms, property matters because owning and using things helps persons come to see themselves as free agents (from memory; Patten 1999 is listed in SEP among non-metaphysical readings, checked). On the more strictly conceptual reading, property is simply what freedom's first existence consists in, and the developmental benefits are consequences, not grounds; the SEP article's emphasis on self-consciousness (citing Benhabib 1984) leans toward the developmental reading (checked). (b) How liberal is Hegel's account? Joachim Ritter (*Person und Eigentum*, 1961, from memory) read §§41–51 as the philosophical recognition of the modern, post-feudal person, freed from estate and status, and thus as a philosophy of the French Revolution's civil code. Marx and, later, Domenico Losurdo (from memory) read the same sections as the ideology of bourgeois ownership. (c) Does it justify private property in the means of production? Nothing in §§41–51 distinguishes kinds of things owned, and Hegel's exceptions (the elements of nature "cannot become private property", and the state may limit private ownership on rational grounds, §46 R) leave the question open. Readers on the left, such as Shlomo Avineri, take the account of poverty in civil society as Hegel's own admission that the property argument, followed through, calls for more than the market gives (SEP cites Avineri 1971 on poverty, checked).

**Argument 14. Civil society and the rabble (PhR §§182–256, esp. §§241–245)**

*What it proves:* that civil society, the market sphere in which each person pursues his own ends and depends on everyone else, is a real and necessary form of freedom, distinct from the state; but that the same dynamic which makes it rich produces a class sunk below the standard of life it treats as essential, and a frame of mind, the rabble's, that refuses the society which excludes it; and that civil society by its own means cannot cure this, so that it is driven beyond itself, abroad into colonies and upward into corporations and the state.

**Text and setting.** Hegel's "civil society" (*bürgerliche Gesellschaft*; Dyde's "civic community") is the second part of "Ethical Life", between the family and the state. The term itself marks a break. For [Hobbes](../thomas-hobbes/04-arguments.md) and [Locke](../john-locke/04-arguments.md) civil society meant political society, the commonwealth that ends the state of nature. Hegel separates the two. Contract theorists who represent the state as "a union of different persons" have described only civil society, he says (PhR §182 A; Dyde p. 186), and his own civil society is the sphere of needs, work, exchange, the administration of justice and what he calls the police, the public authority that regulates markets and provides welfare. He had read the political economists, "Smith, Say, Ricardo" (PhR §189 R; Dyde p. 193), and saw in their science the discovery of reason in the apparently random mass of individual choices. See [Adam Smith](../../eras/03-early-modern/adam-smith.md). The poverty sections, §§241–245, are short, and modern discussion has made them among the most read in the book. A note on the word: Hegel's term is *Pöbel*, which modern translations render "rabble"; Dyde, who is quoted here, translates it "pauper class". Week 7 of the study plan reads §§182–256 in selection.

1. In civil society "every one is an end to himself; all others are for him nothing. And yet without coming into relation with others he cannot realize his ends" (PhR §182 A; Dyde p. 186). Each person's pursuit of his own good, mediated by exchange, produces the good of others.
2. This is a genuine form of freedom, the right of particularity, which the ancient world did not recognize and which belongs to "the modern world which alone has permitted every element of the idea to receive its due" (PhR §182 A; Dyde p. 186).
3. But particularity unchecked has no limit. Civil society "affords a spectacle of excess, misery, and physical and social corruption" (PhR §185; Dyde p. 188).
4. As it grows, wealth concentrates and work is divided: "large fortunes are amassed. On the other side, there occur repartition and limitation of the work of the individual labourer and, consequently, dependence and distress in the artisan class" (PhR §243; Dyde p. 231).
5. Poverty in civil society is not only want. The poor lose "the opportunity to acquire skill or education, the benefit of the administration of justice, the care for health, even the consolation of religion" (PhR §241; Dyde p. 230). "When a large number of people sink below the standard of living regarded as essential for the members of society, and lose that sense of right, rectitude, and honour which is derived from self-support, a pauper class arises, and wealth accumulates disproportionately in the hands of a few" (PhR §244; Dyde p. 231).
6. The rabble is defined by a disposition, not by poverty alone: "Poverty does not of itself make a pauper. The pauper state implies a frame of mind, associated often with poverty, consisting in inner rebellion against the wealthy, against society, and against constituted authority" (PhR §244 A; Dyde p. 231). It "claims support as a right" without the self-respect of earning it (PhR §244 A; Dyde p. 232).
7. Civil society cannot cure this by its own means. Direct support from the rich or from foundations would give the poor subsistence without work, "contrary both to the principle of the civic community and to the feeling its members have of independence and honour"; work provided for them would increase production when there are already too few consumers (PhR §245; Dyde p. 232).
8. "There arises the seeming paradox that the civic community when excessively wealthy is not rich enough. It has not sufficient hold of its own wealth to stem excess of poverty and the creation of paupers" (PhR §245; Dyde p. 232).

Therefore "By means of its own dialectic the civic community is driven beyond its own limits" (PhR §246; Dyde p. 233): outward, into foreign markets and colonization (§§246–248), and inward and upward, into the corporation, which stands to its members "as a second family" (PhR §252; Dyde p. 236), and the state. The corporation is, with the family, the "second ethical root or basis of the state" (PhR §255; Dyde p. 237).

**What each premise rests on.** Premises 1 and 2 rest on Hegel's reading of political economy and on his philosophy of history, in which the free particularity of the individual is the principle of the modern, Christian and post-Revolutionary world (Argument 16). Premise 3 restates the Terror's lesson for the economy: a freedom with no internal measure destroys itself (Argument 10). Premise 4 is an observation of early industrial Britain that Hegel took from his reading of the British press and economists. Premises 5 and 6 rest on the property argument (Argument 13): if property is the first embodiment of freedom, and every one must have property, the propertyless are deprived of more than goods; they lose the recognition that ownership and self-support bring, and the frame of mind of premise 6 is a response to that loss. Premises 7 and 8 rest on an early theory of overproduction: Hegel thinks both alms and public works fail because the market's problem is too many goods for its purchasers, not too few. The "Note" to §245 adds that in Scotland the poor were left "to their fate" and directed to "public begging" as a way of preserving their sense of shame (PhR §245 R; Dyde p. 233), and Hegel reports it without endorsing or rejecting it.

**Objection.** Marx's, and it has two forms. In the *Critique of Hegel's Philosophy of Right* (1843) and its Introduction (1844), Marx turned Hegel's pauper class into the proletariat, a class in radical chains, whose condition is the dissolution of all classes and which can be emancipated only by emancipating society as a whole (from memory). On that reading, §§241–245 refute the rest of the book. Hegel has described a structural injustice produced by the market itself and has then claimed that the state reconciles the particular and the universal; but a state that leaves a class outside the recognition it promises is not the actuality of freedom. Frank Ruda (*Hegel's Rabble*, 2011, from memory) gives a modern version. The rabble claims rights without recognizing duties, and Hegel cannot answer it within the system, because the rabble's claim, that it has a right to exist without being given the means, is a claim the system both creates and cannot fit; Ruda reads the rabble as the point where the *Philosophy of Right* undoes itself. A liberal version of the objection says only that Hegel's remedies are weak: colonies export the problem, and corporations protect those who have a trade, not the "day-labourer" whom the Note to §252 distinguishes from the tradesman.

**Reply.** The literature's, and it is a partial reply. First, Hegel does not hide the problem. "The important question, how poverty is to be done away with, is one which has disturbed and agitated society, especially in modern times" (PhR §244 A; Dyde p. 232), and he states plainly that civil society is not rich enough to solve it. Shlomo Avineri (*Hegel's Theory of the Modern State*, 1972) remarked that this is almost the only place where Hegel leaves a problem open (from memory). Second, Hegel's remedies are not only colonies. The public authority has duties of welfare and education (§§239–240), and the corporation gives the worker a recognized place, an honor that does not depend on wealth (§253). Allen Wood (*Hegel's Ethical Thought*, 1990) and Frederick Neuhouser (*Foundations of Hegel's Social Theory*, 2000) argue that Hegel's framework, the claim that each member of society has a right to the conditions of self-respecting membership, supplies the criterion by which the poverty of civil society counts as a wrong, so the open problem is a problem within the theory, not a refutation of it (from memory). The SEP article agrees that Hegel "lacks any convincing solution to how the problem of poverty might be overcome short of changing the economic system" (citing Raymond Plant 1972, checked). Whether that concession sinks Hegel's claim that the modern state is rational depends on Argument 15.

**Where scholars disagree.** (a) Is the rabble a class or a disposition? Hegel's text supports both, since §244 defines it by a fall below a standard of living and §244 A by a frame of mind; the SEP article reads the rabble as a conviction of alienation more likely among the poor but not exclusive to them (Thom Brooks, citing Brooks 2020, checked). Ruda insists that the rich who think themselves above the law are a rabble too, and draws on Hegel's lectures of 1821–22, which speak of a rabble of the rich (from memory). (b) Does the problem refute the system? Marx and Ruda say yes; Avineri says it marks the limit of Hegel's political theory; Wood, Neuhouser and, in a different idiom, Axel Honneth (*Freedom's Right*, 2011, from memory), who treats the market as a sphere of social freedom to be judged by whether it delivers the recognition it promises, say that the problem is one the Hegelian framework can state and condemn. (c) How far is Hegel's civil society a defense of the market? Readers who stress §189 and Smith read Hegel as an early theorist of the market's rationality; readers who stress §§185 and 243–245 read him as one of its first critics; the SEP article calls Hegel's vision of the economy "pro-market" while noting the poverty problem it raises (checked), and Michael Hardimon and Jean Cohen and Andrew Arato have read Hegel's civil society as the ancestor of the modern concept of a public sphere between market and state (from memory).

**Argument 15. "What is rational is actual": the state as ethical life (PhR Preface, §§257–260)**

*What it proves:* that philosophy's task is to comprehend the rational structure already present in the institutions of its time, not to design a state as it ought to be; and that the modern state, as distinct from civil society, is rational because it unites subjective freedom, the right of each to pursue his particular ends, with a universal end that individuals know and will as their own.

**Text and setting.** The Preface to the *Philosophy of Right* is dated 25 June 1820, and the book appeared in late 1820 with 1821 on its title page, after the Carlsbad Decrees of September 1819 had brought university inspectors and press censorship to the German states (the Wikipedia article on the Decrees, saved, checked for the date and contents). The Preface is polemical. Its targets are J. F. Fries, whom Hegel attacks by name for founding the state on "heart, friendship, and inspiration", and the philosophers of "the ought" generally. Its most quoted sentence is the double saying (*Doppelsatz*). Dyde translates *wirklich* as "real": "What is rational is real; And what is real is rational" (PhR Preface; Dyde p. xxvii). Modern translations, and the library entry, give "actual", which is closer to Hegel's technical term, *Wirklichkeit*, and is used in this dossier outside quotations. The Preface ends with the owl of Minerva, which "takes its flight only when the shades of night are gathering" (PhR Preface; Dyde p. xxx). The argument then runs through the book and comes to a head at the start of "The State", §§257–260. Week 7 of the study plan reads the Preface and these sections.

1. Philosophy's task is to comprehend, not to prescribe: "To apprehend what is is the task of philosophy, because what is is reason", and "philosophy also is its time apprehended in thoughts" (PhR Preface; Dyde p. xxviii). A philosophy that "builds up a world as it ought to be" has "an existence merely in the unstable element of opinion" (PhR Preface; Dyde p. xxix).
2. "Actuality" does not mean whatever happens to exist. Hegel's own gloss, in the *Encyclopaedia*, is that "existence is in part mere appearance, and only in part actuality"; the contingent, the erroneous and the evil exist, but do not deserve "the emphatic name of an actual" (EL §6 R; Wallace pp. 10–11). What is actual is what exists in accordance with its concept.
3. So the double saying is a claim about two things: reason is not a mere ideal but has power to realize itself, and what is truly actual in the present world is rational. It is not a claim that every existing state, law or policy is justified.
4. The modern world contains the institutions whose concept Arguments 12 to 14 have traced: the family, civil society with its rights of property and particularity, and the state.
5. The state is not civil society. If its purpose were "the security and protection of property and personal freedom, the interest of the individual as such would be the ultimate purpose of the social union", and "It would then be at one's option to be a member of the state" (PhR §258 R; Dyde p. 240). This is Hegel's objection to the contract theories of Hobbes, Locke and Rousseau.
6. The state is "the realized ethical idea or ethical spirit" (PhR §257; Dyde p. 240): the will that knows and wills itself, which has its "direct and unreflected existence" in custom and its reflected existence in individuals' self-consciousness (PhR §257; Dyde p. 240). Rationality, "viewed abstractly, consists in the thorough unity of universality and individuality", and concretely "it is the unity of objective freedom with subjective freedom" (PhR §258 R; Dyde p. 241).
7. The modern state achieves that unity in a way the ancient polis could not: "The modern state has enormous strength and depth, in that it allows the principle of subjectivity to complete itself to an independent extreme of personal particularity, and yet at the same time brings it back into the substantive unity" (PhR §260; Dyde p. 248).

Therefore the modern state is "the embodiment of concrete freedom" (PhR §260; Dyde p. 248), and philosophy, finding it so, achieves what the Preface calls reconciliation: "To recognize reason as the rose in the cross of the present, and to find delight in it, is a rational insight which implies reconciliation with reality" (PhR Preface; Dyde p. xxix).

**What each premise rests on.** Premise 1 rests on Hegel's philosophy of history (Argument 16) and on the *Phenomenology*'s account of philosophy as the self-knowledge of spirit: thought comes after the shape of life it knows. Premise 2 rests on the *Logic*'s doctrine of actuality, the unity of essence and existence, which Hegel insists his critics should have consulted (EL §6 R). Premise 3 is an inference from 2, and it is the disputed step. Premise 5 rests on the distinction between civil society and the state (Argument 14), and on the claim that membership in a state is not optional in the way joining an association is. Premises 6 and 7 rest on the whole of "Ethical Life", and on the claim, made in the Remark to §258, that Rousseau found the right principle, the will, but conceived it only as the individual will, so that the state became "a contract, which is based upon caprice, opinion, and optional, explicit consent" (PhR §258 R; Dyde p. 242; see Argument 10).

**Objection.** The objection that defined the book's reputation for a century is that it blesses the Prussian state. Rudolf Haym (*Hegel und seine Zeit*, 1857) read the double saying as a justification of the absolutist Prussia Hegel lived under, and Karl Popper (*The Open Society and Its Enemies*, 1945) took the charge to the extreme, calling Hegel "the father of modern historicism and totalitarianism" (both as reported in the SEP article on Hegel's social and political philosophy, checked). Popper's evidence included the sentence "The state is the march of God in the world" (PhR §258 A; Dyde p. 247). There is a philosophical version of the charge that does not depend on Prussia. If philosophy may only comprehend what is, and the actual is rational, then philosophy has given up the standpoint from which an existing order can be criticized. The Preface's own image confirms it: an owl that flies at dusk can only record a form of life that has already grown old. And even granting the distinction between existence and actuality, someone has to say which existing institutions are actual, and Hegel's answer is the institutions of a constitutional monarchy of the 1820s, with entailed estates, a hereditary monarch and the exclusion of women from public life.

**Reply.** The literature's, built on Hegel's text. On the historical charge, the SEP article reports a virtual consensus that Popper was wrong: the detailed doctrines of the *Philosophy of Right* favored the aims of the Prussian reform era, which the reaction of 1819 opposed, and Hegel defended more democratic institutions than Prussia had, among them trial by jury (citing Avineri 1972 and Knox 1970, checked). The "march of God" sentence comes from the Additions compiled from student notes, not from Hegel's printed text, and Walter Kaufmann argued that it is better rendered as saying that it is the way of God in the world that there should be a state (SEP checked for the Addition point; Kaufmann's rendering from memory). On the philosophical charge, premise 2 is the answer: "actual" is a term of evaluation, so the double saying gives a standard. The *Encyclopaedia* gloss says that ordinary feeling already refuses to call a fortuitous existence actual. Read with the Addition to §258, which concedes that a state may "violate right principles" and be "defective in various ways" while still being a state (PhR §258 A; Dyde p. 247), the doctrine allows criticism of existing states by their own concept, which is the form of criticism Hegel himself practices on poverty (Argument 14). What it forbids is criticism from an ideal with no foothold in the present, the "ought" that Hegel thinks breeds the Terror (Argument 10). Whether that is a reasonable restriction or a disabling one is the real question. On entailed estates, monarchy and women, most modern defenders concede that Hegel's applications fall short of his principle (see the SEP article's sections on the family and on the state).

**Where scholars disagree.** (a) What does the double saying mean? Robert Stern ("Hegel's Doppelsatz: A Neutral Reading", 2006, checked in the SEP bibliography) argues that it is neither conservative nor progressive but a claim about the relation of reason and reality that both sides can accept; Dieter Henrich's 1983 edition of a student transcript of Hegel's 1819–20 lectures gave a variant in which the rational becomes actual and the actual becomes rational, which Karl-Heinz Ilting and others took as evidence that the printed version was shaped by fear of censorship (both from memory). (b) Is the *Philosophy of Right* liberal? The liberal reading of Avineri, T. M. Knox and Allen Wood (*Hegel's Ethical Thought*, 1990) stresses the reform program and the rights of subjectivity; Joachim Ritter (1957) read the book as the philosophical form of the French Revolution's principle, secured by institutions; critics from Haym to Popper, and on the left from Marx's 1843 *Critique* onward, read it as an apology for restoration (Avineri, Knox, Haym and Popper as reported in SEP, checked; Ritter from memory). The SEP article's own verdict is that Hegel's thought "resists tidy classification as conservative, progressive or anything else" (checked). (c) Metaphysical or not? Z. A. Pelczynski's view that the political philosophy can be read without the metaphysics, defended in various forms by Wood, Neuhouser, Alan Patten and Pippin, faces the metaphysical reading of Charles Taylor (*Hegel*, 1975), Beiser and others, on which the state's rationality depends on the *Logic*'s account of the concept (SEP, checked). The two readings meet over §258's claim that the state is "absolutely rational": the first takes it as a claim about the conditions of freedom, the second as a claim about spirit's self-actualization.

**Argument 16. World history as the progress of the consciousness of freedom (PH Introduction)**

*What it proves:* that history is not a heap of events but a rational process, the development of spirit's knowledge that it is free; that this knowledge has grown from the recognition that one is free, through the recognition that some are free, to the recognition that all human beings as such are free; and that reason achieves this through the passions and interests of individuals who do not intend it, so that the suffering of history is not meaningless, though it is not justified case by case.

**Text and setting.** Hegel lectured on the philosophy of world history five times in Berlin, from 1822–23 to 1830–31. He never published the lectures. Eduard Gans edited them from Hegel's manuscripts and students' notes in 1837, and Karl Hegel, the philosopher's son, re-edited them in 1840; J. Sibree translated the second edition for Bohn's Philosophical Library (London, 1857), and Sibree's prefatory matter records the editions. The text is therefore a reconstruction, as the library entry warns, and the Introduction, which combines a manuscript Hegel wrote in 1830 with notes from earlier courses, is its most reliable part. The Introduction distinguishes original, reflective and philosophical history, and states the thesis that the whole course depends on. "The only Thought which Philosophy brings with it to the contemplation of History, is the simple conception of Reason; that Reason is the Sovereign of the World; that the history of the world, therefore, presents us with a rational process" (PH Introduction; Sibree p. 9). Week 8 of the study plan reads the Introduction in selection, as the learning path does.

1. Spirit's essence is freedom, and spirit is self-conscious: what it is depends on what it knows itself to be. So "Universal History" is "the exhibition of Spirit in the process of working out the knowledge of that which it is potentially" (PH Introduction; Sibree p. 18).
2. A people that does not know that human beings as such are free is not free. "The Orientals have not attained the knowledge that Spirit — Man as such — is free; and because they do not know this they are not free. They only know that one is free" (PH Introduction; Sibree pp. 18–19), and the freedom of that one is "only caprice", so "That one is therefore only a Despot; not a free man" (PH Introduction; Sibree p. 19).
3. "The consciousness of Freedom, first arose among the Greeks, and therefore they were free; but they, and the Romans likewise, knew only that some are free, — not man as such", which is why "The Greeks, therefore, had slaves" (PH Introduction; Sibree p. 19).
4. "The German nations, under the influence of Christianity, were the first to attain the consciousness, that man, as man, is free" (PH Introduction; Sibree p. 19). The knowledge arose first in religion and had then to be worked into the secular world, its law and its institutions, which is the task of modern history (Argument 15).
5. So "The History of the world is none other than the progress of the consciousness of Freedom" (PH Introduction; Sibree pp. 19–20). The grades of that consciousness, "the Eastern nations knew only that one is free; the Greek and Roman world only that some are free; whilst we know that all men absolutely (man as man) are free", give the "natural division of Universal History" (PH Introduction; Sibree p. 20).
6. The agents of history are individuals moved by passion and interest, and "nothing great in the World has been accomplished without passion" (PH Introduction; Sibree p. 24). Some individuals, "World-Historical Individuals", pursue aims in which a new principle of the age is contained, without knowing it as such (PH Introduction; Sibree p. 30).
7. Reason uses these passions. "This may be called the cunning of reason, — that it sets the passions to work for itself, while that which develops its existence through such impulsion pays the penalty, and suffers loss" (PH Introduction; Sibree p. 34).

Therefore history, looked at philosophically, has a direction and a meaning, though not the one its actors intend, and not happiness: "The History of the World is not the theatre of happiness. Periods of happiness are blank pages in it" (PH Introduction; Sibree p. 28). The question of what all the suffering was for, which arises when we regard history "as the slaughter-bench at which the happiness of peoples, the wisdom of States, and the virtue of individuals have been victimised" (PH Introduction; Sibree p. 22), is answered by the growth of freedom, which is Hegel's secular theodicy.

**What each premise rests on.** Premise 1 rests on the *Phenomenology* and the *Encyclopaedia*'s philosophy of spirit: spirit is what makes itself, and knowing itself is part of what it makes. Premises 2 to 4 rest on Hegel's reading of Asian, Greek, Roman and Christian civilization, which the body of the lectures fills in at length and which depends on the European scholarship of the 1820s. Premise 4 also rests on the account of the unhappy consciousness and of Christianity as the religion in which the infinite worth of the individual is first known (Argument 9). Premise 5 is the summary, and it is the most quoted sentence of the lectures. Premises 6 and 7 rest on a view of action Hegel shares with Smith's invisible hand and Kant's unsocial sociability in the *Idea for a Universal History* (1784): the unintended results of self-interested acts can serve an end no one intends. See the [Kant dossier](../immanuel-kant/04-arguments.md), whose Argument 12 sets out nature's guarantee of perpetual peace on a similar structure.

**Objection.** Two objections. The first is Popper's. In *The Poverty of Historicism* (1957) and *The Open Society and Its Enemies*, Popper argued that there is no such thing as the meaning or direction of history, that claims to know history's laws are unfalsifiable, and that they license sacrificing present individuals to the supposed goal; Isaiah Berlin's lecture "Historical Inevitability" (1954) made the moral half of the charge, that a philosophy which sees great men as instruments of a world-spirit relieves them, and us, of responsibility (both from memory). The second objection is the one modern readers meet first. Hegel's division of world history puts Asia at the beginning and Germanic Europe at the end, and says of Africa that "it is no historical part of the World; it has no movement or development to exhibit" (PH, Geographical Basis of History; Sibree p. 103). Robert Bernasconi and others have argued that the scheme is not only factually wrong but racially structured, and that the claim "all are free" was made by a Europe that held slaves and colonies (from memory). If the scheme of premises 2 to 5 is Eurocentric prejudice, the "progress of the consciousness of freedom" is the self-congratulation of the culture that wrote it.

**Reply.** The literature's, and on the second objection a partial one. Against Popper, Hegel's claim is not a predictive law. The philosophy of history, like the owl of Minerva (Argument 15), looks back, and Hegel explicitly refuses to say what comes after the present. The Introduction even says that "what experience and history teach is this, — that peoples and governments never have learned anything from history" (PH Introduction; Sibree p. 6). What Hegel claims to show is a standard by which historical change can be judged: freedom as known and institutionalized. A defender such as Terry Pinkard or Robert Pippin reads that standard as the claim that our norms of freedom are the result of the failures of earlier norms, so that the history is a justification of the norms by their genealogy, not a metaphysical plan (from memory). Against Berlin, Hegel does not excuse world-historical individuals; the cunning of reason means that their private aims count for nothing in the result, and that what is particular in them "pays the penalty, and suffers loss". On the second objection, the reply has to separate the structure from the content. The structure of premises 1 and 5, that freedom is a historical achievement and that a society's freedom depends on whom it recognizes as free, does not depend on Hegel's ranking of civilizations, and it can be turned against him: Susan Buck-Morss (*Hegel and Haiti*, 2000, from memory) argued that the slave revolution in Saint-Domingue, which Hegel's journals covered, is a better example of the progress of freedom than Germanic Christianity. The content, Hegel's claims about Africa and Asia, cannot be saved and is now generally taken as a mark of the limits of his time, which by his own Preface philosophy cannot leap over.

**Where scholars disagree.** (a) Metaphysical or not? On the metaphysical reading, defended by Charles Taylor (*Hegel*, 1975) and Frederick Beiser (*Hegel*, 2005), the philosophy of history is a theodicy in which a cosmic spirit realizes itself; on the non-metaphysical reading of Pinkard and Pippin it is an account of how human beings have come to hold themselves to norms of freedom (from memory; the two camps are as SEP's "Hegel" lists them, checked). (b) How Eurocentric is it, and does it matter? Readers such as Bernasconi and Buck-Morss treat the Eurocentrism as structural; others, such as Alison Stone and Pinkard, argue that the logic of freedom can be detached from Hegel's geography and turned into a critique of it (from memory). (c) Does history end? Alexandre Kojève's lectures (1933–39) read Hegel as announcing the end of history with Napoleon, and Francis Fukuyama's *The End of History and the Last Man* (1992) took Kojève's reading into politics; most Hegel scholars reply that Hegel's claim is that the principle of freedom for all has been reached, not that history has stopped, and the Introduction's own warning that philosophy cannot see beyond its time cuts against any end-of-history prophecy (from memory; SEP "Hegel" mentions Kojève's influence, checked).
