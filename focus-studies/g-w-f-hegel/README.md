# Focus Study: G. W. F. Hegel (1770 – 1831)
**English** | [简体中文](../../zh/focus-studies/g-w-f-hegel/README.md)

## The figure in brief

Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel was born in Stuttgart in August 1770, the son of a revenue official in the Duchy of Württemberg. At the Tübingen Seminary he shared rooms and enthusiasms with the poet Friedrich Hölderlin and the young Friedrich Schelling, and the three greeted the French Revolution together. After years as a house tutor in Bern and Frankfurt, he joined Schelling at Jena in 1801, and there, as Napoleon's army took the city in October 1806, he finished the *Phenomenology of Spirit*. A spell as a newspaper editor in Bamberg was followed by eight years as headmaster of the Nuremberg Gymnasium, where he married Marie von Tucher and wrote the *Science of Logic* (1812–16). The *Encyclopaedia of the Philosophical Sciences* (1817) came out of a professorship at Heidelberg, and from 1818 he held Fichte's former chair in Berlin, where he published the *Philosophy of Right* (1820) and lectured to large audiences on history, art, religion and the history of philosophy. He died in Berlin in November 1831, during a cholera epidemic. His students edited the lectures from their notes, and much of what readers know as "Hegel" comes from those editions.

He matters to this library because he made philosophy historical and social. Where Kant asked what the mind must be like for experience to be possible, Hegel asked how mind itself is formed: through the failures of each attempt to know, through the struggle for recognition between self-conscious beings, and through the institutions of family, civil society and state. His claim that "The truth is the whole" (in Baillie's 1910 wording) and that substance is also subject sets the agenda for Marx, Kierkegaard and the British idealists, for Kojève's Paris and the Frankfurt School, and for the recent analytic return to him in Sellars, McDowell, Brandom and Pippin.

## Why he rewards weeks of study

First, *the method*. The *Phenomenology* follows a series of "shapes of consciousness", each of which takes a standard of truth, tests itself against it and fails in a way that produces the next. Once the pattern is clear, the book stops looking like a fog of abstractions and becomes a sequence of arguments with definite premises.

Second, *the arguments*. The refutation of sense-certainty, the struggle for recognition and the reversal of lordship and bondage, the critique of Kant's moral law as empty, the account of property, civil society and the "rabble", and the claim that history is the progress of the consciousness of freedom are each among the most influential arguments of the last two centuries. Each can be put in numbered premises, and each has a live dispute about what it shows.

Third, *the myths*. Hegel is unusually often known through formulas he did not use or did not mean: "thesis, antithesis, synthesis", "the state is God walking on earth", the "end of history", the Prussian apologist. Reading him in guided sections lets the actual claims replace the slogans.

## The study plan at a glance

The library's [learning path](../../learning-path.md) meets Hegel in **Stage 4** (Nineteenth Century: System, Revolt, Society) at row 2, in excerpts only: the *Phenomenology* Preface in selection and lordship and bondage (¶¶178–196), with the *Philosophy of History* Introduction in selection. The path warns that this is the stage's most common abandonment point and says not to read the whole *Phenomenology* or the *Science of Logic* alone. This dossier goes well beyond those excerpts on purpose, but always in guided sections, never cover to cover. [07-study-plan.md](07-study-plan.md) lays out a **9-week plan** at the path's weekly rhythm, and also a short route (the path's own excerpts plus Weeks 3 and 7) for a reader who wants less:

## On the quotations

The dossier quotes only public-domain translations, so that every quotation can be checked word for word: J. B. Baillie's *Phenomenology of Mind* (1910), William Wallace's *Logic of Hegel* (Encyclopaedia Logic, 1892) and *Philosophy of Mind* (1894), S. W. Dyde's *Philosophy of Right* (1896) and J. Sibree's *Philosophy of History* (1857). For reading, the dossier recommends Miller or Pinkard for the *Phenomenology*, Geraets, Suchting and Harris for the *Encyclopaedia Logic*, and Nisbet's translations for the *Philosophy of Right* and the *Introduction to the Philosophy of World History*.

The *Phenomenology* is cited by the standard paragraph numbers of Miller's and Pinkard's editions (PhG ¶178), with Baillie's page where useful; Baillie prints no paragraph numbers, so each passage was matched to its number. The *Encyclopaedia* and the *Philosophy of Right* are cited by Hegel's own sections (EL §82, PhR §258), with "R" for a Remark and "A" for an Addition; the *Philosophy of History* by its parts.
