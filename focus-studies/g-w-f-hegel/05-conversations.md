# Hegel in Conversation — Backward, Sideways, Forward
**English** | [简体中文](../../zh/focus-studies/g-w-f-hegel/05-conversations.md)

*Focus study: G. W. F. Hegel · [Dossier home](README.md) · [Library entry](../../eras/04-19th-century/g-w-f-hegel.md)*

Hegel made conversation with his predecessors part of his method. A philosophy, on his account, is refuted not by being thrown away but by being shown to be one stage of a fuller one: "To refute a philosophy is to exhibit the dialectical movement in its principle, and thus reduce it to a constituent member of a higher concrete form of the Idea" (EL §88 Z; Wallace p. 168). He applied the rule to everyone. Heraclitus, Plato, Aristotle and Spinoza became moments of his system; Kant, Fichte, Jacobi and Schelling became the philosophies of reflection it was meant to complete; the Scottish economists became the first theorists of civil society. A thinker who absorbs everyone invites the charge that he has heard no one, and Hegel's forward conversations are mostly with people who refused to be absorbed: Schopenhauer and Kierkegaard, Feuerbach and Marx, Russell and Popper. The scholarly controversies and the reception by period are in [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md), the life behind the quarrels is in [01-life-and-times.md](01-life-and-times.md), and the arguments cited by number are reconstructed in [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md).

Hegel is quoted in the dossier's public-domain translations, in their own spelling ("Mind" for *Geist*, "Notion" for *Begriff*): Baillie's *Phenomenology of Mind* (1910), Wallace's *Logic of Hegel* (1892) and *Philosophy of Mind* (1894), Dyde's *Philosophy of Right* (1896) and Sibree's *Philosophy of History* (1857). *Phenomenology* paragraphs carry the Miller/Pinkard numbers, with Baillie's page. Read the texts in the modern editions [02-works-and-texts.md](02-works-and-texts.md) recommends. Everyone else is paraphrased; what rests on memory is flagged.

## Backward: what he inherited and what he revised

### Heraclitus

The [Heraclitus](../../eras/01-ancient/heraclitus-of-ephesus.md) entry counts the unity of opposites among his legacies "through Plato and later Hegel", and Hegel agreed: "As the first concrete thought-term, Becoming is the first adequate vehicle of truth. In the history of philosophy, this stage of the logical Idea finds its analogue in the system of Heraclitus" (EL §88 Z; Wallace p. 168). The Eleatics stand for pure being, which proves empty; Heraclitus, for whom all things flow, stands for becoming, the unity of being and nothing (Argument 11). The SEP adds that the lectures on the history of philosophy rank Zeno and Heraclitus above Pythagoras because they grasp what is at the heart of things "in more dynamic ways". The famous boast that no proposition of Heraclitus is missing from the *Logic* comes from the posthumous lecture volumes and is not checked here; read it with the [works file](02-works-and-texts.md)'s caution about lectures assembled from students' notes.

### Plato

[Plato](../../eras/01-ancient/plato.md) supplies dialectic and the *Republic*. For Hegel, the SEP reports, the thought that grasps the unity of opposites "comes to fruition in Plato's dialogue *Parmenides*" (Argument 11). The *Republic* is the surprising debt. The Preface to the *Philosophy of Right* insists that "even Plato's 'Republic,' now regarded as the byword for an empty ideal, has grasped" the essence of Greek ethical life (PhR Preface; Dyde p. xxvi), and the account of civil society names its limit: "Plato in his 'Republic' represents the substantive ethical life in its ideal beauty and truth", but he could meet the new principle of independent particularity only by excluding it, abolishing private property and the family (PhR §185 R; Dyde p. 188). That contrast is Hegel's political project in small. Plato had ethical life without subjective freedom; the modern state must have both (Arguments 13–15). Popper later paired them the other way, as the two great enemies of the open society.

### Aristotle

[Aristotle](../../eras/01-ancient/aristotle.md) is the predecessor Hegel praised most. The *Philosophy of Mind* calls his books on the soul "still by far the most admirable, perhaps even the sole, work of philosophical value on this topic" (EM §378; Wallace 1894). The *Phenomenology* Preface uses his teleology to explain substance as subject: nature is purposive activity, so the result is the same as the beginning (PhG ¶22; Argument 2). And the 1830 *Encyclopaedia* ends with "The eternal Idea, in full fruition of its essence, eternally sets itself to work, engenders and enjoys itself as absolute Mind" (EM §577; Wallace 1894), followed, in Greek and without comment, by Aristotle's passage on the divine thought that thinks itself (Wallace's reference reads "*Met.* XI. 7", the chapter now cited as *Metaphysics* Λ.7). The [Aristotle dossier](../aristotle/05-conversations.md) tells this from the other end. The limit is the polis. The Greeks knew only that some are free, and "Even Plato and Aristotle did not know this" (PH Introduction; Sibree p. 19), that is, that man as such is free (Argument 16).

### Spinoza

[Spinoza](../../eras/03-early-modern/baruch-spinoza.md) is the measure for Hegel's first principle: "everything depends on grasping and expressing the ultimate truth not as Substance but as Subject as well" (PhG ¶17; Baillie p. 15; Argument 2). The *Encyclopaedia Logic* spells the correction out twice. Against the charge of atheism, the system "was not Atheism but Acosmism" (EL §50 R; Wallace p. 106): it denies not God but the world, since finite things vanish into substance. And it lacks a term: "That He is the absolute Person however is a point which the philosophy of Spinoza never reached" (EL §151 Z; Wallace p. 274). The [Spinoza dossier](../baruch-spinoza/05-conversations.md) notes that later scholarship treats acosmism as Hegel's construction, since the *Ethics* grants modes a reality of their own. How Spinozist Hegel himself is remains disputed: Beiser reads substance-as-subject as Spinoza's substance made organic and purposive, Pippin as Kant's subject made social (04, Argument 2).

### Rousseau

The young Hegel's [Rousseau](../../eras/03-early-modern/jean-jacques-rousseau.md) was the theorist of a people that gives itself its laws ([01-life-and-times.md](01-life-and-times.md)). The mature Hegel kept the principle and indicted its application. Rousseau has "the merit of discovering and presenting a principle, which comes up to the standard of the thought, and is indeed thinking itself", namely will; but he conceived will only as the individual's, the state became a contract of particular wills, and when this abstraction came to power "there was enacted the most tremendous spectacle which the human race has ever witnessed" (PhR §258 R; Dyde pp. 241–242). The *Phenomenology*'s absolute freedom tells the same story: "the universal will", admitting no particular institutions, can only destroy (PhG ¶¶582–595; Argument 10). Yet the SEP judges Hegel's own state "a complicated transformation" of Rousseau's and Kant's (Argument 15). Whether Hegel escaped the danger he diagnosed, or, as Isaiah Berlin held (from memory), shared Rousseau's positive liberty, is weighed under Argument 10.

### Kant

[Kant](../../eras/03-early-modern/immanuel-kant.md) is the predecessor Hegel answered most often, and the [Kant dossier](../immanuel-kant/05-conversations.md) calls the verdict "double, deliberately". Kant revived "the consciousness of Reason, or the absolute inwardness of thought" (EL §60 R; Wallace p. 118), then stopped short three times, and each stop is an argument here. He asked that knowledge be examined before it is used, and Hegel replied, "But to seek to know before we know is as absurd as the wise resolution of Scholasticus, not to venture into the water until he had learned to swim" (EL §10 R; Wallace p. 17); the *Phenomenology* Introduction answers by testing each shape of knowing by its own standard (PhG ¶¶73–84; Argument 1). He explained the finitude of the categories by their subjectivity: "Kant however holds that what we think is false, because it is we who think it" (EL §60 Z; Wallace p. 119), whereas for Hegel categories are finite in themselves and the *Logic* shows their limits from inside (Arguments 3–5, 11). And he gave the will an empty law: without ethical life "moral science is converted into mere rhetoric about duty for duty's sake" (PhR §135 R; Dyde p. 128), since non-contradiction condemns theft only once property is assumed (Argument 12). Kantians reply that the formula of humanity gives the law content Hegel ignores; 04 sets out the exchange. In the debate over Hegel himself, Pippin's post-Kantian reading treats the whole system as Kant's self-determining reason made social and historical (SEP).

### Fichte

[Fichte](../../eras/04-19th-century/johann-gottlieb-fichte.md) gave Hegel the problem of the *Logic*, a vocabulary wrongly fastened on it, and recognition. The problem: "To the philosophy of Fichte belongs the great merit of having called attention to the need of exhibiting the necessity of these categories and giving a genuine deduction of them" (EL §42 R; Wallace p. 87). But the I needs a shock from outside to set it moving, and so "Fichte, in consequence, never advanced beyond Kant's conclusion, that the finite only is knowable, while the infinite transcends the range of thought" (EL §60 Z; Wallace p. 120). The vocabulary is "thesis, antithesis, synthesis". The terms are Fichte's; G. E. Mueller's "The Hegel Legend of 'Thesis-Antithesis-Synthesis'" (1958), which the SEP's "Hegel's Dialectics" cites as the classic criticism, traced their attachment to Hegel to Heinrich Moritz Chalybäus's lectures of 1837 ([02-works-and-texts.md](02-works-and-texts.md)). Hegel's own terms run abstract, negative, concrete (the library entry). Recognition is the real debt. Fichte's *Foundations of Natural Right* (1796–97) argued that I become aware of my freedom only when another free being summons me, and the SEP's "Recognition" makes this *Aufforderung* the source of Hegel's idea (Arguments 6–7). The Fichte entry warns against treating him as a stepping-stone, and Ludwig Siep and Axel Honneth hold that Hegel's Jena theory of recognition, closer to Fichte's, is better than the *Phenomenology*'s struggle (04, Argument 7).

### Jacobi

Friedrich Heinrich Jacobi (1743–1819) opened the pantheism controversy in 1785 with the report that Lessing had confessed himself a Spinozist, and drew the moral that demonstration ends in fatalism, so that God and freedom are reached only by a leap of faith, an immediate knowledge (from memory; unchecked). *Faith and Knowledge* (1802) groups him with Kant and Fichte as a philosopher of reflection ([02-works-and-texts.md](02-works-and-texts.md)), and the *Encyclopaedia* gives immediate knowledge its own "attitude of thought to objectivity" (EL §§61–78). Hegel grants the diagnosis. The categories of the understanding are "in their very import finite", and a science advancing from law to law meets only matter, never God (EL §62 R; Wallace p. 123). He rejects the cure: Jacobi's faith has "no other authority than that of a personal revelation" and so "equally admits a belief in the divinity of the Dalai-lama" (EL §63 R; Wallace p. 125). Sense-certainty is, among other things, a refutation of immediacy as knowledge (Argument 3). Jacobi has no library entry.

### Smith, Steuart and the political economists

Hegel was the first major philosopher to build political economy into a theory of the state. "This science has sprung from the soil of modern times", he says, and names Smith, Say and Ricardo as its practitioners (PhR §189 R; Dyde p. 193). His first teacher was Sir James Steuart, whose *Inquiry into the Principles of Political Oeconomy* (1767) Wikipedia calls the first full-fledged treatise of economics. Rosenkranz, who had the manuscript, reports that Hegel wrote a commentary on the German translation of Steuart from 19 February to 16 May 1799, fighting what was dead in Steuart's mercantilism and trying to save the human heart amid competition and the mechanism of labor (Rosenkranz, *Hegel's Leben*, 1844, p. 86, paraphrased from the German). The commentary, which Rosenkranz says was still complete in 1844, is now lost (the loss from memory; unchecked). [Adam Smith](../../eras/03-early-modern/adam-smith.md) is the larger presence. The SEP reports that Hegel agreed with Smith that the market turns "subjective selfishness" into a contribution to everyone's needs (PhR §199), and denied that its plenty trickles down: the unfettered market produces a class caught in poverty, which the state must contain by welfare measures (Argument 14). Lukács's *The Young Hegel* (1948) traced even the formative labor of lordship and bondage to Smith (04, Argument 8).

## Sideways: friends, rivals and opponents

### Schelling

[Schelling](../../eras/04-19th-century/friedrich-schelling.md) was Hegel's room-mate, patron, collaborator and, for twenty-five years, his rival. [01-life-and-times.md](01-life-and-times.md) tells the early story: Tübingen, the Jena post, the *Difference* essay of 1801 for Schelling against Fichte, the *Critical Journal* of 1802–03. The break came with the *Phenomenology* Preface, two of whose images were read as aimed at Schelling's Absolute, the point of indifference of subject and object: the Absolute given out "as the night in which, as we say, all cows are black — that is the very naivete of vacuous knowledge" (PhG ¶16; Baillie p. 15), and the enthusiasm that begins with absolute knowledge "as if shot out of a pistol, and makes short work of other points of view simply by explaining that it is to take no notice of them" (PhG ¶27; Baillie p. 25). Hegel said he meant the imitators; Schelling took it personally, and "their friendship abruptly ended" (SEP; Argument 1).

The quarrel outlived Hegel. The SEP's "Schelling" dates the public criticism to Erlangen in the 1820s, Munich in the 1830s and Berlin from 1841, when he was called to Hegel's old chair to stamp out, in the library entry's phrase, "the dragon-seed of Hegelian pantheism". Hegel's *Logic*, on this criticism, is a complete negative philosophy: it shows what things must be if they exist, but not why anything exists, since it begins from a being that is only a concept. Either the concept comes first, and being is no longer absolute, or being comes first and we must begin "with being without the concept" (SW II/3, p. 164, the SEP's translation). That is a direct challenge to Argument 11, and through Kierkegaard and other Berlin hearers it passed into existentialism. Hegel's reply is the *Logic*'s beginning itself: an identity that does not differentiate itself is an abstraction, and pure being is not opaque to thought but the emptiest thought, which shows its own emptiness (EL §§86–88). The Schelling entry warns that the "night" is a polemic, not Schelling's doctrine, and that partisan verdicts on the feud should be distrusted.

### Hölderlin

Friedrich Hölderlin (1770–1843), Hegel's closest friend at Tübingen and again in Frankfurt, where he found Hegel his post in 1797, mattered philosophically: until about 1800, the SEP says, Hegel's turn to Kant and Fichte came "under the influence of Hölderlin and Schelling". In the fragment "Judgment and Being" (usually dated 1795), Hölderlin argued that self-consciousness already divides subject from object, so it presupposes an unknowable prior unity, being, and that judgment (*Ur-teil*) is the original division (from memory; unchecked; Dieter Henrich's studies made the fragment central). Hegel kept the etymology and reversed the conclusion. "Reprising an etymological point made by Hölderlin", the SEP notes, the *Logic* treats judgment as a separation that the judging subject affirms as a unity; for Hegel the unity is reached by thought, not lost behind it. The second debt is love, which in the Frankfurt manuscripts unites what Kant's law divides, and which the SEP traces into the *Philosophy of Right*'s family as "the types of human inter-subjectivity earlier broached by Hölderlin and the romantics" (Arguments 6–7). Hölderlin has no library entry.

### Schleiermacher

[Schleiermacher](../../eras/04-19th-century/friedrich-schleiermacher.md), Berlin's most celebrated preacher, was Hegel's colleague from 1818 and his rival in the philosophy of religion. The library entry gives the sharpest line: in his 1822 foreword to Hinrichs's book on religion, Hegel remarked that if religion were the feeling of dependence, "the dog would be the best Christian" (the entry's English; the foreword is not saved here). The charge is the one he made against Jacobi: feeling without concepts is mere subjective certainty. His alternative is Week 8's absolute spirit, in which religion and philosophy have the same content, religion in pictorial representation and philosophy in concepts (Enc. §§564–573). The entry adds the defense: Schleiermacher's feeling is immediate self-consciousness, a structure of awareness, not an emotion. The rivalry was also institutional; the SEP's "Feuerbach" records that the young Feuerbach came to Berlin in 1824 on the pretext of studying with Schleiermacher, but in fact for Hegel.

### Goethe

Goethe is the contemporary Hegel admired without reserve. In the paragraphs Week 6 reads, on the understanding, he cites him for the virtue of limits: "The man who will do something great must learn, as Goethe says, to limit himself" (EL §80 Z; Wallace p. 145). Goethe oversaw Jena University as Weimar's minister when Hegel taught there ([01-life-and-times.md](01-life-and-times.md)). The philosophical link is the theory of colors: Hegel took Goethe's side against Newton's account of white light, a stance the SEP calls more controversial than his support of Kepler (PN §320 Addition), adding that his quarrel was with what he took to be Newton's "monstrous metaphysics" rather than his science. Goethe's primal phenomenon, seen in nature rather than inferred behind it, is close to the criticism of a hidden world of forces in Argument 5. Goethe has no library entry.

### Schopenhauer: the Berlin lecture clash

[Arthur Schopenhauer](../../eras/04-19th-century/arthur-schopenhauer.md) chose to be Hegel's rival. In 1820, as the [Schopenhauer dossier](../arthur-schopenhauer/01-life-and-times.md) tells it, he qualified as a *Privatdozent* at Berlin before a faculty that included Hegel, then announced his lectures for the same hour as Hegel's. Students chose Hegel; Schopenhauer's room emptied after one semester, and he never taught again. (The story of a trial-lecture exchange about a horse lying down in the street is not checked here.) The polemic ran one way. The library entry reports that he despised Hegel as a charlatan and obscurantist, and the [Schopenhauer dossier](../arthur-schopenhauer/05-conversations.md) records "On University Philosophy" (*Parerga*), which lumps Fichte, Schelling and Hegel together as sophists building systems to flatter state and church; it judges, rightly, that he never engaged the *Phenomenology* or the *Logic* on their own terms. The opposition is nonetheless real. For Hegel world history is the progress of the consciousness of freedom (Argument 16); for Schopenhauer the world's inner nature is a blind, purposeless will, a position the Schopenhauer entry calls "a direct inversion of Hegel, mounted at exactly the same time and place". The charge of obscurity outlived both men, and Popper took Schopenhauer's side (below).

## Forward: the heirs and the critics

### Feuerbach

The school split within a decade; the SEP, after John Toews (1985), groups the followers into right, center and left, and the left began with religion, in Strauss's *Life of Jesus* (1835). Ludwig Feuerbach had heard, the SEP reports, all of Hegel's Berlin lectures except those on aesthetics, the logic twice. He broke publicly in "Toward a Critique of the Hegelian Philosophy" (1839), which, as 04 reports, charged that the *Phenomenology*'s presuppositionless start is staged (Argument 1) and that sense-certainty refutes the word "this", not the sensible thing (Argument 3). *The Essence of Christianity* (1841) read God as humanity's own essence estranged, and the "Provisional Theses" (1842) turned the method on Hegel: speculative philosophy inverts subject and predicate, so the truth is Hegel turned right way up (SEP; Argument 2). The [Feuerbach and Stirner](../../eras/04-19th-century/sidebars-feuerbach-and-stirner.md) entry gives the sequel: Stirner's *The Ego and Its Own* (1844) called Feuerbach's "Man" a new god, and Marx called it an abstract essence without history.

### Marx and the Young Hegelians

[Marx](../../eras/04-19th-century/karl-marx.md) studied at Berlin, heard Hegel's pupil Gans on law, and joined the Young Hegelians around Bruno Bauer, whose dismissal in 1842 and the censorship of Ruge's journals broke the movement up (Wikipedia, "Young Hegelians", checked). His engagement with Hegel ran in three stages. The *Critique of Hegel's Philosophy of Right* (1843) applied Feuerbach's inversion to the state: Hegel makes the Idea the subject and family and civil society its predicates, and so dresses existing institutions as necessities of reason (Argument 15). The Paris manuscripts of 1844 praised the *Phenomenology* for grasping labor as human self-creation and charged that Hegel knew only abstract mental labor (Arguments 6, 8). The afterword to *Capital* (1873) said the dialectic stands on its head and must be turned right side up to find the rational kernel in the mystical shell (paraphrased from memory; unchecked). The deepest continuity is the rabble: the SEP observes that Marx took Hegel's account of a class produced and impoverished by the market as evidence for abolishing the property rights Hegel kept at the center of civil society (Argument 14). Western Marxism, from Lukács's *History and Class Consciousness* (1923), put more of Hegel back into Marx (SEP; Wikipedia).

### Kierkegaard

[Kierkegaard](../../eras/04-19th-century/soren-kierkegaard.md) heard Schelling in Berlin in 1841–42, delighted and then disappointed (the Schelling entry), and the *Concluding Unscientific Postscript* (1846) made Schelling's complaint existential: a logical system is possible, an existential system is not, since no existing person stands at its end (paraphrased; Arguments 1, 11). The unhappy consciousness, a stage for Hegel, is for Kierkegaard the lasting condition of the individual before God (Argument 9), and *Fear and Trembling* (1843) asks whether a duty to God can suspend the ethical life that Hegel made the home of duty (Argument 12). The SEP warns against the picture of an arch opponent: Niels Thulstrup (1967) held that Kierkegaard took little from Hegel, Jon Stewart (2003) that his targets were often Danish Hegelians such as Hans Lassen Martensen and that some of his central ideas are creative developments of Hegel's.

### The British Idealists: Green, Bradley, McTaggart

Hegel reached Britain late; Wikipedia dates the turn to James Hutchison Stirling's *The Secret of Hegel* (1865). The [British idealists](../../eras/04-19th-century/british-idealists.md) entry describes how T. H. Green and F. H. Bradley made idealism the leading philosophy in British universities from the 1870s. Green took from Hegel the view that relations are the work of mind and a positive freedom that society secures for its members, the basis of the New Liberalism (Arguments 13, 15). Bradley's *Ethical Studies* (1876) is the most Hegelian of their books: Essay IV attacks Kant's "duty for duty's sake", Dyde's very phrase in PhR §135 R (Argument 12), and Essay V, "My Station and Its Duties", finds the self in family, community and nation (Argument 15). Bradley's Absolute, a single experience beyond relations, is nearer to Schelling than to Hegel's self-differentiating subject. J. M. E. McTaggart reconstructed the *Logic* step by step in *Studies in the Hegelian Dialectic* (1896) and a *Commentary* (1910), concluding to a timeless community of selves without God (from memory; unchecked), and defended Hegel's retributivism in "Hegel's Theory of Punishment" (1896, in Wikipedia's bibliography). This British Hegel is the one Russell and Moore rejected.

### Dewey

[John Dewey](../../eras/05-20th-century/john-dewey.md) took his doctorate at Johns Hopkins in 1884 and wrote at first as a Hegelian; the library entry tells how Darwin and William James displaced the Hegelianism. What had drawn him, he recalled, was a demand for unification, an emotional craving that only an intellectual subject matter could satisfy, and he kept much of Hegel's account of history and society while rejecting absolute knowing (paraphrased from Wikipedia's quotation and summary, checked). Richard Bernstein, whom Wikipedia follows, counts three moments of Hegel in American pragmatism: the St. Louis *Journal of Speculative Philosophy* (from 1867), Dewey's generation, and the "Pittsburgh Hegelians" below (Arguments 7, 16).

### Croce

Benedetto Croce's *What Is Living and What Is Dead of the Philosophy of Hegel* (1907; English 1915) set the Italian program: keep the dialectic, drop the system. Its central charge, in outline, is that Hegel confused the dialectic of opposites, such as being and nothing, with the relation of distinct concepts, such as beauty and truth, and so forced art, nature and history into one chain of contradictions (from memory; unchecked; Arguments 11, 16). Wikipedia also makes Croce the canonical source of the claim that Hegel announced the death of art, which his defenders deny he held; the *Aesthetics* says only that art is no longer the highest form in which spirit knows itself, a different thesis (Week 8). Croce has no library entry.

### Kojève and the French reception: Hyppolite, Sartre

The French Hegel is largely Kojève's. The IEP reports that Alexandre Kojève took over Koyré's seminar at the École Pratique des Hautes Études and taught the *Phenomenology* there from 1933 to 1939; the lectures appeared as *Introduction à la lecture de Hegel* (1947; English 1969), and his hearers and readers included Merleau-Ponty, Lacan, Bataille, Queneau, Aron and Breton. He read Hegel "through the twin lenses of Marx's materialism and Heidegger's temporalised ontology" (IEP): desire is desire for another's desire, history begins in the struggle for recognition, the working slave is its motor, and it ends in universal, equal recognition (Arguments 6–8, 16). 04 records the verdict of scholars: the reading made the passage famous, made "slave" standard for *Knecht*, and goes far beyond the text. Jean Hyppolite, whom the IEP pairs with Kojève, translated the *Phenomenology* (1939–41) and wrote the standard French commentary (1946) (both from memory; unchecked). Through Strauss and Allan Bloom, Kojève's end of history reached Fukuyama's *The End of History and the Last Man* (1992). [Sartre](../../eras/05-20th-century/jean-paul-sartre.md)'s debt is argued: the IEP calls his attendance contested but judges Kojève's reading as important as Heidegger for *Being and Nothingness* (1943), whose look reworks master and slave without hope of reconciliation (Argument 7). Beauvoir and Fanon carried the dialectic into feminism and anti-colonial thought (04, Argument 8). A later French generation, the SEP adds, turned against Hegel in the 1960s.

### Adorno and the Frankfurt School

For the [Frankfurt School](../../eras/05-20th-century/frankfurt-school.md), the SEP's "Critical Theory" says, Hegel has been "a continual reference point", from Marcuse (1941) and Adorno (1963) to Benhabib and Honneth: they took his holism and immanent critique and rejected his metaphysical, teleological and reconciling tendencies. Adorno is the sharpest case. Against the Preface's "The truth is the whole" (PhG ¶20; Baillie p. 17; Argument 1), *Minima Moralia* (1951) answered that the whole is the false (paraphrased from memory; unchecked), and his negative dialectics rejects, in the SEP's phrase, "Hegel's affirmative synthesis" for the sake of the nonidentical. Axel Honneth went back to the Jena Hegel: *The Struggle for Recognition* (1992) rebuilds recognition through love, rights and esteem, and *Freedom's Right* (2011) rewrites the *Philosophy of Right* as a theory of social freedom (SEP; Arguments 7, 15).

### Russell and Moore's revolt

The SEP presents early analytic philosophy as a revolt against Hegel. Russell and Moore had been educated in British idealism (Wikipedia, checked) and turned on it at the end of the 1890s; Moore's "The Refutation of Idealism" (1903) attacked the claim that to be is to be experienced (from memory; unchecked). For Russell, the SEP reports, the new logic of Frege and Peano had "destroyed" Hegel's metaphysics by overturning the Aristotelian logic on which it rested, and for decades Hegel was seen in analytic philosophy as a historical figure of little genuine interest. Russell's target among the idealists was the doctrine of internal relations (from memory), and 04 takes up his charge that Hegel confused the "is" of predication with the "is" of identity (Argument 4). The [Russell](../../eras/05-20th-century/bertrand-russell.md) and [Moore](../../eras/05-20th-century/g-e-moore.md) entries give the programs that replaced him.

### Popper's attack

[Popper](../../eras/05-20th-century/karl-popper.md) gave the case against Hegel its widest audience. "What is Dialectic?" (1940) argued that admitting contradictions would mean the breakdown of science, since from a contradiction anything follows (SEP, "Hegel's Dialectics"; Argument 5). *The Open Society and Its Enemies* (1945) devoted a long chapter, "Hegel and the New Tribalism", to Hegel as the chief modern source of historicism and an apologist for Prussian power whose cult of the state prepared nationalism and racism (Wikipedia's summary, checked; Arguments 15–16). The replies came quickly. J. N. Findlay's introductory monograph (1958) was, the SEP says, apparently written as a riposte, and Walter Kaufmann's "The Hegel Myth and Its Method" (*From Shakespeare to Existentialism*, 1959, pp. 88–119) charged Popper with relying on a student anthology, stitching "quilt quotations" from different contexts, and taking an editor's Addition, the line on the state as the march of God (PhR §258 A), for Hegel's words. Kaufmann noticed too that Popper "constantly makes common cause with Schopenhauer". The library entry reports that most Hegel scholars now find Popper's reading "seriously distorted"; the [reception file](06-reception-and-debates.md) weighs what remains of the charge, chiefly the claims for war and the state (PhR §§257–260, 324).

### Taylor

Charles Taylor's *Hegel* (1975) did most to bring Hegel back into English-language philosophy. The SEP groups it with Michael Rosen (1984) and Beiser (2005) as the classic metaphysical reading, on which God or spirit becomes actual only through finite minds (Argument 2), and his essay on the opening of the *Phenomenology* (1972) reads sense-certainty as a transcendental argument (04, Argument 3). Its lasting influence is political: freedom situated in community made Hegel an ancestor of the communitarian critique of liberalism, and "The Politics of Recognition" (1992) carried Argument 7 into the debate on multiculturalism (from memory; unchecked). The library entry lists Taylor among those who "work in his shadow". Taylor has no library entry.

### The analytic return: Sellars, McDowell, Brandom, Pippin

Hegel returned to analytic philosophy through Kant. Wilfrid Sellars attacked the "myth of the given", the idea that some knowledge is had without concepts, and the SEP compares the argument to sense-certainty (Argument 3); Sellars is in the library's [group entry](../../eras/05-20th-century/analytic-philosophy-after-quine.md), and his own description of his lectures as incipient Hegelian meditations is from memory. John McDowell argued that experience is conceptual throughout and reads the self-consciousness chapter as the allegory of a single mind (04, Argument 6). Robert Brandom's *A Spirit of Trust* (2019) reads the *Phenomenology* as an account of conceptual norms instituted by reciprocal recognition, and determinate negation as material incompatibility (Arguments 3–4, 7). Robert Pippin (1989, 2019) and Terry Pinkard read Hegel as completing Kant's critique by making self-determination social and historical. Wikipedia reports Bernstein's name for McDowell and Brandom, the "Pittsburgh Hegelians", and that neither claims to explain Hegel as he understood himself.

The debate is live, and the SEP gives both sides. Stephen Houlgate, Robert Stern and Beiser hold that the *Logic* is an ontology, and Houlgate charges that Pippin misses its beginning in pure being; Pippin replies that Hegel has a metaphysics in a new sense, a science of the forms that make anything intelligible (Arguments 2, 11), which is why, the SEP notes, the label "non-metaphysical" has largely been dropped. Either way Russell's verdict has been reversed: by the century's end, in the SEP's words, analytic philosophers "had started to take Hegel seriously", though a favorable reassessment "has still a long way to go".

## The themes trace

The library's [problem index](../../themes.md) places Hegel in six of its nine threads, and this dossier argues for two more.

- [§1 Knowledge & Skepticism](../../themes.md#1-knowledge--skepticism--what-can-we-know-and-how-do-we-know-it) — Skepticism as a necessary station: consciousness tests its own standard and is driven beyond each shape. Weeks 1–4; Kant and Jacobi behind, Schelling beside, Kierkegaard, Sellars and McDowell ahead (Arguments 1, 3–5, 9).
- [§3 God & Religion](../../themes.md#3-god--religion--does-god-exist-and-what-follows-for-faith-and-reason) — Religion pictures what philosophy comprehends. Week 8; Spinoza and Jacobi behind, Schleiermacher and the late Schelling beside, Strauss, Feuerbach and Kierkegaard ahead (Argument 2).
- [§4 Ethics & the Good Life](../../themes.md#4-ethics--the-good-life--how-should-i-live-and-what-is-right) — Ethical life beyond Kantian morality. Weeks 5 and 7; Kant and Plato behind, Bradley, Taylor and Honneth ahead (Argument 12).
- [§5 Politics & Justice](../../themes.md#5-politics--justice--how-should-we-live-together-and-who-may-rule) — The state as the actuality of concrete freedom; property, civil society and the rabble. Week 7; Plato, Rousseau, Smith and Steuart behind, Marx, Green, Popper and the communitarians ahead (Arguments 10, 13–15).
- [§6 Self & Identity](../../themes.md#6-self--identity--what-am-i-and-what-makes-me-the-same-person-over-time) — Self-consciousness exists only in being recognized. Week 3; Fichte and Hölderlin behind, Kojève, Sartre, Beauvoir, Fanon, Honneth and Brandom ahead (Arguments 6–8).
- [§9 History & Progress](../../themes.md#9-history--progress--does-history-have-a-direction-and-can-we-know-it) — World history as the progress of the consciousness of freedom. Week 8; Kant behind, Schopenhauer beside, Marx, Croce, Kojève, Fukuyama and Popper ahead (Argument 16).

His absence from §7 and §8 is a filing gap. The [Language thread](../../themes.md#7-language--meaning--how-do-words-and-thoughts-hook-onto-the-world) is where sense-certainty belongs: the "this" that cannot be said without becoming a universal is an early argument that meaning is general and conceptual, and Sellars, McDowell and Brandom's inferentialism descend from it (Week 2; Arguments 3–4; Feuerbach against). The [Science thread](../../themes.md#8-science--method--how-should-inquiry-proceed-and-what-does-it-prove) is where dialectic belongs as a method, the refutation of a theory from inside by its own standard: the Introduction's test of each shape, the *Logic*'s immanent development of categories, the quarrel with Newton over a hidden world of forces (Weeks 1, 2 and 6; Arguments 1, 5, 11; Kant behind, Goethe beside, Popper's charge that contradiction ends science and the paraconsistent replies ahead). The corrections log proposes both additions.

[§2 Mind & Body](../../themes.md#2-mind--body--what-is-mind-and-how-does-it-relate-to-the-body) is a near miss. The *Philosophy of Mind* praises Aristotle's books on the soul above all others and makes mind the truth of nature, not a second substance beside it (EM §§378, 381), but the dossier reads it only in passing, and the thread's mind-body question is not the one Hegel asked.
