# Hegel Received: Right and Left, Inverted, Eclipsed, Rehabilitated, and the Quarrels Still Open
**English** | [简体中文](../../zh/focus-studies/g-w-f-hegel/06-reception-and-debates.md)

*Focus study: G. W. F. Hegel · [Dossier home](README.md) · [Library entry](../../eras/04-19th-century/g-w-f-hegel.md) · [Arguments, part 1](04-arguments.md) · [Arguments, part 2](04-arguments.md)*

Hegel died in November 1831 as the most famous philosopher in Germany, at the head of a school that held chairs, ran a review journal and was editing his collected works. Within ten years the school had split over the Gospels, the government had called Schelling to Berlin to counter it, and its most gifted pupils were turning Hegel's method against religion and the Prussian state. After 1848 German academics treated him, in Marx's phrase, as a "dead dog", while in Britain and America he became the founder of a new idealism. The analytic movement buried him, Kojève's Paris lectures resurrected him as the philosopher of desire, struggle and the end of history, and Popper indicted him in 1945 as a source of modern totalitarianism. The second half of the twentieth century produced a liberal Hegel (Kaufmann, Avineri, Wood), a cosmic Hegel (Taylor, Beiser), a post-Kantian Hegel of norms and social practice (Hartmann, Pippin, Pinkard, Brandom), a revised metaphysical Hegel (Houlgate, Stern), a theorist of recognition (Honneth), and a Eurocentric Hegel whose philosophy of history must be read against the colonial world it ignored (Bernasconi, Buck-Morss). This file tells that story by era, sets out four live debates with names on both sides, and closes with the standard misconceptions in full form and the myths about the life.

The dossier goes past the [learning path](../../learning-path.md) on purpose. The path sets only the *Phenomenology* Preface, Lordship and Bondage and the *Philosophy of History* Introduction, in selection; Week 9 of the [study plan](07-study-plan.md) adds the reception in named selections, never cover to cover. Hegel is quoted only from public-domain translations saved for this dossier, each in its own spelling and capitals: J. B. Baillie's *Phenomenology of Mind* (1910), William Wallace's *Logic of Hegel* (1892), S. W. Dyde's *Philosophy of Right* (1896) and J. Sibree's *Philosophy of History* (Bohn, 1857). Marx is quoted from the Moore and Aveling translation of *Capital* (1887) as transcribed by marxists.org, with some spelling modernized. Read Hegel in the modern editions named in [02-works-and-texts.md](02-works-and-texts.md). Citations follow the dossier: PhG ¶n in the Miller and Pinkard numbering, EL §n, PhR §n with "R" for a Remark and "A" for an Addition, PH by heading with Sibree's page. Dates and positions are checked against the SEP ("Hegel"; "Hegel's Dialectics"; "Hegel's Social and Political Philosophy"; "Schelling"; "Feuerbach"; "Critical Theory"; "Recognition"), the IEP ("Alexandre Kojève"), Gustav Mueller's note on the triad legend (1958), Walter Kaufmann's "The Hegel Myth and Its Method" (as reprinted in 1959), and Wikipedia articles where named; points resting on memory are marked "from memory" and listed in the corrections log.

## The school divides, 1831–1841

The SEP sums up the first decade: Hegel "had been a supporter of progressive but non-revolutionary politics, but his followers divided into factions broadly groupable as those of the left, right and centre (Toews 1985)" (SEP, "Hegel," §1). The quarrel began over religion. Hegel had taught that philosophy and Christianity have one content, grasped by philosophy in concepts and by religion in images (Enc. §§564–573; [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md)), which left open whether the Gospel history belongs to the content or only to the image.

David Friedrich Strauss answered in *The Life of Jesus Critically Examined* (1835–36): the Gospel narratives are myths produced by the early Christian community, and the truth they carry, the unity of the divine and the human, belongs to the human species, not to one man in Nazareth (SEP, "Feuerbach," §1, summarizing). The book confirmed conservative theologians such as Hengstenberg and Heinrich Leo in their suspicion that Hegel's philosophy was incompatible with the historical faith, and the Berlin Hegelians' journal publicly disowned Strauss (SEP, ibid.). The party labels came out of the dispute. Borrowing the seating of the French Chamber, Strauss sorted the Hegelians by their answer to the Gospel question: those who held the whole history compatible with Hegel's philosophy on the right, those who held part of it compatible in the center, those who held none of it compatible on the left (Wikipedia, "Young Hegelians"; that he did so in his *Streitschriften* of 1837 is from memory). Karl Ludwig Michelet, editor of the *History of Philosophy* lectures, took the scheme into his *Geschichte der letzten Systeme der Philosophie in Deutschland* (1837–38) and made it the standard map (from memory). Wikipedia notes that no one of the period called himself a "Right Hegelian", a term Strauss meant as an insult, and that recent historians question the left–right paradigm. Johann Philipp Gabler and H. F. W. Hinrichs appear in its list of the Right; Rosenkranz is usually placed in the center (so hegel.net's survey of the school, which puts Michelet and Gans in the "left center").

The Left, or Young Hegelians, turned critique from the Gospels to religion as such and then to the state. Arnold Ruge and Theodor Echtermeyer founded the *Hallische Jahrbücher* in 1838 as their organ (SEP, "Feuerbach"); Bruno Bauer led the Berlin circle *Die Freien* in its most radical phase, 1840–42 (Wikipedia); Ludwig Feuerbach began his public break with the Hegelian cause in "Toward a Critique of the Hegelian Philosophy" (1839) and argued in *The Essence of Christianity* (1841) that God is the projected essence of the human species (SEP, "Feuerbach"). Max Stirner's *The Ego and Its Own* (1844) turned the same weapons on Feuerbach (see the library's [Feuerbach and Stirner](../../eras/04-19th-century/sidebars-feuerbach-and-stirner.md) sidebar).

**Schelling in Berlin, 1841.** The government then called Schelling to Hegel's old chair, "reputedly because the government of the day had wanted to counter the influence that Hegelian philosophy had exerted on a generation of students" (SEP, "Hegel," §1). His first Berlin lectures drew Kierkegaard, Engels, Bakunin, Ranke, Burckhardt and Alexander von Humboldt, though he was soon largely ignored (SEP, "Schelling"). His argument was serious. Hegel's logic, on Schelling's account, is a "negative philosophy" that can say what anything must be if it exists but not that anything exists; a "positive philosophy" must begin from existence itself (SEP, "Schelling," §5). The SEP traces two consequences. Schelling's criticisms of Hegel's rationalism passed into existentialism, above all through Kierkegaard, who had gone to Berlin to hear him, and Schelling's own picture of Hegel helped fix "the orthodox or traditional understanding of Hegel as a metaphysical thinker in the pre-Kantian dogmatic sense" (SEP, "Hegel," §1), the picture the post-Kantian readers of the 1980s set out to dismantle (debate 1; [05-conversations.md](05-conversations.md) on Kierkegaard).

## Marx's inversion

Marx studied in Berlin from 1836 (the year from memory) and heard Eduard Gans ([01-life-and-times.md](01-life-and-times.md)). His *Critique of Hegel's Philosophy of Right* (written 1843) charged Hegel with turning real subjects, the people and civil society, into predicates of an abstract Idea and with presenting existing Prussian institutions as its embodiment, and the *Economic and Philosophical Manuscripts* (1844) took over the *Phenomenology*'s account of alienation and of labor as self-creation (summaries from memory; the SEP's "Critical Theory" traces alienation from the *Phenomenology* to the 1844 manuscripts). The slogan came in the Afterword to the second German edition of *Capital* (1873). Marx calls his dialectic "not only different from the Hegelian, but is its direct opposite": for Hegel the process of thinking, "under the name of 'the Idea,'" is "the demiurgos of the real world", while for Marx "the ideal is nothing else than the material world reflected by the human mind, and translated into forms of thought". Then he explains why he still calls himself Hegel's pupil:

> The mystification which dialectic suffers in Hegel's hands, by no means prevents him from being the first to present its general form of working in a comprehensive and conscious manner. With him it is standing on its head. It must be turned right side up again, if you would discover the rational kernel within the mystical shell.

— Marx, *Capital*, vol. I, Afterword to the Second German Edition (1873), trans. Samuel Moore and Edward Aveling (1887)

The image of inversion has governed the reception on both sides. Marxists read Hegel as a revolutionary method trapped in a conservative system (Engels's *Ludwig Feuerbach*, 1886, from memory). Hegel's defenders blame the Marxist tradition for two legends corrected below: Mueller (1958) traces the thesis–antithesis–synthesis formula through Marx's *Poverty of Philosophy* (1847), calling it "Marxism superimposed on Hegel", and the "Prussian" Hegel owes much to the 1843 *Critique*. Mueller writes as a polemicist against Marx; his evidence is better than his tone. Western Marxism later re-imported what the inversion left out: "many so-called Western Marxists re-incorporated further Hegelian elements back into their forms of Marxist philosophy", in reaction to Soviet orthodoxy (SEP, "Hegel," §1), from Georg Lukács's *History and Class Consciousness* (1923) and *The Young Hegel* (1948) to the Frankfurt School.

## Haym and the "Prussian state philosopher", 1848–1900

After 1848, the SEP says, "Hegelian idealism had seemed to collapse dramatically" in German academic philosophy (SEP, "Hegel," §1). Its obituary was Rudolf Haym's *Hegel und seine Zeit* (Berlin, 1857), lectures by a liberal who had sat in the Frankfurt National Assembly of 1848 (Wikipedia, "Rudolf Haym"). Its fifteenth lecture is titled "Preußen und die Rechtsphilosophie", and a running head announces that the Hegelian philosophy "constitutes itself as the philosophy of the Restoration" (*Die Hegel'sche Philosophie constituirt sich zur Restaurationsphilosophie*; Haym 1857, contents and p. 361, read in the archive.org scan). The core of the charge is that the Preface's double saying, "What is rational is real ; And what is real is rational" (PhR Preface; Dyde p. xxvii), was read "as saying that the absolutist Prussia that Hegel lived under was somehow rational and so justified (Haym 1857)" (SEP, "Hegel's Social and Political Philosophy," §1.1). "Prussian state philosopher" is the label later writers gave Haym's charge; this file did not find it in Haym's text. Rosenkranz replied with an *Apologie* ([01-life-and-times.md](01-life-and-times.md)).

Haym's Hegel was the one most of the next century knew. The charge had a liberal source, not a Marxist one, and it was aimed at the Berlin Hegel of 1820, the year after the Carlsbad Decrees, which is why the twentieth-century defenders had to answer Haym's history as well as Popper's polemic (debate 2). The German revival came from another direction: Wilhelm Dilthey's study of the young Hegel (1906) turned attention to his development and his early writings (SEP, "Hegel," §1, on Dilthey's historical work; the date and the early writings from Kaufmann 1959), and Benedetto Croce's *What Is Living and What Is Dead of the Philosophy of Hegel* (1907, from memory) began what Mueller calls the "Hegel Renaissance of the twentieth century" (Mueller, p. 414).

## British and American idealists, 1865–1920

As German Hegelianism faded, an English-speaking one began. James Hutchison Stirling's *The Secret of Hegel* (1865) is credited by Wikipedia's articles on Stirling and on British idealism with winning converts at a time when accurate translations were lacking. The translations this dossier quotes followed: Wallace's *Logic* (first edition 1874, from memory; second 1892), Dyde's *Philosophy of Right* (1896) and Baillie's *Phenomenology* (1910), after Sibree's *Philosophy of History* (1857). Their vocabulary, "Mind" for *Geist* and "real" for *wirklich*, is the vocabulary through which the British first read him ([02-works-and-texts.md](02-works-and-texts.md)).

The leading British idealists were T. H. Green (1836–1882), F. H. Bradley (1846–1924) and Bernard Bosanquet (1848–1923), followed by J. M. E. McTaggart, H. H. Joachim, R. G. Collingwood and, last, G. R. G. Mure (Wikipedia, "British idealism"). The SEP's wording is careful: Green and Bradley "developed metaphysical ideas which they related back to Hegel's thought" (SEP, "Hegel," §1). Few adopted Hegel wholesale, and Wikipedia observes that the *Logic* found almost no purchase in their work. What they took was an Absolute forming one coherent whole, internal relations, a coherence theory of truth, the concrete universal and, in politics, a social conception of the self turned against Spencer and the utilitarians. Green's state is justified only by what it contributes to the lives of individual persons (Wikipedia); it is not Haym's Prussia. McTaggart's commentaries on the *Logic* are among the sources the SEP's dialectics article cites for the textbook triadic reading. See the library's [Green and Bradley](../../eras/04-19th-century/british-idealists.md) entry.

In the United States the center was St. Louis, where Henry Conrad Brokmeyer and William Torrey Harris led a circle that published the *Journal of Speculative Philosophy* from 1867 to 1893, with early work by Josiah Royce (Wikipedia, "St. Louis Hegelians"). Royce's absolute idealism and the young John Dewey's Hegelianism carried the influence into American pragmatism (from memory), the tradition the post-Kantian readers would later draw on (SEP, "Hegel," §2.3).

## The analytic eclipse, 1900–1950

The idealists' students turned on them. For Bertrand Russell and G. E. Moore, Hegel was the chief target, and the SEP gives the logic of the dismissal: "For Russell, the revolutionary innovations in logic starting in the last decades of the nineteenth century with the work of Frege and Peano had destroyed Hegel's metaphysics by overturning the Aristotelian logic on which, so Russell claimed, it was based", so that Hegel "came to be seen within the analytic movement as an historical figure of little genuine philosophical interest" (SEP, "Hegel," §1). Russell's version, that Hegel's monism rests on the false assumption that every proposition has subject–predicate form, is discussed under Argument 4 in [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md) (Russell's wording from memory). The eclipse was never total: Hegel remained "a figure of interest within other philosophical movements such as existentialism and Marxism" (SEP, "Hegel," §1). But Schopenhauer's charge of charlatan obscurity, reported in the library entry, became the analytic textbook's verdict, and the textbooks repeated one another; once the triad legend was established, Mueller wrote, their authors "copied it from their predecessors" (Mueller, p. 414).

## Kojève's Paris, 1933–1939

The century's most influential reading of Hegel was given orally, to a small seminar. From 1933 to 1939 Alexandre Kojève, a Russian émigré who had written a doctorate under Jaspers on Vladimir Solovyov, lectured on the *Phenomenology* at the École pratique des hautes études in Paris (Wikipedia, "Alexandre Kojève"; IEP). Wikipedia names among his hearers Raymond Queneau, Georges Bataille, Maurice Merleau-Ponty, André Breton, Jacques Lacan, Raymond Aron and Éric Weil; whether Sartre attended is, the IEP says, "a matter of on-going contention". Queneau edited the lectures as *Introduction à la lecture de Hegel* (1947), and Allan Bloom's abridged English edition appeared in 1969 (SEP bibliography). The SEP credits the lectures with shaping "a generation of thinkers, including Jean Hyppolite, Jean-Paul Sartre and the psychoanalyst Jacques Lacan" (SEP, "Hegel," §1).

Kojève's Hegel is anthropological and atheist. On the IEP's account he reads Hegel through Marx, so that history is humanity's self-production through labor, and through Heidegger, so that man is the being aware of his death. Desire, above all the desire to be recognized, is "the engine of history". Lordship and Bondage becomes the key to the whole: humanity begins when one being risks its life for prestige and another submits; history is the slave's long labor of transforming nature and himself; and it ends in a universal state in which everyone recognizes everyone (IEP; [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Arguments 6–8). On Wikipedia's summary, Kojève held that "ideological history in a limited sense had ended with the French Revolution and the regime of Napoleon"; later history extends that principle to the rest of the world.

The French reception followed Kojève and then rebelled. Sartre's *Being and Nothingness* (1943) rests, in the IEP's words, on "a thoroughly Kojèveian philosophical anthropology"; Lacan's theory of desire replays the seminar; Simone de Beauvoir and Frantz Fanon turned the master–slave dialectic on gender and colonial race ([04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 8). Then "a later generation of French philosophers coming to prominence in the 1960s tended to react against Hegel" (SEP, "Hegel," §1), and the Hegel they rejected was largely Kojève's Hegel of totality and the end of history. In America Kojève's influence ran through Leo Strauss and his student Allan Bloom to Bloom's student Francis Fukuyama, whose *The End of History and the Last Man* (1992) made the thesis a claim about liberal democracy after the Cold War (IEP). Wikipedia notes that Kojève's own end of history "points as much to a socialist–capitalist synthesis as to a triumph of liberal capitalism". Debates 3 and 4 ask how much of this is Hegel's.

## Germany after 1900: Marcuse and the Frankfurt School

In Germany the SEP finds "important Hegelian elements" in "the approaches of thinkers of the Frankfurt School, such as Theodor Adorno, and later Jürgen Habermas", and in Gadamer's hermeneutics (SEP, "Hegel," §1). The SEP's article on critical theory is stronger: "Hegel's work has been a continual reference point for Frankfurt School philosophers", from Marcuse (1941) and Adorno (1963) to Benhabib and Honneth, who used his holism and his method of immanent critique "while eschewing his metaphysical, teleological, and reconciliatory tendencies" (SEP, "Critical Theory"). Herbert Marcuse's *Reason and Revolution: Hegel and the Rise of Social Theory* (1941, the year the SEP's "Critical Theory" gives; the title from memory), written in American exile, defended Hegel as a philosopher of reason and freedom whose heir was critical social theory, not the National Socialist state (summary from memory); it appeared four years before Popper. Adorno's *Three Studies on Hegel* (1963) and *Negative Dialectics* (1966) turned dialectic against Hegel's conclusion: where the Preface says "The truth is the whole" (PhG ¶20; Baillie p. 17), Adorno answered that the whole is the false (*Minima Moralia*, 1951; his wording from memory, so not quoted), and the SEP says he rejects "Hegel's affirmative synthesis".

## Popper's indictment and the liberal rehabilitation, 1940–1972

The wartime question was whether Hegel had prepared the total state. In Britain T. M. Knox's "Hegel and Prussianism" (*Philosophy*, January 1940) began an exchange with E. F. Carritt in the April and July issues (Kaufmann 1959). The definitive indictment came from Karl Popper's *The Open Society and Its Enemies* (London: Routledge, 1945), written in New Zealand during the war; its second volume, *The High Tide of Prophecy: Hegel, Marx, and the Aftermath*, has a chapter, "Hegel and the New Tribalism", presenting Hegel as the chief modern source of historicism and an apologist for Prussian power (Wikipedia, "The Open Society and Its Enemies"). Popper called Hegel "the father of modern historicism and totalitarianism", relying on remarks such as the state as "the march of God in the world", found "not in Hegel's own text, but in the published lecture note 'Additions' (or Zusätze) by his students" (SEP, "Hegel's Social and Political Philosophy," §1.1). Kaufmann lists further charges with Popper's page numbers: that Hegel's "one aim" was to serve Frederick William III, that he wanted "to stop rational argument", and that his dialectics were designed "to pervert the ideas of 1789" (Kaufmann 1959, quoting Popper).

The replies came from three directions. **Walter Kaufmann**'s "The Hegel Myth and Its Method" (*Philosophical Review*, 1951; reprinted 1959) attacked the scholarship: Popper ignored the work on Hegel's development from Dilthey to Franz Rosenzweig's *Hegel und der Staat*, relied largely on Scribner's *Hegel Selections*, a students' anthology, stitched "quilt quotations" together from different contexts with dots, and took over a mistranslation of the §258 Addition, whose German says only that it is the way of God with the world that there should be the state, in an Addition whose editor admitted the choice of words was sometimes his own (Kaufmann 1959, §§3–4, paraphrased). His *Hegel: A Reinterpretation* followed in 1965. **J. N. Findlay**'s *Hegel: A Re-examination* (1958), on the SEP's reading "apparently written as a riposte" to Popper, aligned Hegel with later Wittgenstein (SEP, "Hegel," §2.3). **Shlomo Avineri**'s *Hegel's Theory of the Modern State* (1972), with Knox, made the historical case. The SEP's own summary is that the detailed comments in the main body of the *Philosophy of Right* "consistently favour the aims of the Prussian Reform era which were opposed by the newly ascendant reactionaries after 1819", and, citing Avineri 1972 and Knox 1970, that Hegel defended "a new form of democratic institutions complete with jury trials that was more progressive" than Prussia had (SEP, "Hegel's Social and Political Philosophy," §1.1). Avineri singles out the §258 Addition as the sentence most responsible for the state-worshipping image and prints its German, *Es ist der Gang Gottes in der Welt, dass der Staat ist* (Avineri, ch. 9, as shown in the Cambridge Core abstract).

On the narrow question the verdict is now settled: "Today, most commentators agree that Popper and others were wrong to claim Hegel endorsed some view of authoritarianism". On the wider one it is not. Hegel "did also defend the traditional family and constitutional monarchy", and there is "a virtual consensus today that his work resists tidy classification as conservative, progressive or anything else" (SEP, "Hegel's Social and Political Philosophy," §1.1). Debate 2 sets out what remains open.

## Taylor, and the post-Kantian turn

Charles Taylor's *Hegel* (1975) made Hegel a whole philosopher English-speaking readers could study again. Taylor read the project as an attempt to unite the Enlightenment's radical autonomy with the expressivist ideal, from Herder, of a life that expresses who one is and binds one to nature and community, and he read spirit as a cosmic subject that posits the world as the condition of its own existence (summary from memory; [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 2). The SEP counts him, with Michael Rosen (1984) and Frederick Beiser (2005), as an English-language voice of the traditional metaphysical picture (SEP, "Hegel," §2.2). His verdict was double: the ontology of cosmic spirit is no longer credible, but Hegel's picture of the self as embodied, expressive and social is more alive than ever (from memory); his essay "The Politics of Recognition" (1992) took that picture into the debate over multiculturalism (SEP, "Recognition").

The post-Kantian reading began in Germany. Klaus Hartmann's "Hegel: A Non-Metaphysical View" (in Alasdair MacIntyre, ed., *Hegel: A Collection of Critical Essays*, 1972) read the *Logic* as a theory of categories, the concepts any account of what is must use, without a cosmic subject; with Dieter Henrich, Rolf-Peter Horstmann and others, the SEP says, it played an important role "in the revival of interest in Hegel in academic philosophy in the second half of the century" (SEP, "Hegel," §1 and bibliography). The North American version followed in Robert Pippin's *Hegel's Idealism: The Satisfactions of Self-Consciousness* (1989) and Terry Pinkard's *Hegel's Phenomenology: The Sociality of Reason* (1994). "In Pippin's influential account, Hegel had transformed philosophy by deindividualizing and historicizing Kant's idea of freedom as self-determination" (SEP, "Hegel," §2.3): Kant's unity of self-consciousness becomes a shared, historical practice of holding claims to account, and Pinkard's *Phenomenology* is the story of how a community's standards of authority fail by their own lights. Both drew on Wilfrid Sellars's socialized Kant, and "similar but more explicitly analytic readings of Hegel would emerge in the work of Robert Brandom (2009, 2019) and John McDowell (2006, 2018)" (ibid.); Brandom's *A Spirit of Trust* (2019) reads the *Phenomenology* as a theory of conceptual content and of the mutual recognition that institutes norms. The label has changed: "An earlier designation as the non-metaphysical view opposing the traditional metaphysical view came to be dropped for the unwanted suggestion that Hegel espoused no metaphysics" (SEP, "Hegel," §2.3), and Pippin now calls Hegel's logic a metaphysics "in a new sense" (SEP, §2.4). This file uses the SEP's name, "the post-Kantian view", and the older one, which most readers still meet.

## The metaphysical revival: Beiser, Houlgate, Stern

The post-Kantian reading drew two kinds of critic, and the SEP keeps them apart (SEP, "Hegel," §§2.2, 2.4). Defenders of the traditional picture, Beiser (*Hegel*, 2005) and Horstmann (2006), argued that the post-Kantians rehabilitate Hegel by subtraction; they were, in the SEP's words, portrayed "as projecting onto Hegel views a contemporary philosopher would like to find there rather than what is actually to be found". For Beiser, Hegel's idealism is an organic monism and the philosophy of history a theodicy ([04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 16). The revised metaphysical readers accept much of the post-Kantians' Kant but deny that Hegel gave up metaphysics. Stephen Houlgate (*The Opening of Hegel's Logic*, 2005; *Hegel on Being*, 2022) reads the *Logic* as an ontology in which presuppositionless thought discovers the structure of being; Robert Stern (*Hegel, Kant and the Structure of the Object*, 1990; *Hegelian Metaphysics*, 2009) defends a conceptual realism in which universals and kinds are features of the world; the SEP adds Kenneth Westphal, James Kreines, Karen Ng and Jacob McNulty (SEP, "Hegel," §2.4). Many of them share the post-Kantians' aim of freeing Hegel from "the extravagant views traditionally ascribed to him" (ibid.).

## Recognition theory: Honneth

Axel Honneth's *Kampf um Anerkennung* (1992; *The Struggle for Recognition*, trans. Joel Anderson, 1995) gave Kojève's theme a non-Kojèvean form. It went back past the *Phenomenology* to the Jena manuscripts, where Hegel, the SEP explains, "expands the Fichtean motif by referring to the Hobbesian idea of a fundamental struggle—albeit not of self-interest but of recognition" (SEP, "Recognition"). Honneth distinguished three spheres: love, through which persons gain self-confidence; legal respect, self-respect; and social esteem for their contributions, self-esteem (SEP, "Recognition," citing Honneth 1995, ch. 5). Social conflicts are at bottom struggles against the denial of recognition, and his *Freedom's Right* (2011) reconstructs Hegel's ethical life for present institutions (SEP, "Critical Theory"). The SEP's article on Hegel lists Honneth with Robert Williams (1992), Pippin (2008), and Heikki Ikäheimo and Arto Laitinen (2011) among the "non-traditional interpreters" who use recognition "in non-Kojèvean ways" (SEP, "Hegel," §3.1).

## Hegel, race and colonialism

The newest strand reads the philosophy of history against the world it left out. The Introduction's section on the geographical basis of history devotes several pages to Africa. "The Negro, as already observed, exhibits the natural man in his completely wild and untamed state", and "there is nothing harmonious with humanity to be found in this type of character" (PH Introduction, "Geographical Basis of History"; Sibree p. 97). Slavery is treated in the same pages:

> Slavery is in and for itself injustice, for the essence of humanity is Freedom ; but for this man must be matured. The gradual abolition of slavery is therefore wiser and more equitable than its sudden removal.

— PH Introduction, "Geographical Basis of History"; Sibree p. 103

The section ends: "At this point we leave Africa, not to mention it again. For it is no historical part of the World ; it has no movement or development to exhibit", and "What we properly understand by Africa, is the Unhistorical, Undeveloped Spirit, still involved in the conditions of mere nature" (Sibree p. 103). The text is a lecture course edited by Gans and Karl Hegel from manuscripts and students' notes ([02-works-and-texts.md](02-works-and-texts.md)), and these pages lean on missionaries' and travelers' reports, but no editor invented their view. The SEP calls the whole scheme "a clearly Eurocentric account of history" (SEP, "Hegel," §3.2.2).

The argument is over what follows. Robert Bernasconi ("Hegel at the Court of the Ashanti", 1998, from memory) showed how selectively Hegel used his sources and argued that the racial hierarchy is built into the philosophy of history (from memory). Susan Buck-Morss argued from the other side in the essay "Hegel and Haiti" (2000), expanded as *Hegel, Haiti, and Universal History* (2009): Hegel must have known of the slave revolution in Saint-Domingue (1791–1804) from the journals he read, and Lordship and Bondage registers, without naming it, the event that made freedom universal (Wikipedia, "Susan Buck-Morss"; SEP, "Critical Theory," bibliography). The SEP places her book within the postcolonial critique of the "idea of a universal history in which the central engine of progress is located in modern Europe" (SEP, "Critical Theory"). Her case is contested: Pinkard and others doubt that the text supports a specific historical reference ([04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 8), and a skeptic adds that the lecturer of the Africa pages drew no lesson from Haiti. Fanon's objection, that the colonial master wants work from the slave, not recognition, remains the sharpest challenge to reading the dialectic as a model of colonial liberation (ibid.). No serious reader now defends the Africa pages. The open question, debated by Bernasconi on one side and Alison Stone and Pinkard on the other (positions from memory; [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 16), is whether "the progress of the consciousness of Freedom" is a principle Hegel failed to apply or the self-portrait of the culture that wrote it.

## Live debates

The [arguments files](04-arguments.md) set out these debates with their texts; they are summarized here.

**(1) Metaphysical or post-Kantian?** What do "spirit", "the Absolute" and "the Idea" refer to? On the traditional reading of Taylor (1975), Rosen (1984) and Beiser (2005), a cosmic subject, a God who "becomes actual only via its particularization in the minds of 'his' finite material creatures" (SEP, "Hegel," §2.2). On the post-Kantian reading of Pippin (1989), Pinkard (1994), Brandom (2019) and McDowell, the self-correcting, shared and historical practice of holding ourselves to norms, with the *Logic* as an account of the conditions of any thought of an object (SEP, §2.3). On the revised metaphysical reading of Houlgate, Stern, Westphal and Kreines, Hegel accepts Kant's critique of dogmatic metaphysics but not the conclusion that thought cannot know things as they are, so the *Logic* is ontology (SEP, §2.4). Each side has texts. The metaphysical readers cite "Logic therefore coincides with Metaphysics, the science of things set and held in thoughts, — thoughts accredited able to express the essential reality of things" (EL §24; Wallace p. 45) and the Preface's demand to grasp the ultimate truth "not as Substance but as Subject as well" (PhG ¶17; Baillie p. 15; Argument 2). The post-Kantians cite Hegel's debt to Kant's unity of apperception and the *Phenomenology*'s path from consciousness to spirit as a social achievement (Arguments 3–8). The SEP calls the "unfolding dialectic" between them a "fertile space" (SEP, §2.4). The gap has narrowed, since Pippin allows a metaphysics "in a new sense" and Houlgate builds on Kant's critique, but it has not closed: the question is now what kind of metaphysics the *Logic* is.

**(2) Prussian apologist or liberal reformer?** The prosecution runs from Haym (1857) through Marx's 1843 *Critique* to Popper (1945): the double saying blesses what exists; the Preface attacks Fries and the student movement; the book appeared after the Carlsbad Decrees; it defends hereditary monarchy, entailed estates and the exclusion of women from public life; and Hegel enjoyed the protection of the minister Altenstein ([01-life-and-times.md](01-life-and-times.md); Argument 15). The defense, from Knox (1940) and Kaufmann (1951) through Avineri (1972) and Allen Wood (*Hegel's Ethical Thought*, 1990), answers that the book's institutions, an estates assembly with public debates, trial by jury, a professional civil service and protected spheres for civil society and conscience, belong to the Reform-era program the reaction of 1819 defeated (SEP, "Hegel's Social and Political Philosophy," §1.1); that "actual" is a term of assessment, so the double saying states a standard (EL §6 R; Argument 15); and that the most notorious sentence is an editor's Addition (misconception 2). Student transcripts of the lectures on right, edited by Karl-Heinz Ilting (1973–74) and Dieter Henrich (1983), show Hegel speaking more freely in the lecture hall than in print, and Ilting argued that the book was adapted to the censorship (from memory; [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md)). The positions now stand roughly thus: almost no one holds Popper's totalitarian Hegel; most hold that he was a moderate reformer within the constitutional monarchy of the 1820s; and the dispute is over whether his principle of freedom demands more than his institutions grant, for the rabble, for women and for the franchise (Argument 14). The SEP's verdict, that his work "resists tidy classification", is where the dossier stands.

**(3) Did Hegel think history had ended?** Kojève said yes, in principle, with Napoleon; Fukuyama put liberal democracy at the end. The text supports less. Hegel says that the Christian-Germanic nations "have attained the knowledge that All are Free" (PH, Sibree's contents to the Introduction, keyed to p. 19) and that "Europe is absolutely the end of History, Asia the beginning" (PH Introduction; Sibree p. 109), but the second sentence is about the course of the sun from East to West. He calls America the place "where, in the ages that lie before us, the burden of the World's History shall reveal itself" and refuses to say more, because "as regards History, our concern must be with that which has been and that which is" (PH Introduction; Sibree p. 90). Pippin and Pinkard read this as saying that the principle of freedom for all has been reached while its realization is unfinished; Kojève's heirs, Bloom and Fukuyama, read a completed history ([03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md), tension 2; Argument 16; positions from memory).

**(4) Is the master–slave reading Hegel's or Kojève's?** After Kojève, Lordship and Bondage was read as Hegel's philosophy of history in miniature, and Marxists took the slave's labor for the germ of the proletariat. Against this, Gadamer (1973), H. S. Harris (*Hegel's Ladder*, 1997, position from memory) and most recent commentators stress that the passage is a shape of self-consciousness in chapter IV, not of spirit; that its outcome is not a revolt but stoicism, scepticism and the unhappy consciousness (Argument 9); and that the full account of mutual recognition comes only with conscience and forgiveness at the end of chapter VI ([04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 7). Pippin and Brandom read the master as a model of authority without answerability; Honneth and Williams read the passage as one stage in a theory of recognition that Hegel worked out more fully at Jena. The dossier adopts the library entry's caution: Lordship and Bondage "is one passage about recognition and labor, not the whole system and not a literal theory of historical slavery". Still open are whether it has a historical referent (Buck-Morss against the skeptics) and whether the struggle is a necessary stage or a failed first attempt (Argument 7).

## Misconceptions, in full form

The [library entry](../../eras/04-19th-century/g-w-f-hegel.md)'s "Misreadings to avoid" gives short forms of the first and fourth, and [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md) treats the first under dialectic.

- **"Hegel's method is thesis, antithesis, synthesis."** The three words are Fichte's vocabulary, and Hegel never offers them as his method. Gustav Mueller's "The Hegel Legend of 'Thesis-Antithesis-Synthesis'" (*Journal of the History of Ideas*, 1958) gives the evidence: Glockner's *Hegel-Lexikon* "does not list the Fichtean terms 'thesis, antithesis, synthesis' together", the triad does not occur as Hegel's method in his collected works, and Rosenkranz's *Life* never mentions it (Mueller, pp. 411–412). Mueller traces the formula to Heinrich Moritz Chalybäus, whose lectures to Dresden Kantians in 1835–36, published in 1837, called Being, Nothing and Becoming "this first methodical thesis, antithesis, and synthesis" and "an example or schema for all that follows" (Mueller, pp. 413–414, quoting the 1843 edition), and through Marx, then a Berlin student, to *The Poverty of Philosophy*. What Hegel says is subtler than the legend. In the *Phenomenology* Preface he credits the form: "Now that the triplicity, adopted in the system of Kant — a method rediscovered, to begin with, by instinctive insight, but left lifeless and uncomprehended — has been raised to its significance as an absolute method, true form is thereby set up in its true content" (PhG ¶50; Baillie p. 46). What he attacks is its use as a pattern applied from outside: "For we see it there reduced to a lifeless schema, to nothing better than a mere shadow, and scientific organisation to a synoptic table" (PhG ¶50; Baillie p. 47). His own account of the logical moments names three sides of every thought, the understanding, the dialectical and the speculative (EL §79; Argument 11 and Week 6), not three positions in a debate. One qualification: the SEP's article on Hegel's dialectics grants that the Being–Nothing–Becoming opening fits the triadic description and that versions of the triadic reading "continue to have currency", while warning against applying that textbook example "too dogmatically" to the rest (SEP, "Hegel's Dialectics").

- **"Hegel said the state is God walking on earth."** The source is the Addition to PhR §258, which Dyde renders "The state is the march of God in the world ; its ground or cause is the power of reason realizing itself as will" (PhR §258 A; Dyde p. 247). Three things are wrong with the quotation-book version. It is not Hegel's printed text: the Additions were compiled after his death from students' lecture notes (SEP, "Hegel's Social and Political Philosophy," §1.1; [01-life-and-times.md](01-life-and-times.md)), and Kaufmann notes that the editor admitted the wording was sometimes his own. It is not what the German says: *Es ist der Gang Gottes in der Welt, dass der Staat ist* means, in Kaufmann's rendering, that it is the way of God with the world that there should be the state, a claim about the state's place in a rational order, not that the state is God. And "walking on earth" adds a picture found in neither the German nor Dyde (where it first appeared was not checked). The same Addition cuts against state-worship: "When thinking of the idea of the state, we must not have in our mind any particular state, or particular institution", and a state "may be declared to violate right principles and to be defective in various ways" (PhR §258 A; Dyde p. 247). Fairness requires the rest. The same Addition calls the idea of the state "this actual God", and the *Philosophy of History*, also an editors' compilation, says "The State is the Divine Idea as it exists on Earth" (PH Introduction; Sibree p. 41). Hegel's language about the state is religious; what that means depends on debate 1.

- **"Hegel said history is over."** He said that the principle that all human beings as such are free has been reached, and that philosophy comprehends the history that led to it. He did not say nothing more would happen: the same lectures call America "the land of the future" and decline to prophesy about it, since history's concern is "with that which has been and that which is" (PH Introduction; Sibree p. 90), and the *Philosophy of Right* says that philosophy "is its time apprehended in thoughts" and that "It is just as foolish to fancy that any philosophy can transcend its present world, as that an individual could leap out of his time" (PhR Preface; Dyde pp. xxviii–xxix). The end-of-history thesis is Kojève's construction, developed by Fukuyama (debate 3).

- **"Hegel worshipped the Prussian state."** Hegel was a Württemberger who came to Prussia in 1818, aged forty-eight ([01-life-and-times.md](01-life-and-times.md)); as Mueller puts it, "Hegel is not a Prussian, but a Suabian" (Mueller, p. 414). The "German World" at the end of the *Philosophy of History* is not Germany, still less Prussia: Hegel's "Germanische Welt", Mueller notes of the same term at the close of the *Philosophy of Right*, means the Germanic peoples who rebuilt Europe after the fall of the Roman empire, and Germany is not even mentioned (Mueller, p. 414; [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md)). The state of the *Philosophy of Right*, with estates, public debate and jury trials, was more liberal than the Prussia of 1820 and followed the Reform-era program (SEP, "Hegel's Social and Political Philosophy," §1.1). And his last political essay, on the English Reform Bill, was partly stopped by the Prussian censorship ([01-life-and-times.md](01-life-and-times.md), citing the IEP). What survives of the charge belongs to debate 2: Hegel defended a hereditary monarch, entailed estates and a narrow political class, under a government that was censoring his colleagues.

- **"The owl of Minerva is a prophecy."** The image is often quoted as if philosophy foresaw the coming night, or as Hegel's prediction that his era was ending. The passage denies philosophy foresight. It concerns the "desire to teach the world what it ought to be": "For such a purpose philosophy at least always comes too late. Philosophy, as the thought of the world, does not appear until reality has completed its formative process, and made itself ready", and then: "When philosophy paints its grey in grey, one form of life has become old, and by means of grey it cannot be rejuvenated, but only known. The owl of Minerva takes its flight only when the shades of night are gathering" (PhR Preface; Dyde p. xxx). Minerva's owl is wisdom, and wisdom about a form of life comes only when that form has matured, too late to prescribe to it. The image is a claim about the retrospective character of philosophical knowledge, and it is why Hegel will not say what comes next (misconception 3). Whether it also implies that Hegel's own world had "become old" is a further question readers still argue about; the text says only that a form of life must be mature to be known.

## Myths about the life

[01-life-and-times.md](01-life-and-times.md) ("Weighing the sources") sorts the stories by witness. The common myths:

- **"The world-spirit on horseback."** Hegel's letter of 13 October 1806 calls Napoleon "this world-soul" (*Weltseele*) and has him "astride a horse" (library entry; the letter as translated in English Wikipedia, "Hegel"; [01-life-and-times.md](01-life-and-times.md)). The compressed "world-spirit on horseback" (*Weltgeist zu Pferde*) is later shorthand, and German Wikipedia files the proverb under *Weltseele zu Pferde*; the letter does not say *Weltgeist*.
- **"Only one man ever understood me, and even he didn't."** The last words are Heine's report and are usually called apocryphal ([01-life-and-times.md](01-life-and-times.md)).
- **"Hegel died of cholera."** That was the official diagnosis in the epidemic of 1831, and Rosenkranz's *Life* (1844) reports it; some modern scholars think another gastrointestinal illness more likely (English and German Wikipedia, "Hegel"; [01-life-and-times.md](01-life-and-times.md), whose corrections log asks for the library entry's "probably of cholera" to be hedged).
- **The tree of liberty.** The story that Hegel, Hölderlin and Schelling planted one at Tübingen rests on late testimony; the library entry rightly says they "are said to have" done it ([01-life-and-times.md](01-life-and-times.md)).
- **"Hegel was Prussia's official philosopher."** He held the Berlin chair, enjoyed Altenstein's protection, was rector in 1829–30 and received the Order of the Red Eagle, third class, in 1831 ([01-life-and-times.md](01-life-and-times.md)). There was no such office, and ten years after his death the government called Schelling to Berlin to counter his school (SEP, "Hegel," §1).
