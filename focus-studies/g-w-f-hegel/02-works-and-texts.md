# Works and Texts: The Corpus in Reading Order
**English** | [简体中文](../../zh/focus-studies/g-w-f-hegel/02-works-and-texts.md)

*Focus study: G. W. F. Hegel · [Dossier home](README.md) · Library entry: [G.W.F. Hegel](../../eras/04-19th-century/g-w-f-hegel.md) · Arguments in full: [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md)*

Hegel published four books in his lifetime, and only one of them is a continuous treatise that he wrote for readers rather than for students. The SEP sorts the corpus into five kinds: the two stand-alone books, the *Phenomenology of Spirit* (1807) and the *Science of Logic* (1812–16); the handbooks written for his lecture courses, the *Encyclopaedia of the Philosophical Sciences* (1817, revised 1827 and 1830) and the *Elements of the Philosophy of Right* (1820); the lecture courses on history, art, religion and the history of philosophy, assembled after his death from his notes and his students' transcripts; the essays and reviews he published along the way; and the early manuscripts on religion and politics from the years before Jena. The library entry gives the first three kinds and the lectures. This file adds the early and Jena writings, and it explains what each text is as a physical object, because with Hegel that question decides how far a sentence can be trusted.

Two facts shape how the corpus is read. First, much of the most-quoted "Hegel" was not written by Hegel. The *Encyclopaedia* and the *Philosophy of Right* are compact numbered paragraphs that he meant to expand aloud in the lecture room, and his editors filled that gap after his death with *Zusätze* or "Additions" compiled from students' notes. The four great lecture series are editors' books built from manuscripts and transcripts of different years. The famous sentences about the state, about Africa, about the end of art, and many of the vivid illustrations of the dialectic come from these layers. The dossier's rule is simple: Hegel's own paragraphs and Remarks are evidence of what he held; Additions and lecture editions are evidence of what he said in class, as one student or editor heard it, and they are flagged as such whenever an argument leans on them. Second, the *Phenomenology* and the *Logic* are hard in different ways. The first is a narrative of shapes of consciousness, each refuted by its own experience, and it can be read in guided sections. The second is a continuous derivation of categories with almost no examples, and the [learning path](../../learning-path.md) is right that it should never be read solo. The dossier takes the *Logic* through the shorter *Encyclopaedia* version in Week 6 and leaves the *Science of Logic* itself for later.

Quotations in this file keep the spelling and capitals of the saved public-domain translations: J. B. Baillie's *Phenomenology of Mind* (1910), William Wallace's *Logic of Hegel* (2nd ed., 1892), S. W. Dyde's *Philosophy of Right* (1896) and J. Sibree's *Lectures on the Philosophy of History* (1857). Nothing inside quotation marks is modernized, and nothing from a modern translation is quoted.

## The forms Hegel wrote in

**The numbered handbook.** The *Encyclopaedia* and the *Philosophy of Right* are written in short numbered sections (§§), each a dense statement, often followed by a Remark (*Anmerkung*) in which Hegel adds polemic, history and examples. He explained the form in the Preface to the *Philosophy of Right*: "The immediate occasion for publishing these outlines is the need of placing in the hands of my hearers a guide to my professional lectures upon the Philosophy of Right" (PhR, Preface). The paragraphs presuppose the lecture. That is why the posthumous editors added the lecture material, and why a reader meets three layers on the page: Hegel's paragraph, Hegel's Remark, and an editor's Addition. Wallace prints the layers in different type without labels, and Dyde calls the Remarks "Notes"; see the citation section below.

**The continuous book.** The *Phenomenology* and the *Science of Logic* are continuous prose divided into chapters and headed sections, with no numbered paragraphs. The paragraph numbers used in English scholarship on the *Phenomenology* (¶1 to ¶808) were introduced by A. V. Miller's 1977 translation and kept by Terry Pinkard's (from memory; logged unchecked). Baillie prints none, which is why the dossier gives both the paragraph number and Baillie's page.

**The lecture course.** In Berlin Hegel lectured on a cycle of subjects, returning to each every second year or so and rewriting the course each time. Karl Hegel, who re-edited the history lectures in 1840, put the difficulty plainly: "every delivery was with him a new act of thought" (PH, Preface to the Second Edition; Sibree p. xxv). A printed lecture course is therefore a composite of five or six different courses, and the editors' choices about which year to follow are part of the text.

**The journal essay and the review.** In Jena Hegel wrote long essays for the *Critical Journal of Philosophy* that he edited with Schelling (SEP), and in Berlin he wrote reviews for the *Jahrbücher für wissenschaftliche Kritik* (from memory; logged unchecked). These are polemical and dated, and they are where the early Hegel defines himself against Kant, Fichte and Jacobi.

## The early writings (1793–1800)

Before Jena, Hegel was a house tutor in Bern (1793–96) and Frankfurt (1797–1800), and the SEP describes him in these years as working on religious and social themes, picturing himself as a reforming educator in the mold of Lessing and Schiller. He published almost nothing. The manuscripts survived and were first collected by Herman Nohl, a student of Wilhelm Dilthey, as *Hegels theologische Jugendschriften* (Tübingen, 1907); the University of Pennsylvania Press page for T. M. Knox's translation, *Early Theological Writings* (Chicago, 1948; the SEP gives the same date), calls Nohl's volume definitive. Knox translates the most important pieces but not all of Nohl: Penn's page names the omitted "National Religion and Christianity" fragments and the "Life of Jesus". What Knox does include are the two long essays on which the reputation of the young Hegel rests. "The Positivity of the Christian Religion" (Bern, c. 1795–96) asks how a teaching of moral autonomy, Jesus's, became a religion of authority and statute, and answers in a broadly Kantian voice. "The Spirit of Christianity and its Fate" (Frankfurt, c. 1798–1800) turns against that Kantian voice: it treats Kant's moral law as one more lordship over the self, sets love against law, and develops the idea of a "fate" that the criminal brings on himself by injuring a life he shares with his victim (dates and summary from memory; logged unchecked).

**Difficulty:** moderate; the prose is clearer than the mature works. **Place:** not set reading. These texts matter for two dossier arguments. Argument 12 (the empty formalism of Kant's moral law) has its first draft here, and the theme of a unity of life broken by opposition and restored through it, which the *Phenomenology* turns into method, is first worked out in the Frankfurt fragments. Dilthey's *Jugendgeschichte Hegels* (1905) and Nohl's edition gave the twentieth century a "young Hegel" of life and love against the old Hegel of the system, and that contrast runs through Lukács and the French reception (see [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md)).

## Jena: the *Difference* essay (1801) and *Faith and Knowledge* (1802)

In January 1801 Hegel joined Schelling at Jena, and that year he published his first philosophical book, *The Difference between Fichte's and Schelling's System of Philosophy* (SEP; the IEP dates the move to January 1801). The SEP summarizes its thesis: Schelling had succeeded where Fichte failed in systematizing and so completing Kant's transcendental idealism. Fichte's system, on this account, starts from the I and never reaches nature except as the I's limit; Schelling's "philosophy of identity" sets a philosophy of nature beside a philosophy of the self and grounds both in an Absolute that is neither. The essay's real interest for a reader of the later Hegel is its account of why philosophy is needed at all: a culture divided into oppositions it cannot reconcile, which reason must overcome rather than escape. The standard English is H. S. Harris and Walter Cerf, *The Difference Between Fichte's and Schelling's System of Philosophy* (SUNY, 1977; SEP).

*Faith and Knowledge* (*Glauben und Wissen*) appeared in the *Critical Journal* in 1802 (archive.org's catalogue record of the SUNY translation; a publisher's blurb places it in volume 2, part 1, Tübingen: Cotta). Its three main sections examine Kant, Jacobi and Fichte, and charge all three with the same failure: a "philosophy of reflection" that makes the finite understanding absolute and leaves God as an object of faith beyond knowledge. The essay ends with the image of a speculative Good Friday, the death of God felt as the deepest loss of the age and taken up as a moment of the Absolute (from memory; logged unchecked). Cerf and Harris translated it for SUNY in 1977 (SUNY's page and the archive.org record).

**Difficulty:** high; both essays assume the whole Kant–Fichte–Schelling debate. **Place:** background to Week 1. Read the [Fichte](../../eras/04-19th-century/johann-gottlieb-fichte.md) and [Schelling](../../eras/04-19th-century/friedrich-schelling.md) entries instead; the learning path makes Fichte the one predecessor Hegel cannot be read without. The Jena lecture manuscripts on logic, metaphysics, natural law and the philosophy of spirit (1802–06), published only in the twentieth century, belong to the specialist and are not set.

## The *Phenomenology of Spirit* (1807)

The *Phenomenology* was written at speed in Jena and printed while it was still being written. Baillie's introduction, drawing on Hegel's letters, gives the story: printing began in February 1806; the Bamberg publisher refused to pay until he was sure he had half the manuscript; Hegel's friend Niethammer guaranteed the contract on 29 September, on condition that the last of the manuscript arrive by 18 October; and Hegel sent the final instalments in the days around the battle of Jena (Baillie, Translator's Introduction, pp. xi–xii). The book appeared in 1807 as the first part of a projected "System of Science" (Baillie, p. xvi). On Baillie's account the first edition's title page also called it the "Science of the Experience of Consciousness", a title dropped in later editions (p. xv). The Preface was written last, after the body of the book (from memory; logged unchecked). Schelling, the SEP notes, read a barbed criticism of himself in it, and their friendship ended.

**What it is.** The *Phenomenology* is an introduction to philosophical science that works by taking ordinary consciousness at its word. Each "shape" of consciousness holds a standard of what knowledge is, tries to live up to it, and finds by its own experience that it cannot; the new shape that results knows what the old one learned. The sequence runs from Consciousness (sense-certainty, perception, force and the understanding; chs. I–III) through Self-Consciousness (desire, recognition, lordship and bondage, the unhappy consciousness; ch. IV) and Reason (ch. V) to Spirit, where the shapes become forms of a shared social world, from the Greek city through the Enlightenment and the Terror to conscience (ch. VI), and finally to Religion (ch. VII) and Absolute Knowing (ch. VIII). Arguments 3–10 and half of Argument 12 come from this book. How it is unified is disputed. Some readers take the turn at Spirit, where individual shapes give way to historical worlds, as a change of plan made under deadline. Others find one design throughout. Hegel himself, in a letter Baillie quotes, hoped that a second edition would let him "unload some of the ballast, and get the ship to float more easily" (Baillie, p. x). The composition debate and the metaphysical and non-metaphysical readings of what the book is for are set out in [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md).

**The Preface.** The Preface (¶¶1–72) is Hegel's manifesto for the whole system rather than an introduction to this book, and it begins by doubting that a preface can say anything true about philosophy: it seems "not only superfluous, but, in view of the nature of the subject, even inappropriate and inexpedient to begin, as writers usually do, with a preface" (PhG ¶1; Baillie p. 1). The objection is that a preface states results, and in philosophy a result without the path to it is false. The image that follows is the most famous in Hegel: "The bud disappears when the blossom breaks through, and we might say that the former is refuted by the latter" (PhG ¶2; Baillie p. 2), and yet bud, blossom and fruit are moments of one life. The Preface then attacks an Absolute in which all difference is lost, "the night in which, as we say, all cows are black" (PhG ¶16; Baillie p. 15), and states the program the rest of the system carries out: "everything depends on grasping and expressing the ultimate truth not as Substance but as Subject as well" (PhG ¶17; Baillie p. 15). Three paragraphs later comes the sentence that names Argument 1: "The truth is the whole" (PhG ¶20; Baillie p. 17). The Introduction (¶¶73–89), shorter and plainer, explains the method of letting consciousness test itself, and many teachers start there.

**Difficulty:** very high, and uneven; the Preface and chapter IV are dense but famous enough to have guides at every level, while chapters V and VII are long and allusive. **Place:** Weeks 1–5, in guided sections only: the Preface in selection, chapters I–IV nearly whole (¶¶90–230), and two blocks from chapter VI (¶¶444–476 sel., ¶¶582–595). This goes well past the learning path, which sets only the Preface in selection and ¶¶178–196. The dossier does so on purpose, because the lordship and bondage passage is the end of an argument that begins with sense-certainty, and read alone it turns into the parable the library entry warns against. But it is still not the whole book. Reason, the rest of Spirit, Religion and Absolute Knowing are read in summary from [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md), and the dossier does not pretend that nine weeks of selections amount to having read the *Phenomenology*.

**Editions.** Hegel's text of 1807 was reprinted in 1832 as volume II of the collected works, with a second edition in 1841 (from memory; logged unchecked); Baillie names the second edition of 1843 (*Werke* II) and Georg Lasson's carefully collated edition of 1907, which he used in revising his translation (Baillie, pp. xxxi–xxxii). The critical text is volume 9 of the *Gesammelte Werke* (1980; from memory, logged unchecked). Baillie published his translation in two volumes in 1910 and a revised one-volume edition in 1931; the revision is still in copyright in some countries and is the text of most online "Baillie" pages, so the dossier quotes only the 1910 scan (see the quote log).

## The *Science of Logic* (1812–16; Book I revised 1832)

Hegel wrote the larger *Logic* as headmaster of the Nuremberg gymnasium. Wallace's bibliographical notice gives the dates: the first volume, the Objective Logic, appeared in two parts in 1812 and 1813, and the second, the Subjective Logic, in 1816 (Wallace, p. xiv). The three books are the Doctrine of Being (1812), the Doctrine of Essence (1813) and the Doctrine of the Concept (1816). Wallace, writing in 1892, called it "the work which is the real foundation of the Hegelian philosophy" (Wallace, p. xiv), and few scholars since have disagreed about its place, whatever they think it achieves. In the last months of his life Hegel revised the Doctrine of Being for a second edition. It appeared in 1832, after his death, and the preface to it is dated a week before he died (from memory; logged unchecked). The other two books were never revised. Modern translations follow the same mixed text. George di Giovanni's Cambridge translation (2010), on its publisher's description, contains the revised Book I (1832) with Book II (1813) and Book III (1816).

**What it is.** The *Logic* is not a treatise on inference. It sets out the basic categories of thought, beginning from "being" with no further determination, and argues that each category, thought through, proves unstable and passes into a richer one, until the whole sequence is grasped as one self-determining "Idea". Because Hegel holds that these categories are also the forms of what is, the *Logic* "coincides with Metaphysics" (EL §24). Whether that makes it a metaphysics of reality or a theory of the conditions of thought is the center of the debate between metaphysical readers (Beiser, Houlgate, Stern) and non-metaphysical ones (Pippin, Pinkard, Brandom), and [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md) gives both sides. Argument 11 (being, nothing, becoming) is the *Logic*'s first move, which the dossier takes from the *Encyclopaedia* version.

**Dialectic, and the triad that is not Hegel's.** The *Logic* is where readers look for "the dialectic", and where the thesis–antithesis–synthesis formula most misleads them. The three words are Fichte's vocabulary, not Hegel's. Gustav Mueller's classic note, "The Hegel Legend of 'Thesis-Antithesis-Synthesis'" (*Journal of the History of Ideas*, 1958), argues that Hegel's texts show no such pattern and never use the three terms together as his method, and he traces the formula to Heinrich Moritz Chalybäus. Chalybäus's popular lectures to a circle of Kantians in Dresden, published in 1837 as a history of speculative philosophy from Kant to Hegel, presented being, nothing and becoming as a first thesis, antithesis and synthesis and as the schema for all that follows; Sibree's 1857 preface still recommends that book to English readers as a guide to the Hegelian method (Sibree, p. vi). The SEP's "Hegel's Dialectics" adds that the pattern does fit some transitions, being, nothing and becoming among them, but fits many more badly. Hegel himself, in the *Phenomenology* Preface, says that the "triplicity" adopted in Kant's system was a method "rediscovered, to begin with, by instinctive insight, but left lifeless and uncomprehended", and complains that the use made of it had reduced it to "a lifeless schema" (PhG ¶50; Baillie pp. 46–47). The dossier's treatment is in [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md) and Week 6.

**Difficulty:** the highest in the corpus; there are almost no examples, and the vocabulary is Hegel's own. **Place:** not set reading. The learning path's rule stands: never the *Science of Logic* solo. Week 6 reads the *Encyclopaedia Logic* instead, and a reader who wants the larger book afterward should take the Doctrine of Being with a commentary (Houlgate's *The Opening of Hegel's Logic* is the usual guide; see [08-bibliography.md](08-bibliography.md)). A. V. Miller's translation (1969) is the older English standard and the one most secondary literature cites (from memory; logged unchecked).

## The *Encyclopaedia of the Philosophical Sciences* (1817, 1827, 1830)

The *Encyclopaedia* is the whole system in outline, written as a handbook for Hegel's lectures. The first edition appeared at Heidelberg in 1817, a thin octavo of xvi and 288 pages, with the Logic taking pages 1–126, the Philosophy of Nature 127–204 and the Philosophy of Spirit 205–288 (Wallace, p. xv). Hegel revised it twice in Berlin, for the second edition of 1827 and the third of 1830, which is the text normally translated (Wallace, p. xv). Wallace's notice singles out what 1827 added to the Logic: a long section on the three "attitudes (or positions) of thought to objectivity", with a fuller criticism of the Critical philosophy and a discussion of Jacobi and the intuitionists (Wallace, p. xviii). That section is EL §§26–78, and it is the best short statement of where Hegel stands on Kant. The three parts are the Logic (the "Lesser Logic", §§1–244 in 1830), the Philosophy of Nature (§§245–376) and the Philosophy of Spirit (§§377–577), which runs from subjective spirit (soul, consciousness, mind) through objective spirit (right, morality, ethical life) to absolute spirit (art, revealed religion, philosophy). The SEP confirms the range of the Philosophy of Nature, §§245–376 of the 1830 edition.

Two passages make the *Encyclopaedia* indispensable even for a reader who never reads it through. The Introduction contains Hegel's own gloss on the slogan from the *Philosophy of Right* Preface: in ordinary speech every passing thing is called actual, but for philosophy "existence is in part mere appearance, and only in part actuality" (EL §6 R; Wallace p. 10). This is the passage the library entry cites when it says that "rational is actual" is not a blessing on whatever exists, and it is set for Week 7 with Argument 15. And the closing paragraphs on absolute spirit (§§553–577) are the only treatment of art, religion and philosophy that Hegel published in his maturity. The SEP's "Hegel's Aesthetics" notes that his published thoughts on art are just §§556–563 of the 1830 edition.

**The Zusätze.** After Hegel's death the editors of the collected works added to the *Encyclopaedia* long "Additions" (*Zusätze*) assembled from his lecture manuscripts and students' notes: for the Logic by Leopold von Henning, for the Philosophy of Nature by Karl Ludwig Michelet, for the Philosophy of Spirit by Ludwig Boumann (the Michelet attribution from the Hegel.net glossary; Henning and Boumann from memory, logged unchecked). The SEP notes that the Philosophy of Nature was first published as such in 1842, from the 1830 paragraphs supplemented by Berlin lecture transcripts. The Additions are lively and full of examples, and many of the best-known sentences in "Hegel's Logic" are Additions. They are also not Hegel's text: they come from lectures of different years, some given on earlier editions of the book, and the editor chose and joined them. Wallace translated the Logic with the Additions, and Geraets, Suchting and Harris's Hackett edition carries them in its subtitle ("with the Zusätze"). The dossier cites an Addition as "EL §n Z" and does not rest an argument on one alone.

**Difficulty:** high but navigable; the numbered paragraphs make it possible to read in slices, and the Remarks and Additions give the examples the *Science of Logic* withholds. **Place:** Week 6 (EL §§19–25, 79–82, 84–88) and Week 8 (§§553–577). The learning path does not set it; the dossier adds it as the manageable way into the *Logic*.

## The *Philosophy of Right* (1820) and its Additions

*Elements of the Philosophy of Right* (*Grundlinien der Philosophie des Rechts*) is Hegel's last book and his only full political philosophy. It came out in Berlin in late 1820 with a title page dated 1821, as the library entry says; the SEP gives 1820 in its survey of the works and 1821 in its biography, and both are right in this sense. Hegel's Preface says that he had been lecturing on the subject from the section of the 1817 *Encyclopaedia* that covers it, and that this book "covers the same ground in a more detailed and systematic way" (PhR, Preface). The SEP calls it an expansion of a section of the *Encyclopaedia*, and notes that transcripts of his earlier Heidelberg lectures on the subject have since been published (in English as *Lectures on Natural Right and Political Science*, 1995).

**What it is.** After an Introduction on the will and freedom (§§1–33), the book has three parts: Abstract Right (§§34–104: property, contract, wrong), Morality (§§105–141: purpose, intention, the good and conscience) and Ethical Life (§§142–360), which is divided into the family (§§158–181), civil society (§§182–256) and the state (§§257–360), ending with world history (§§341–360). The section numbers are Dyde's table of contents, which follows Hegel's. Dyde's English for the parts is of its time: "Ethical Observance" for *Sittlichkeit*, "Civic Community" for civil society, "the Police" for the public authority of §§231–249 (Dyde, Contents). Arguments 12–15 come from this book. Its Preface is the source of two of Hegel's most quoted sentences, both in Dyde's wording. One is "What is rational is real; And what is real is rational" (PhR, Preface). The other is the image of philosophy arriving late: "The owl of Minerva takes its flight only when the shades of night are gathering" (PhR, Preface). Note that Dyde renders *wirklich* as "real", where Wallace's Hegel, quoting the same sentence in the *Encyclopaedia*, says "What is reasonable is actual" (EL §6 R; Wallace p. 10). The dossier uses "actual" in its own prose, for the reason EL §6 gives.

**The Additions.** The book as Hegel published it has two layers: the numbered paragraphs, and his Remarks under many of them. A third layer came in 1833, when Eduard Gans re-edited the book for the collected works and added *Zusätze* drawn from students' notes of Hegel's lectures; Dyde notes that Gans dated his editor's preface 29 May 1833 (Dyde, Translator's Preface, p. ix). Dyde's judgment of the Additions is fair and still useful: they "usually cast a welcome light upon the text", yet they are "not even supervised by Hegel" (Dyde, Translator's Preface, p. xiii). They matter for the politics, because several of the sayings used to make Hegel a worshipper of the state are Additions rather than Hegel's paragraphs, the "march of God in the world" Addition to §258 above all (PhR §258 A; Dyde p. 247). Allen Wood's Cambridge edition marks the Additions as such and reports which student's notes each comes from (from memory; logged unchecked). The dossier cites them as "PhR §n A" and checks every political claim against the paragraph and Remark first.

**Difficulty:** moderate in the Introduction and Morality, high in the logic-driven transitions, and lower than any other Hegel text in civil society and the state, where the reader meets markets, courts, poverty and constitutions. **Place:** Week 7, with the Preface whole and selected paragraphs. The Week 8 *Encyclopaedia* reading (§§553–577) picks up where the *Philosophy of Right* stops, at the passage from world history to absolute spirit.

## The Berlin lecture courses

In Berlin Hegel lectured on logic and metaphysics, the philosophy of nature, the philosophy of spirit, right, world history, aesthetics, religion and the history of philosophy. After his death a society of his friends (the *Verein von Freunden des Verewigten*) published his works in eighteen volumes (1832–45), and the lecture courses were their largest addition: editors' books made from Hegel's manuscripts and from students' notebooks of several different years (the edition's name and dates from memory, logged unchecked; Karl Hegel's preface refers to "the association for publishing Hegel's works", Sibree p. xxvi). The SEP lists the courses published this way as nature, spirit, history, aesthetics, religion and the history of philosophy. The four that matter for this dossier have different editorial histories.

**The philosophy of history.** Hegel gave the course five times, every second winter from 1822–23 to 1830–31 (the preface to Nisbet's Cambridge *Introduction*; Gans's preface also counts five deliveries). Eduard Gans edited it first, in 1837, mainly from the latest course, and says what the job was: "The Editor had to present Lectures in the form of a Book" (PH, Preface to the First Edition; Sibree p. xxi). For part of the Introduction he had a draft that Hegel began writing out in 1830 (same). In 1840 Hegel's son Karl re-edited it, using Hegel's own manuscripts as the base "and using the notes only for the purpose of rectification and arrangement" (PH, Preface to the Second Edition; Sibree p. xxvi), because the early courses, in his view, were the more vigorous. Sibree's 1857 English prints both prefaces and so follows Karl Hegel's text. Twentieth-century editors went further. Johannes Hoffmeister's edition of the Introduction separates Hegel's own manuscript drafts from the transcribed material (Nisbet's translation prints the 1830 draft as its own chapter, "Second Draft (1830)"; the first draft's date, 1822, is from memory, logged unchecked), and H. B. Nisbet's *Lectures on the Philosophy of World History: Introduction* (Cambridge) translates Hoffmeister's edition and keeps his brackets for every word Hoffmeister supplied (Cambridge Core, translator's preface). The SEP lists the Brown and Hodgson translation of the manuscripts and the 1822–23 lectures (Oxford, 2011) for the newer critical text. Argument 16's thesis, that world history is "the progress of the consciousness of Freedom" (PH Introduction; Sibree pp. 19–20), stands in every version. The long surveys of China, India and Africa are the parts where the editions differ most, and the dossier treats their claims as Hegel's lectures as reported, not as his book. **Place:** Week 8, the Introduction only.

**Aesthetics.** The SEP's "Hegel's Aesthetics" (Houlgate) gives the history. Hegel lectured on art in Heidelberg in 1818 and in Berlin in 1820–21, 1823, 1826 and 1828–29. His student H. G. Hotho published the lectures in 1835 (revised 1842), from a manuscript of Hegel's that is now lost and a series of transcripts, and this is the text of T. M. Knox's *Aesthetics: Lectures on Fine Art* (Oxford, 1975). Annemarie Gethmann-Siefert argues that Hotho gave the lectures a stricter system than Hegel did and added material of his own, so that readers should rely on the transcripts instead; Houlgate replies that with the manuscript lost the charge cannot be settled, and that her own reading has been questioned. Transcripts of the Heidelberg course, made by Hegel's assistant F. W. Carové, were found in the cathedral library at Freising in 2022 and are being edited under Klaus Vieweg (SEP). **Place:** Week 8 in summary; "the end of art" thesis is discussed in [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md).

**The philosophy of religion.** Hegel lectured on religion four times in Berlin (1821, 1824, 1827, 1831), and Philipp Marheineke's edition of 1832, revised in 1840 with Bruno Bauer, merged the courses (dates from memory; logged unchecked). Peter C. Hodgson's edition, translated in three volumes (Oxford, 2007–08, per the SEP), presents each course separately, which shows Hegel changing the order of the "determinate religions" from year to year. **Place:** Week 8 in summary, with EL §§564–571 on revealed religion.

**The history of philosophy.** Karl Ludwig Michelet edited the lectures as three volumes of the collected works, and E. S. Haldane and F. H. Simson's translation (1892–96) follows his second edition of 1840–44 (SEP). Hegel lectured on the subject from Jena onward, and the SEP lists a translation of the 1825–26 course alone (Oxford, 2006–09). The Haldane and Simson text is public domain and is the one most quoted in older English writing on Hegel and the Greeks. **Place:** reference only; [05-conversations.md](05-conversations.md) uses it for Hegel on Kant, Fichte and Spinoza.

## The collected editions

Three German editions matter. The "friends' edition" of 1832–45 is the one the nineteenth century read, with the Additions and the edited lectures; Wallace cites it as the *Werke*, and Baillie names its *Phenomenology* volume, the second edition of 1843 (Baillie, p. xxxii). The Suhrkamp *Werke in zwanzig Bänden*, edited by Eva Moldenhauer and Karl Markus Michel (1971), reprints the friends' edition in revised form and is still the most common German reading text (SEP; "revised form" from memory, logged unchecked). The critical edition is the *Gesammelte Werke* (Meiner, 1968–). Meiner's page gives its plan. The German Research Foundation commissioned it in 1957, the Hegel-Archiv at the Ruhr-Universität Bochum edited it, and since the Academy's support ended in 2016 it has continued on project funding. Its first division, in 22 volumes, contains everything Hegel printed and every surviving manuscript in his hand. Its second division, volumes 23–30, edits the lectures from the students' transcripts themselves, course by course, and two supplementary volumes catalogue Hegel's library. Between the two stands Meiner's *Vorlesungen: Ausgewählte Nachschriften und Manuskripte* (1983–), the series from which the Oxford translations of single lecture courses were made (SEP).

For the student this has one practical consequence. Any quotation from a lecture course, or from an Addition, should be traced to the paragraph or manuscript it glosses before it is used as evidence. The critical edition now makes that possible; the books most people still read do not.

## A note on citation and editions

***Phenomenology*** is cited by paragraph in the Miller and Pinkard numbering, with Baillie's page when the dossier quotes him: (PhG ¶178; Baillie p. 175). Miller and Pinkard number the same paragraphs, so a ¶ reference finds the place in either. Baillie prints no numbers; the dossier's writers matched his paragraphs to the standard numbers using a numbered English text for orientation only, and where a paragraph could not be pinned they cite the section instead (PhG IV.A). Baillie's chapter headings are his own and differ from Miller's ("Mind" for Spirit, "Lordship and Bondage" for the section Pinkard calls "Mastery and Servitude", from memory; logged unchecked), so trust the number, not the heading.

**The *Encyclopaedia*** is cited by section: EL §n for the Logic, Enc. §n for the other two parts, "R" for Hegel's Remark and "Z" for an editor's Addition (EL §6 R, EL §24 Z). Wallace keeps Hegel's section numbers but does not label the layers: the Remark follows the paragraph, and the Additions follow in smaller type, numbered (1), (2) where there are several. Check a layer against Geraets, Suchting and Harris, which labels them, before citing.

**The *Philosophy of Right*** is cited by section, with "R" for Hegel's Remark and "A" for Gans's Addition (PhR §258 R, PhR §258 A). Dyde keeps Hegel's numbers and prints the Remark as "Note" and the Addition as "Addition"; the Preface is cited as PhR, Preface.

**The philosophy of history** is cited by Sibree's part and section heading, with his page where readable (PH Introduction; Sibree p. 19). Sibree's text is Karl Hegel's 1840 edition, so its Introduction is not the Introduction of Hoffmeister and Nisbet, and a passage found in one may be missing or reordered in the other.

**Other works** are cited by title and section or page of the translation named in [08-bibliography.md](08-bibliography.md). The *Science of Logic* is cited by book and section heading, with di Giovanni's page (SL, Doctrine of Being, "Becoming"; di Giovanni p. 59–60), since the dossier quotes no translation of it.

**Spelling and wording.** Quotations keep the translator's spelling and capitals, which is why Spirit is "Mind" in Baillie and Ethical Life is "Ethical Observance" in Dyde. The older translations also differ from the modern ones in key terms. Baillie and Dyde often have "real" where Miller, Pinkard and Nisbet have "actual" (*wirklich*), and Wallace has "notion" for what modern translators call the "concept" (*Begriff*). [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md) gives the German and both renderings for each term.

**Reading editions (the dossier's rulings).** The learning path names Miller and Pinkard and says a guided-excerpts volume matters more than the translation; the dossier rules as follows.

- ***Phenomenology*:** Terry Pinkard (trans.), *The Phenomenology of Spirit* (Cambridge Hegel Translations, 2018; with Michael Baur), the modern scholarly standard, in clear present-day English with a bilingual text in the hardback (the bilingual layout from memory; logged unchecked). Or A. V. Miller (trans.), *Phenomenology of Spirit* (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1977; paperback 1979), with J. N. Findlay's paragraph-by-paragraph analysis, which is the reason to own it. Most secondary literature before 2018 quotes Miller. Either serves; Miller plus Findlay is the better solo companion, Pinkard the better English.
- ***Encyclopaedia Logic*:** T. F. Geraets, W. A. Suchting and H. S. Harris (trans.), *The Encyclopaedia Logic: Part I of the Encyclopaedia of Philosophical Sciences with the Zusätze* (Indianapolis: Hackett, 1991). It translates the prefaces to all three editions, carries the Additions, and labels them as Additions.
- ***Philosophy of Right*:** Allen W. Wood (ed.), H. B. Nisbet (trans.), *Elements of the Philosophy of Right* (Cambridge Texts in the History of Political Thought, 1991). Wood's notes are the best short guide to the politics and to which passages are Additions.
- **Philosophy of history:** H. B. Nisbet (trans.), *Lectures on the Philosophy of World History: Introduction: Reason in History*, from Hoffmeister's edition, with an introduction by Duncan Forbes (Cambridge; 1975, from memory, logged unchecked). For the body of the lectures, Sibree remains the only complete English of the old edition.
- **Aesthetics:** T. M. Knox (trans.), *Aesthetics: Lectures on Fine Art*, 2 vols. (Oxford, 1975), read with the SEP's warning about Hotho.
- **Not recommended solo:** any *Science of Logic*; the full *Phenomenology* cover to cover; anthologies that print famous sentences without saying whether they come from a paragraph, a Remark or an Addition.

**Quoted editions.** Baillie (1910), Wallace (1892), Dyde (1896) and Sibree (1857) are quoted because they are public domain, not because they are the best translations. Baillie is free and often loose, Wallace is accurate and Victorian, Dyde is stiff, and Sibree is readable but translates a text that later editors largely replaced. Where an argument turns on a word, the dossier checks it against the modern reading edition and says so.

## The works in the dossier's reading order

The [learning path](../../learning-path.md) gives Hegel in Stage 4, row 2, as "excerpts only": the *Phenomenology* Preface in selection and ¶¶178–196, with the *Philosophy of History* Introduction in selection, and "never the *Science of Logic*, solo". The dossier keeps that core and widens it to nine weeks, about 55 hours all-in, in guided sections and never cover to cover; [07-study-plan.md](07-study-plan.md) gives the split.

1. **Week 1, life, Kant and Fichte, the Preface:** PhG Preface in selection (¶¶1–37 sel.); the Fichte and Schelling entries for the *Difference* essay's problem. Arguments 1–2.
2. **Week 2, Consciousness:** PhG ¶¶90–165 (sense-certainty, perception, force and the understanding). Arguments 3–5.
3. **Week 3, Self-consciousness:** PhG ¶¶166–196 (desire, recognition, lordship and bondage). Arguments 6–8.
4. **Week 4, the freedom of self-consciousness:** PhG ¶¶197–230 (stoicism, scepticism, the unhappy consciousness). Argument 9.
5. **Week 5, Spirit:** PhG ¶¶444–476 sel. (ethical life and *Antigone*); ¶¶582–595 (absolute freedom and the Terror); ¶¶419–437 for Argument 12. Argument 10.
6. **Week 6, logic in outline:** EL §§19–25, 79–82, 84–88; what "dialectic" does and does not mean. Argument 11.
7. **Week 7, *Philosophy of Right*:** Preface; §§1–33 sel., 41–51, 105–141 sel., 142–157, 182–256 sel., 257–260; with EL §6 R. Arguments 12–15.
8. **Week 8, history, art and religion:** PH Introduction; Enc. §§553–577 (absolute spirit); the aesthetics and religion lectures in summary. Argument 16.
9. **Week 9, reception; capstone:** the Left and Right Hegelians, Marx and Kierkegaard in the selections named in [07-study-plan.md](07-study-plan.md); capstone essay.
