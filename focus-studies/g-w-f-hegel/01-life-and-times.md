# Life and Times: Stuttgart, Tübingen, Jena, Nuremberg, Heidelberg, Berlin


Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel lived for sixty-one years, from 1770 to 1831, and the shape of his life follows the shape of the age that his philosophy tried to understand. He was a schoolboy in the Duchy of Württemberg under the old Holy Roman Empire, a theology student when the Bastille fell, a lecturer at Jena when Napoleon's army took the city, a newspaper editor and then a headmaster in the new kingdom of Bavaria that Napoleon had made, a professor at Heidelberg in the year after Waterloo, and for his last thirteen years the most famous philosopher in Prussia, in the decade of the Carlsbad Decrees and the restoration of the old order. He saw the Revolution, the Terror, the Empire and its fall, and he died the year after the July Revolution of 1830 in Paris, in the same year he wrote against the English Reform Bill. Week 5 of this dossier reads his account of "absolute freedom and the Terror" and Week 8 his claim that world history is the progress of the consciousness of freedom; both read differently once you know that their author lived through what they describe.

Hegel wrote no autobiography. The record is of three kinds. First, the letters: his son Karl edited a first selection, *Briefe von und an Hegel* (Leipzig, 1887), and the modern edition by Johannes Hoffmeister and Friedhelm Nicolin (four volumes, 1952–1981) is the standard; the English selection by Clark Butler and Christiane Seiler, *Hegel: The Letters* (1984), is in copyright and is not quoted here. Second, the testimony of students and friends, gathered first by Karl Rosenkranz (1805–1879), whose *Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel's Leben* appeared in Berlin in 1844 as a supplement to the friends' edition of the works. Third, the records of the institutions he passed through. The standard modern life in English is Terry Pinkard's *Hegel: A Biography* (Cambridge University Press, 2000). Where a date or detail is checked here, the source is named; what rests on general knowledge of the literature is flagged in the logs.

Hegel is quoted in the public-domain translations the dossier uses throughout: J. B. Baillie's *Phenomenology of Mind* (1910), S. W. Dyde's *Philosophy of Right* (1896), and J. Sibree's *Philosophy of History* (Bohn, 1857), whose prefaces by Hegel's editors are themselves part of the story. Two phrases from the German letter of 13 October 1806 are quoted in the original, from Karl Hegel's 1887 edition.

## Stuttgart, 1770–1788

Hegel was born in Stuttgart, the capital of the Duchy of Württemberg, on 27 August 1770; his family called him Wilhelm. His father, Georg Ludwig Hegel (1733–1799), was a secretary in the duke's revenue office, from a family of officials and pastors; the IEP calls him "an official in the government of the Duke of Württemberg". His mother, Maria Magdalena Louisa Fromm (1741–1783), taught him the first Latin declension before he started school, and died of a fever when he was thirteen.

From the mid-1770s (the IEP gives 1777–1788; other accounts give 1776 as his entry) he attended the Stuttgart Gymnasium illustre. He was a model pupil and a great excerpter, copying long passages from the classics and the writers of the German Enlightenment, Lessing, Klopstock and the "popular philosophers" such as Christian Garve, into a diary and notebooks. Rosenkranz, who had these papers, summed up the schooling as belonging wholly to the Enlightenment in principle and wholly to classical antiquity in curriculum (Rosenkranz, *Hegel's Leben*, 1844, p. 10, paraphrased from the German).

## The Tübingen Stift, 1788–1793

In October 1788, at eighteen, Hegel entered the Tübinger Stift, the Protestant seminary attached to the University of Tübingen, where the duchy trained its pastors at state expense. The SEP gives the years 1788–1793 and says he studied "first philosophy and then theology"; the IEP adds that he took the master's degree in 1790. He seems to have gone there because it was free, not because he meant to be a pastor; he never took a parish.

His room-mates and friends were the two people who mattered most to his early thought: Friedrich Hölderlin (1770–1843), who became one of the great German poets, and Friedrich Wilhelm Joseph Schelling (1775–1854), five years younger, who was publishing philosophy before he was twenty. The SEP says that these friendships "clearly had a major influence on Hegel's philosophical development", and that "for a while the intellectual lives of the three were closely intertwined". Hölderlin drew Hegel toward Schiller and the Greeks, whose civic religion looked to all three like a living alternative to the theology of their teachers, who were using Kant's moral philosophy to prop up orthodoxy. Hegel was less a technical philosopher than his friends; he read Rousseau, and he imagined himself, in the SEP's description, as a reforming educator in the line of Lessing and Schiller.

The French Revolution broke out in their second year. In 1792 a political club in the Stift read the French newspapers, and Hegel and Hölderlin were known as its enthusiasts. The famous story is that the three friends planted a "tree of liberty" outside Tübingen; the evidence for it is late and thin, and the library entry rightly reports it as something they "are said to have" done. What is better attested is that Hegel never gave up the principles of 1789. The Terror sobered him, but he is reported to have raised a glass every year on 14 July to the storming of the Bastille, and his Berlin lectures on the philosophy of history still call the Revolution "a glorious mental dawn". Week 5's reading of "absolute freedom and the Terror" is the mature form of the young man's hope and disappointment. He passed the final theological examination in 1793 and left Tübingen.

## House tutor in Bern and Frankfurt, 1793–1800

For seven years Hegel earned his living as a *Hofmeister*, a private tutor in a wealthy household. From 1793 to 1796 he taught the children of Captain Karl Friedrich von Steiger, of one of the patrician families that governed the republic of Bern. At the Steigers' summer estate at Tschugg he had a library, and read Montesquieu, Grotius, Hobbes, Hume, Locke, Machiavelli, Rousseau, Spinoza, Thucydides and Voltaire; and in Bern he had a close view of an oligarchy. In January 1797 Hölderlin, now in Frankfurt, found him a post with the family of Johann Noe Gogel, a wine merchant (the IEP: "with the help of his friend Hölderlin"), and the Frankfurt years brought the two friends back into daily conversation. When his father died in January 1799, Hegel received a small inheritance, enough to think again of a university career.

The writings of these years were not published in his lifetime; they reached print only in the twentieth century, as the "early theological writings" (T. M. Knox's English selection, *Early Theological Writings*, appeared in 1948, as the SEP's bibliography records). They are as much about politics and history as about theology. In Bern Hegel wrote a *Life of Jesus* that presents Jesus as a teacher of Kantian moral autonomy, and an essay on the "positivity" of the Christian religion, on how a religion of freedom turned into one of external authority and ceremony. In Frankfurt, under Hölderlin's influence, the tone changed: *The Spirit of Christianity and its Fate* (c. 1798–1800) sets love, which unites what Kant's moral law divides, against law and duty. The SEP sums up: until around 1800 Hegel "devoted himself to developing his ideas on religious and social themes", and seemed to see his future as "a type of modernising and reforming educator". The IEP notes the "radical tendency" of his critique of orthodox religion.

Two other texts show where he was heading. One is a single sheet in Hegel's hand, now called "The Oldest System-Program of German Idealism" (c. 1796–97), which calls for a *Mythologie der Vernunft*, a mythology of reason, and announces that the state, as a machine, must cease; whether Hegel wrote it or copied it from Schelling or Hölderlin is still disputed. The other is an unfinished essay on the constitution of the German Empire (1799–1802), whose theme, in the IEP's words, is that "the Empire is a thing of the past", and that Germany "was no longer a state governed by law"; it ends on the uncertain hope that some Machiavellian genius might save Germany as a whole. The empire was dissolved in 1806, while Hegel was finishing the *Phenomenology*.

Around 1800, the SEP says, "under the influence of Hölderlin and Schelling, his interests turned more to issues arising from the critical philosophy" of Kant and Fichte. In November 1800 he wrote to Schelling, now a professor at Jena, that the ideal of his youth had had to turn into the form of reflection and into a system, and asked for help in finding a place there.

## Jena, 1801–1807

Jena, a small university town in the duchy of Saxe-Weimar, was in the 1790s the center of German philosophy. The SEP explains why: K. L. Reinhold had taught Kant's philosophy there, and then Fichte, "who taught there from 1794 until his dismissal on the grounds of atheism at the end of the decade". The Romantics were there or nearby, and Goethe, as minister in Weimar, oversaw the university. Schelling had come in 1798 and, at twenty-three, was the most famous young philosopher in Germany.

Hegel arrived in January 1801. To lecture he had to habilitate, and he did so with a Latin dissertation on the orbits of the planets (*De orbitis planetarum*, 1801), which criticized Newton's celestial mechanics from the side of Kepler and Schelling's philosophy of nature. (A passage in it on the arithmetical series used to argue that there must be a planet between Mars and Jupiter was later used to mock him; the SEP reports that critics portrayed him as "trying to deduce the number of planets in the solar system in an a priori way", and that defenders such as Posch (2011) and Ferrini (2012) answer that this rests on distorted readings of the text.) He became a *Privatdozent*, an unsalaried lecturer paid by student fees.

The same year he published his first philosophical book, *The Difference between Fichte's and Schelling's System of Philosophy* (1801), usually called the *Difference* essay. In the SEP's summary, he "argued that Schelling had succeeded where Fichte had failed in the project of systematizing and thereby completing Kant's transcendental idealism". Fichte's idealism, on Hegel's reading, was only subjective: it began from the self and could never get to nature except as something the self posits against itself. Schelling, by setting a philosophy of nature beside the philosophy of the self, made the identity of subject and object objective as well. The essay also states a diagnosis that Hegel would repeat all his life: philosophy is needed because life has fallen into division, *Entzweiung*, and its task is to heal the split.

In 1802 and 1803, the SEP continues, "Hegel and Schelling worked closely together, editing and contributing to the *Critical Journal of Philosophy*". Hegel's contributions include *Faith and Knowledge* (1802), a critique of Kant, Jacobi and Fichte as philosophers of reflection, and the essay on natural law (1802–03), which the IEP describes as a critique of both the empirical and the formal, Kantian approaches. The collaboration had a cost: "on the basis of this association Hegel came to be dogged for many years by the reputation of being a 'mere' follower of Schelling (who was five years his junior)". Schelling left Jena for Würzburg in 1803.

Alone in Jena, Hegel lectured and wrote drafts of a system that he did not publish. These manuscripts (the *Realphilosophie*, first edited by Johannes Hoffmeister in the twentieth century) contain early versions of the struggle for recognition and of the analysis of labor and its division that the IEP singles out. In February 1805, after he wrote to Goethe to complain that his rival Jakob Friedrich Fries had been promoted ahead of him, he was made an extraordinary professor, still almost without salary (the IEP: "having attained a professorship in 1805"). Fries will appear again in the Berlin years.

Hegel's private life in Jena was precarious. His landlady, Christiana Charlotte Burkhardt, née Fischer, whose husband had left her, became pregnant by him, and on 5 February 1807 she gave birth to his son, Georg Ludwig Friedrich Fischer, known as Ludwig. Hegel had promised her marriage and withdrew the promise when he left Jena; he learned of the birth in Bamberg. The boy was brought up first by Johanna Frommann, the sister of the Jena publisher Carl Friedrich Ernst Frommann (German Wikipedia's account, from the biographical literature).

## The *Phenomenology* and the battle, 1806

The book that made Hegel's name was written in a hurry. It was meant as the first part of a "System of Science", an introduction leading ordinary consciousness up to the standpoint of philosophy, and it grew in the writing into something much larger. Baillie's Translator's Introduction (1910), drawing on the 1887 *Briefe*, tells the story of the contract, which explains the shape of the book. Hegel had agreed to deliver the manuscript to his Bamberg publisher in installments, to be paid by the sheet once half had arrived. Printing began in February 1806. When Hegel claimed that half was in, the publisher refused to pay and cut the edition from a thousand copies to seven hundred and fifty. Hegel's friend Friedrich Immanuel Niethammer, a theologian now in Bavarian service, stepped in, and on 29 September 1806 signed a new contract by which he would himself pay the publisher if Hegel failed to send the rest of the manuscript by 18 October (Baillie, pp. xi–xii).

Meanwhile the war came to Jena. Prussia had gone to war with Napoleon, and on 13 October 1806 French troops occupied the town. That evening Hegel wrote to Niethammer, and the letter, which Karl Hegel printed in 1887 (*Briefe* I, pp. 67–68, dated "Jena. Montag den 13 Octbr. 1806"), contains the most quoted sentence of his correspondence. He had seen the emperor, *diese Weltseele* (this world-soul), riding out through the town to reconnoiter, and he wrote that it was a wonderful feeling to see such an individual, concentrated at one point, *auf einem Pferde sitzend* (sitting on a horse), reaching out over the world and mastering it. The phrase later became "the world-spirit on horseback", but Hegel wrote *Weltseele*, not *Weltgeist*, and the same letter is full of practical worries: officers quartered in friends' houses, the post, the manuscript. The next day, 14 October, Napoleon routed the Prussian army outside the town. French soldiers were quartered in Hegel's house, and the SEP sums up the result: "The occupation of Jena by Napoleon's troops as Hegel was completing the manuscript restricted the activities of the university and Hegel departed."

Hegel later told Schelling that he had finished the book at midnight before the battle. Baillie, who quotes the claim, is skeptical of the drama in it:

> In a letter to Schelling, in which Hegel promises to send him a copy of the book, Hegel asks indulgence for the unsatisfactory character of the last parts of the work, and says, as if by way of explanation, that the "composition of the book was concluded at midnight before the battle of Jena." This sounds a little hollow and melodramatic.

— Baillie, Translator's Introduction, *The Phenomenology of Mind* (1910), vol. I, p. xi

Baillie's own explanation of the hurried last chapters is the contract: the deadline, not the cannon, compressed "Religion" and "Absolute Knowledge" into a few pages. The manuscript of the final part was finished on the night of the 13th and sent to Bamberg on the 20th, once things had quieted down (Baillie, p. xi). The book appeared in the spring of 1807 as *System der Wissenschaft. Erster Theil, die Phänomenologie des Geistes*.

Its Preface, written last, when the rest was in print, reads in this setting as a report on the times as well as a program for philosophy:

> For the rest it is not difficult to see that our epoch is a birth-time, and a period of transition. The spirit of the age has broken with the world as it has hitherto existed, and with the old ways of thinking, and is in the mind to let them all sink into the depths of the past and to set about its own transformation.

— PhG ¶11; Baillie, vol. I, p. 10

The same Preface ended a friendship. Attacking a formalism that answers every question by saying that in the Absolute all is one, Hegel wrote that to give out "its Absolute as the night in which, as we say, all cows are black — that is the very naivete of vacuous knowledge" (PhG ¶16; Baillie, vol. I, p. 15; Baillie's spelling, without the diaeresis). Hegel told Schelling that the criticism was aimed at his imitators, not at him. The SEP reports the result: "Schelling ... interpreted a barbed criticism in the *Phenomenology*'s preface as aimed at him, and their friendship abruptly ended." The correspondence soon stopped.

## Bamberg and the newspaper, 1807–1808

With no salary and an illegitimate son to support, Hegel took the job Niethammer found for him: editor of the *Bamberger Zeitung*, a daily newspaper in the Bavarian town of Bamberg, from March 1807 (the IEP gives 1807; the SEP says he did the work "apparently very successfully"). Bavaria was now Napoleon's ally, and the paper was pro-French. He also learned what it was to work under censorship: in the autumn of 1808 the Bavarian authorities investigated the paper for printing news of troop movements, and he begged Niethammer for a different post (the episode is reported in English Wikipedia's summary of Pinkard).

## Nuremberg: the Gymnasium and the *Science of Logic*, 1808–1816

Niethammer was now a senior official in Munich, and in November 1808 he had Hegel appointed rector of the Aegidien-Gymnasium in Nuremberg and professor of the preparatory sciences there. The SEP dates the Nuremberg years "from 1808 to 1815"; Hegel in fact left for Heidelberg in the autumn of 1816, and the IEP, the library entry and other accounts give 1808–1816. For eight years he ran a school and taught in it. His philosophy courses for the senior classes, dictated in numbered paragraphs and followed by questions and discussion, were later edited from his notes and the pupils' notebooks by Rosenkranz as the *Philosophische Propädeutik* (vol. 18 of the collected *Werke*, 1840). The numbered paragraph with spoken commentary became the method of the *Encyclopaedia* and the *Philosophy of Right*.

The poet Clemens Brentano, passing through, described the rector as a wooden, honest man reading the medieval German *Heldenbuch* and the *Nibelungenlied* and translating them into Greek as he read so that he could enjoy them (German Wikipedia quotes Brentano's letter).

In Nuremberg he wrote and published his largest book, the *Science of Logic*: the first volume, the Objective Logic, came out in two parts, the Doctrine of Being in 1812 and the Doctrine of Essence in 1813, and the second volume, the Subjective Logic or Doctrine of the Concept, in 1816. The SEP says simply that during the Nuremberg years "he married and started a family, and wrote and published his *Science of Logic*". The *Logic* is the book in which Hegel set out to derive the basic categories of thought from one another, beginning from the emptiest of all, pure being, which proves to be no different from nothing, so that the truth of both is becoming. Week 6 of this dossier reads that opening, in the shorter version of the *Encyclopaedia Logic* (§§86–88), and the learning path's warning stands: this is not a book to read cover to cover alone.

## Marriage and family

On 16 September 1811 Hegel, then forty-one, married Marie Helena Susanna von Tucher (1791–1855), twenty years old, the eldest daughter of a Nuremberg patrician family; her parents had hesitated because his position seemed insecure, and Niethammer wrote on his behalf (the dates are those given in German and English Wikipedia from the biographical literature). Their first child, a daughter, died shortly after birth. Two sons followed: Karl (1813–1901), who became a distinguished historian and edited his father's letters in 1887 and the second edition of the *Philosophy of History* in 1840, and Immanuel (1814–1891), who followed a theological career and, with his brother, kept their father's papers.

Ludwig Fischer, the son born in Jena in 1807, had been raised by others. In 1817, aged ten, he joined the Hegel household (English Wikipedia: in April 1817 at Heidelberg, after his mother's death; German Wikipedia: brought to Nuremberg by his mother, who now demanded a settlement). The arrangement went badly. Ludwig was shy and felt himself treated as a lesser son; he was sent to Stuttgart as a commercial apprentice, quarreled with his father, and was made to take his mother's name, Fischer. In 1825 he enlisted for six years in the Dutch colonial army and went to the East Indies, and he died of a fever in Batavia in the summer of 1831, a few months before his father; the news never reached Hegel. Readers of the *Philosophy of Right* on the family (PhR §§158–181) may keep in mind that Hegel's own household included a son he did not fully acknowledge.

His sister Christiane, who had long suffered from mental illness, drowned herself early in 1832, a few months after his death (English Wikipedia's account).

## Heidelberg and the *Encyclopaedia*, 1816–1818

In 1816, with Napoleon defeated and the *Logic* complete, Hegel had offers from Erlangen, Berlin and Heidelberg, and chose Heidelberg, where he took the chair of philosophy that had been vacant, German Wikipedia notes, since Spinoza declined it in 1673. In his inaugural lecture on 28 October 1816 he welcomed the first steps toward German unity in the founding of the German Confederation, in the hope that pure science might flourish again now that the war was over (German Wikipedia's summary). The IEP dates the appointment "One year following the defeat of Napoleon at Waterloo (1815)".

At Heidelberg he published, in 1817, the *Encyclopaedia of the Philosophical Sciences in Outline*, a textbook for his lectures: the whole system in numbered paragraphs, a Logic followed by a Philosophy of Nature and a Philosophy of Spirit. The SEP describes it as the work "in which an abbreviated version of the earlier *Science of Logic* (the *Encyclopaedia Logic* or *Lesser Logic*) was followed by the application of its principles to the philosophy of nature and the philosophy of spirit". It was the book he went on rewriting: the second edition appeared in 1827 and the third in 1830. The library entry calls it "the most manageable overview he published", and Week 6 of this dossier reads its Logic rather than the *Science of Logic* for that reason. At Heidelberg Hegel also lectured for the first time on aesthetics and gave a course on natural right and political science whose student transcript, published in the twentieth century, is the first version of the *Philosophy of Right* (SEP: the later book was "based on lectures given at Heidelberg"). In 2022 the biographer Klaus Vieweg found more than four thousand pages of Heidelberg lecture notes taken by Friedrich Wilhelm Carové, including a long-sought transcript of the aesthetics lectures (as Wikipedia reports).

Heidelberg also brought Hegel back to the politics of his home state. In a long review of the proceedings of the Württemberg Estates for the *Heidelberger Jahrbücher* (1817), he took the king's side in the dispute over a new constitution, against Estates who defended their old privileges; it was his first public stand on a living political quarrel.

## Berlin, 1818–1831

On 26 December 1817 Karl vom Stein zum Altenstein, the first Prussian minister of culture, offered Hegel the chair of philosophy at the University of Berlin, which had been vacant since Fichte's death in 1814. Hegel accepted, and gave his inaugural lecture on 22 October 1818. The SEP calls the Berlin chair "the most prestigious position in the German philosophical world".

**The Prussian context and the Carlsbad Decrees.** Hegel arrived as the reform era was ending. Student fraternities, the *Burschenschaften*, carried the hopes of the war of liberation for a united and constitutional Germany, and Hegel's old rival Fries, now at Jena, was one of their heroes and spoke at their Wartburg festival in 1817. On 23 March 1819 a student and fraternity member, Karl Ludwig Sand, murdered the conservative writer August von Kotzebue. Metternich used the murder, and a second attempted assassination that summer, to call a conference at Carlsbad, and on 20 September 1819 the Federal Diet of the German Confederation passed the Carlsbad Decrees. They "banned nationalist fraternities ('Burschenschaften'), removed liberal university professors, and expanded the censorship of the press", and placed a government agent at every university to watch "the spirit which is shown by the instructors in the university in their public lectures" (the decree text as given in the English Wikipedia article). In Prussia the "demagogue persecutions" followed: Fries lost his chair, the Berlin theologian W. M. L. de Wette was dismissed, and English Wikipedia, summarizing Pinkard, reports that Hegel and pupils such as Leopold von Henning and Carové were harassed and put under surveillance by the interior minister, Prince Sayn-Wittgenstein, and his circle at court.

Hegel's conduct in these years is part of the long argument about his politics. He is reported to have intervened for arrested students and to have helped de Wette privately, while defending in public the state's right to dismiss him; and the Preface he wrote for the *Philosophy of Right* in June 1820 contains a personal attack on Fries:

> Mr. Fries, one of the leaders of this shallow-minded host of philosophers, on a public festive occasion, now become celebrated, has not hesitated to give utterance to the following notion of the state and constitution:

— *Philosophy of Right*, Preface, tr. Dyde (1896), p. xxi

The "public festive occasion" is the Wartburg festival. To attack Fries in print after he had lost his chair under the decrees looked to many readers like siding with the police, and the Preface's other famous sentences were read in the same light: the double sentence that the library entry glosses, in Dyde's wording,

> What is rational is real ;
> And what is real is rational.

— *Philosophy of Right*, Preface, tr. Dyde (1896), p. xxvii (Dyde's line break and spacing)

and the image near its close:

> The owl of Minerva takes its flight only when the shades of night are gathering.

— *Philosophy of Right*, Preface, tr. Dyde (1896), p. xxx; the Preface is signed "Berlin, June 25th, 1820."

The book itself, the *Grundlinien der Philosophie des Rechts*, appeared in the autumn of 1820 with the date 1821 on its title page (the library entry's "published late 1820; title page dated 1821"; the SEP gives 1821). Whether it is a defense of Prussia as it was in 1820, or of a constitutional, representative monarchy that Prussia was not, is a debate this dossier takes up in Week 7 and in the [reception file](06-reception-and-debates.md). His defenders argue, as the library entry puts it, that he defended a constitutional monarchy with representative elements, more liberal than the Prussia of 1820; his critics from Rudolf Haym (1857) onward read the Preface and the attack on Fries as the voice of the Restoration. The IEP notes that "Some have considered Hegel to be a nationalistic apologist for the Prussian State of the early 19th century, but his significance has been much broader".

**The lectures.** In Berlin Hegel published little more than the revised *Encyclopaedia* (1827, 1830) and reviews, many for the *Jahrbücher für wissenschaftliche Kritik*, the review journal his circle founded in 1827. His work was lecturing, normally ten hours a week (German Wikipedia), on logic, nature, spirit, natural right, world history (five times, from 1822–23 to 1830–31, as Gans's preface records), aesthetics, religion and the history of philosophy. He was by all accounts a poor speaker, and students came anyway, from all over Germany and beyond. The SEP says that in the ten years before his death "Hegel enjoyed celebrity at Berlin". He was rector of the university in 1829–30 (English Wikipedia gives October 1829 to September 1830; the IEP says he "was given the honor of being elected Rector" in 1830). In his vacations he traveled to the Netherlands, Vienna and Paris, and saw Goethe in Weimar for the last time.

**The school.** By the mid-1820s there was a recognizable Hegelian school of students, colleagues and officials: the jurist Eduard Gans, whose lectures the young Karl Marx would attend; the theologian Philipp Marheineke; Karl Ludwig Michelet; Heinrich Gustav Hotho; Leopold von Henning; Johannes Schulze of Altenstein's ministry; and Rosenkranz. A translator's footnote in Sibree gives G. H. Lewes's list of its members (Gans, Rosenkranz, Michelet, Werder, Marheineke and Hotho; Sibree, p. xxiii). Schopenhauer, then a young *Privatdozent* in Berlin, is the best-known opponent of these years.

**The last year.** In 1830 revolution broke out again in Paris, and in 1831 the British Parliament debated the Reform Bill. Hegel was disturbed by the unrest of 1830, and his last political essay, on the English Reform Bill, was written in 1831 for the government newspaper, the *Preussische Staatszeitung*. The IEP notes that it "was interrupted due to censure by the Prussian King because of the perception of its being overly critical and anti-English", and that the remainder was printed separately and circulated privately. In 1831 the king gave him the Order of the Red Eagle, third class.

## Death, November 1831

Cholera reached Berlin in August 1831. Hegel left the city and took lodgings in Kreuzberg, then outside it, and returned when the new semester began in October, in the mistaken belief that the epidemic had largely subsided (English Wikipedia's account, from Pinkard). He died on 14 November 1831, aged sixty-one. The IEP states that he "died of cholera in Berlin". He was buried on 16 November, as he had wished, in the Dorotheenstadt cemetery next to Fichte and the aesthetician Karl Solger.

The cause of death is less certain than the reference books suggest. The physicians certified cholera, and Rosenkranz states it as fact; but the letter of Hegel's widow that he prints says she had seen no symptom of cholera in Hegel, and that the doctors had recognized it as the form that destroys the inner life without outward symptoms (Rosenkranz, *Hegel's Leben*, 1844, pp. 422–423, paraphrased from the German). English Wikipedia, citing Pinkard's biography, says that "it is likely he died from another gastrointestinal disease", and German Wikipedia quotes the judgment that he probably died "an einem chronischen Magenleiden und nicht an Cholera, wie die offizielle Diagnose lautete", of a chronic stomach ailment and not of cholera, as the official diagnosis had it. The library entry's "probably of cholera" should therefore be hedged: officially of cholera, and possibly of something else (see the corrections log). The famous last words that Heinrich Heine reported, that only one man had understood him and even he had not, are often described as apocryphal.

## The *Werke* of the Friends, 1832–1845

Soon after the funeral, Hegel's students and friends formed an association to publish his works, and it is from their edition, not from Hegel's own hand, that most of the "Hegel texts" of the nineteenth century come. Its title page names it: *Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel's Werke. Vollständige Ausgabe durch einen Verein von Freunden des Verewigten* (complete edition by an association of friends of the deceased), published by Duncker und Humblot in Berlin from 1832 to 1845 in eighteen volumes, with Rosenkranz's *Life* as a supplement in 1844 and the letters, edited by Karl Hegel, added as volume 19 in 1887. Scholars call it the *Freundesvereinsausgabe*. Its editors, as German Wikisource lists them, were the school: Michelet, Johann Schulze (the *Phenomenology*), Leopold von Henning (the *Logic* and the *Encyclopaedia Logic*), Ludwig Boumann, Eduard Gans (the *Philosophy of Right* and the *Philosophy of History*), Hotho (the *Aesthetics*), Marheineke (the philosophy of religion), Friedrich Förster, and Rosenkranz.

Two features of this edition matter for any reader today. First, the books Hegel published himself, the *Encyclopaedia* and the *Philosophy of Right*, were reprinted with "Additions" (*Zusätze*) compiled by the editors from Hegel's lecture notes and students' transcripts and inserted after his paragraphs. Dyde's translator's preface (1896) states the problem fairly: these are "Additions made by students of Hegel from his oral lectures and comments", and "as they are mere additions, not even supervised by Hegel", they must be read with care. This dossier marks them "§n A" (PhR) and "§n Z" (EL). Second, the lecture courses on history, art, religion and the history of philosophy were not books at all until the editors made them so. Gans, who edited the *Philosophy of History* in 1837, says so plainly in his preface:

> The Editor had to present Lectures in the form of a Book : he was obliged to turn oral discourse into readable matter : the notes of students and the manuscripts which constituted his materials were of different dates

— Gans, Preface to the First Edition (1837), in Sibree, *The Philosophy of History*, p. xxi

Three years later Hegel's son Karl re-edited the same lectures for a second edition (1840), because, as he explains in his own preface, the courses of different years did not agree, and "every delivery was with him a new act of thought" (Karl Hegel, Preface to the Second Edition, in Sibree, p. xxv). The association named three of its members to supervise him. The modern critical *Gesammelte Werke* (Meiner, 1968–) and the transcript series *Vorlesungen: Ausgewählte Nachschriften und Manuskripte* (1983–), both in the SEP's bibliography, separate what Hegel wrote from what his students heard in a given year. Week 8 reads the *Philosophy of History* Introduction in Sibree, which translates the second edition, and the [works file](02-works-and-texts.md) explains which modern editions to use alongside it.

## After Hegel: the school divides

The school did not survive its founder as a single body. The SEP summarizes: Hegel "had been a supporter of progressive but non-revolutionary politics, but his followers divided into factions broadly groupable as those of the left, right and centre (Toews 1985)". The split came first over religion, with David Friedrich Strauss's *Life of Jesus* (1835), and then over politics; the Left or Young Hegelians, Feuerbach and then Marx among them, turned the dialectic against religion and the Prussian state. After Hegel's death the government called Schelling to Berlin, "reputedly because the government of the day had wanted to counter the influence that Hegelian philosophy had exerted on a generation of students" (SEP), and Kierkegaard was among his hearers. The [conversations file](05-conversations.md) and the [reception file](06-reception-and-debates.md) follow these lines forward.

## Weighing the sources

**Rosenkranz (1844).** Karl Rosenkranz's *Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel's Leben* (Berlin: Duncker und Humblot, 1844), published as a supplement to the friends' edition, is the first biography and still a primary source, because Rosenkranz had the family's papers, including the school diary and early manuscripts, and quotes from them. It is also a school document, written by a disciple to present the master as the friends wanted him remembered. Its counterpart is Rudolf Haym's *Hegel und seine Zeit* (Berlin, 1857), a liberal's attack written after 1848, which made the Berlin Hegel the philosopher of the Prussian Restoration; Rosenkranz answered Haym with an *Apologie*. Kuno Fischer's *Hegels Leben, Werke und Lehre* (1901) and Wilhelm Dilthey's study of the young Hegel turned attention to the early manuscripts.

**Pinkard (2000).** Terry Pinkard's *Hegel: A Biography* (Cambridge University Press, 2000) is the standard life in English, built from the letters and archives, and much of the modern record used here (Ludwig Fischer, the Berlin politics, the cause of death) reaches reference works through it. Pinkard is also one of the leading non-metaphysical interpreters of Hegel (the SEP groups him with Pippin), and readers may find that the biography presents Hegel above all as a philosopher of modern social freedom rather than of cosmic Spirit. Readers should know that the debate over how to read Hegel, metaphysical (Frederick Beiser, Stephen Houlgate, Robert Stern) against non-metaphysical or post-Kantian (Robert Pippin, Pinkard, Robert Brandom), is a live one, set out in the SEP and in the [reception file](06-reception-and-debates.md); a biography written from one side will stress the evidence that suits it. Klaus Vieweg's German biography, *Hegel: Der Philosoph der Freiheit* (2019), is the main recent alternative.

**What is solid.** The dates of the appointments, the publications and the marriage; the Jena letter of 13 October 1806, printed in 1887; the contract story of the *Phenomenology*, which Baillie took from the letters; the Preface to the *Philosophy of Right* and its date; the Carlsbad Decrees; the date and place of death; the friends' edition and its editors' own prefaces.

**What rests on report.** The tree of liberty; the yearly toast to the Bastille; most of the stories of Hegel as a lecturer; Heine's version of the last words; the details of his conduct toward arrested students and toward de Wette, which come from letters and later testimony and are told differently by his defenders and his critics.

**What is disputed.** The authorship of the "Oldest System-Program"; how far the *Philosophy of Right* accommodates the Prussia of 1820; whether Hegel died of cholera; and how far any of the posthumous lecture volumes, assembled from notes of different years, can be read as his considered view.

**What the library entry says, and where this file differs.** This file agrees with [the library entry](../../eras/04-19th-century/g-w-f-hegel.md) on the outline: Stuttgart, the Stift with Hölderlin and Schelling, the tutor years, Jena, the *Phenomenology* finished as Napoleon took the city, the "world-soul" letter, Nuremberg, Heidelberg, Berlin, and the posthumous lectures. It adds the Bamberg year and Ludwig Fischer, notes that the Jena post was an unsalaried lectureship, and hedges one point: the entry's "probably of cholera" should say that cholera was the official diagnosis and that Pinkard doubts it (see the corrections log).
