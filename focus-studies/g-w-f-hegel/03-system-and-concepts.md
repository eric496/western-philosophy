# Hegel: The System and Its Concepts

*Focus study · [Dossier home](README.md) · Library entry: [G. W. F. Hegel](../../eras/04-19th-century/g-w-f-hegel.md) · Arguments in full: [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md)*

## The shape of the problem

Hegel inherited a question from Kant and refused Kant's answer to it. Kant had argued that the categories through which we think objects (substance, cause, unity and the rest) are forms of our understanding, valid for things as they appear to us and silent about things as they are in themselves. Fichte and Schelling tried to close that gap from the side of the self and from the side of nature. Hegel's bet is that the gap was never real: that thought, followed far enough and honestly enough, turns out to be the structure of what is, and that the mind which thinks it is not a spectator outside the world but the world's own way of coming to know itself. The library entry puts the result in one line: reality, history and mind form one self-developing whole, which comes to understand itself through conflict and reconciliation.

Two sentences from the Preface to the *Phenomenology of Spirit* (1807) state the program. "The truth is the whole" (PhG ¶20; Baillie p. 17). And "everything depends on grasping and expressing the ultimate truth not as Substance but as Subject as well" (PhG ¶17; Baillie p. 15). The first says that no single proposition, perception or category is true on its own; truth belongs to the completed development in which each partial view finds its place. The second says that this whole is not a thing lying there to be described, like Spinoza's one substance, but an activity, something that develops, differentiates itself and comes back to itself in knowing itself. Arguments 1 and 2 in [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md) reconstruct both claims.

This file sets out the concepts that carry that program, in the order the dossier reads them: first the architecture of the finished system (Logic, Nature, Spirit), then the *Phenomenology* that leads up to it, then what Hegel means by negation and dialectic, then the Logic's beginning, nature in outline, the *Philosophy of Right*, world history, and art, religion and philosophy. Each concept is defined in Hegel's own words before any criticism, and his words are technical: "actual", "notion", "spirit", "immediate", "abstract" and "negation" all have senses that ordinary usage gets wrong. The arguments that use these concepts are numbered 1–16 in [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md); the scholarly fights are in [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md).

A warning about scope belongs at the start. The learning path takes Hegel as "excerpts only" (Stage 4, row 2): the *Phenomenology* Preface in selection, Lordship and Bondage (¶¶178–196), and the *Philosophy of History* Introduction in selection, with the rule "Not the full *Phenomenology*, and never the *Science of Logic*, solo", because this is the most common point at which Stage 4 readers give up. This dossier goes further on purpose, through the rest of the *Phenomenology*'s self-consciousness chapter, two blocks of the Spirit chapter, the *Encyclopaedia Logic* in outline and most of the *Philosophy of Right*. It does so in guided sections, never cover to cover, and this file says where it is going beyond the path. The *Science of Logic* itself stays closed: the Logic is read in its shorter *Encyclopaedia* form, and only in the paragraphs named.

## The system and its parts

The finished system is set out in the *Encyclopaedia of the Philosophical Sciences* (1817; revised 1827 and 1830), a compendium Hegel wrote for his students to follow in lectures. Its plan is stated in one section. Philosophy "is subdivided into three parts : I. Logic, the science of the Idea in and for itself. II. The Philosophy of Nature: the science of the Idea in its otherness. III. The Philosophy of Mind : the science of the Idea come back to itself out of that otherness" (EL §18). "Idea" (*Idee*) here is a technical term: not a thought in someone's head but the full rational structure of reality, the unity of concept and objectivity (EL §§213–215, paraphrased). The three parts are not three subjects that happen to be bound together. They are one structure considered first purely, as the system of the categories; then as it exists outside itself, scattered in space and time; and then as it returns to itself in minds that know it.

Hegel describes the shape of the whole with an image he uses more than once. "Each of the parts of philosophy is a philosophical whole, a circle rounded and complete in itself", and because each circle breaks through the limits of its medium and gives rise to a wider one, "The whole of philosophy in this way resembles a circle of circles" (EL §15). The end of the *Encyclopaedia* closes the outer circle: philosophy, having traveled through nature and spirit, "has gone back to its beginning: its result is the logical system but as a spiritual principle" (Enc. §574). A system shaped like this has no foundation in the usual sense, no first truth on which the rest is stacked. What justifies the beginning is the whole that comes out of it, which is the claim of Argument 1.

The third part, the Philosophy of Spirit, has its own three divisions, and they organize most of what readers know as "Hegel". In Wallace's translation: "The development of Mind (Spirit) is in three stages" (Enc. §385). *Subjective spirit* is mind in its "self-relation": the individual soul, consciousness and intelligence, the topics of anthropology, phenomenology and psychology. *Objective spirit* is mind "realised, i.e. in a *world* produced and to be produced by it: in this world freedom presents itself under the shape of necessity" (Enc. §385): law, morality, the family, the economy and the state, which the *Philosophy of Right* expands into a book. *Absolute spirit* is mind "in its absolute truth" (Enc. §385), knowing itself in art, religion and philosophy. Hegel adds at once that the first two are finite (Enc. §386, paraphrased): the individual mind and the social world never fully match what they are trying to be, and absolute spirit is the sphere where spirit's knowledge of itself is no longer limited by an external object.

Where the *Phenomenology* fits is a long-standing puzzle. It was published in 1807 as the "first part" of a system, and it is also, in its own terms, an introduction that leads ordinary consciousness up to the standpoint from which the system begins. In the later *Encyclopaedia* a short section called "Phenomenology of Mind" reappears inside subjective spirit, covering only consciousness, self-consciousness and reason (Enc. §§413–439, paraphrased). The two roles do not sit easily together. The SEP article treats the 1807 book as a propaedeutic, an education of the reader up to "the standpoint of purely conceptual thought from which philosophy can be done", and notes that in its later chapters the shapes of consciousness give way to what look more like configurations of human social life (SEP, "Hegel," §3.1.1). [02-works-and-texts.md](02-works-and-texts.md) gives the publication history.

The vocabulary of the three parts recurs at every level. Hegel likes to describe a development as moving from what something is *an sich* (in itself, implicitly, as a seed is implicitly a tree), through what it is *für sich* (for itself, explicitly, set over against what it is not), to what it is *an und für sich* (in and for itself, both at once: explicit and at home with itself). The Logic, Nature and Spirit division is that pattern on the largest scale. So is the division of the Logic itself, as "the notion implicit and in germ", "the being-for-self and show of the notion", and "the notion in and for itself" (EL §83). So, as the *Philosophy of Right* will show, is the passage of the will from abstract right through morality to ethical life. The pattern is not a mechanical formula, and the next sections say why. But noticing it is the quickest way to find one's place in any Hegel text.

## The *Phenomenology*: a ladder and a highway of despair

The *Phenomenology* answers a question the system cannot answer for itself: why should anyone who starts where ordinary people start, with the senses, common sense and the sciences, accept the standpoint of the Logic at all? Hegel's Preface is scornful of philosophies that begin from an intuition of the Absolute and declare it beyond all distinctions. To give out such an Absolute "as the night in which, as we say, all cows are black" is "the very naivete of vacuous knowledge" (PhG ¶16; Baillie p. 15). The target is usually taken to be Schelling's philosophy of identity, or its imitators. What Hegel offers instead is a path. The individual "has the right to demand that science shall hold the ladder to help him to get at least as far as this position" (PhG ¶26; Baillie p. 23). The *Phenomenology* is that ladder.

What makes it a ladder and not a lecture is its method, set out in the Introduction (PhG ¶¶73–89). Hegel does not test consciousness against a standard brought in from outside, since any such standard would beg the question. Each shape of consciousness carries its own standard: it takes something to be true (its object, as it understands it) and it has a view of what knowing that object amounts to. The examination consists in watching whether the shape's knowledge measures up to its own object. When it does not, consciousness has to change its view of the object, and with it its standard. The new object comes out of the failure of the old one. Hegel says that this is "experience" in a special sense, and that the pathway to science is itself a science, the "Science of the Experience of Consciousness" (PhG ¶88; Baillie p. 88).

For the consciousness making the journey, the experience is painful. Each time, what it took for the truth is lost. "Because of that, the road can be looked on as the path of doubt, or more properly a highway of despair" (PhG ¶78; Baillie p. 78). Hegel at once distinguishes this from ordinary doubt, in which we jostle against "this or that supposed truth" and then return to it, "so that at the end the matter is taken as it was before" (PhG ¶78; Baillie p. 78). The *Phenomenology*'s scepticism is thoroughgoing and cumulative: it is "the conscious insight into the untruth of the phenomenal knowledge" (PhG ¶78; Baillie p. 78), and it does not give the lost positions back. It is also, Hegel claims, self-completing. The goal lies where knowledge "is no longer compelled to go beyond itself, where it finds its own self" (PhG ¶80; Baillie p. 80), and its concept and its object finally correspond. That point is absolute knowing.

Two features of the method shape every chapter. First, Hegel distinguishes what happens for consciousness from what happens "for us", the philosophical observers who already know where the road leads. For the consciousness in the grip of experience, what has arisen "has merely the character of object, while, for us, it appears at the same time as a process and coming into being" (PhG ¶87; Baillie p. 88). Consciousness experiences each transition as a loss and a surprise; the reader can see why it had to happen. Second, the order of the shapes is not a history of the world, though from the Spirit chapter on it borrows historical material. Sense-certainty, the master and the bondsman, and stoicism are logical positions that real people and cultures have occupied, not dated events. The library entry's reply to the "speculative anthropology" objection on Lordship and Bondage turns on exactly this point.

## Determinate negation, and what dialectic is and is not

The engine of the *Phenomenology* is named in the Introduction, and it is the single most useful idea to carry into any Hegel text. A position that fails does not fail into nothing. The sceptic, Hegel says, "always sees in the result only pure nothingness, and abstracts from the fact that this nothing is determinate, is the nothing of that out of which it comes as a result" (PhG ¶79; Baillie p. 80). A refutation has a content: it shows *why* the position failed, and that is information about what a better one would have to do. "When once, on the other hand, the result is apprehended, as it truly is, as determinate negation, a new form has thereby immediately arisen ; and in the negation the transition is made by which the progress through the complete succession of forms comes about of itself" (PhG ¶79; Baillie p. 80). That is *bestimmte Negation*, determinate negation, and it explains how the *Phenomenology* can claim that its sequence is necessary without importing a plan from outside. Each new shape is the old one's failure, understood.

The same structure has a name in German that Hegel exploits on purpose: *aufheben*, usually rendered "sublate" in modern English, "supersede" or "cancel" in Baillie. In the chapter on perception he stops to say what the word means: "to supersede (*aufheben*) is at once to negate and to preserve" (PhG ¶113; Baillie p. 107). In ordinary German the verb means to cancel or abolish, as a law is repealed, and also to keep or put away, as one keeps a letter; it also carries the literal sense of lifting up. Hegel uses all three at once. What is *aufgehoben* is no longer valid as a self-standing truth, it is retained as a moment of something larger, and it is raised to a higher level. The bud, in the Preface's image, "disappears when the blossom breaks through, and we might say that the former is refuted by the latter" (PhG ¶2; Baillie p. 2); and yet the stages are moments of one organic unity, each as necessary as the other.

In the *Encyclopaedia Logic*, Hegel gives the most compact statement of what he means by dialectic. "In point of form Logical doctrine has three sides : (a) the Abstract side, or that of understanding : (b) the Dialectical, or that of negative reason : (c) the Speculative, or that of positive reason" (EL §79; Wallace prints Greek letters α, β, γ, rendered here as a, b, c). The understanding fixes concepts and holds them apart: finite, infinite; one, many; cause, effect. In the dialectical moment "these finite characterisations or formulae supersede themselves, and pass into their opposites" (EL §81). And "The Speculative stage, or stage of Positive Reason, apprehends the unity of terms (propositions) in their opposition" (EL §82). Two clarifications follow in the text, and both matter. First, these are not three parts of logic or three steps a thinker performs in order: "These three sides do not make three parts of logic, but are stages or 'moments' in every logical entity" (EL §79). Second, the dialectic is not something the philosopher does to a concept from outside. Hegel complains that "It is customary to treat Dialectic as an adventitious art, which for very wantonness introduces confusion and a mere semblance of contradiction into definite notions" (EL §81 R), and insists instead that the dialectical principle "constitutes the life and soul of scientific progress, the dynamic which alone gives immanent connexion and necessity to the body of science" (EL §81 R). When the dialectic is used by the understanding alone, its result is mere negation, and "Dialectic becomes Scepticism" (EL §81 R). The positive result, as in the *Phenomenology*, comes from seeing that the negation is determinate: "The result of Dialectic is positive, because it has a definite content" (EL §82 R).

**What dialectic is not.** Most readers arrive with a formula: Hegel's method is "thesis, antithesis, synthesis". It is not Hegel's formula. The SEP's article on Hegel's dialectics states that, though Hegel was clearly influenced by Fichte, "he never adopted Fichte's triadic 'thesis—antithesis—synthesis' language in his descriptions of his own philosophy", though he did apparently use it in his lectures to describe Kant (SEP, "Hegel's Dialectics," §1, citing Mueller 1958 and Solomon 1983). The triad entered Hegel's reputation largely through Heinrich Moritz Chalybäus, a Kiel professor whose popular lectures on speculative philosophy from Kant to Hegel, published in 1837, described Being, Nothing and Becoming as a first methodical thesis, antithesis and synthesis and as a schema for the rest; so Gustav E. Mueller argued in "The Hegel Legend of 'Thesis-Antithesis-Synthesis'" (*Journal of the History of Ideas*, 1958), who also claimed that Marx picked the phrasing up in Berlin (Mueller 1958, pp. 413–414, paraphrased). The SEP adds a caution on the other side: versions of the triadic reading still have defenders, and the Being-Nothing-Becoming transition does fit the pattern, as do a few others; but many transitions do not, and the pattern should not be applied "too dogmatically" (SEP, "Hegel's Dialectics," §1). Hegel himself attacked, in the Preface, the formalistic use of Kantian "triplicity" that turns triadic form into a lifeless schema stamped onto a subject matter like a formula in mathematics (PhG ¶50, paraphrased; the SEP article discusses the passage, "Hegel's Dialectics," §1).

The practical lesson for a reader is simple. Do not look for a thesis, then a rival claim, then a compromise. Look for a position that, taken at its word, turns out to contradict itself, and then ask what the specific shape of that self-contradiction teaches. Often the "third" term is not a compromise between two rivals but the first position's own truth, which it could not state while it held itself apart.

## The road of the *Phenomenology*: consciousness to absolute knowing

The book's chapters fall into six large groups, and the dossier reads parts of each. What follows is the road in outline, with the argument numbers of [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md).

**Consciousness (chapters I–III, PhG ¶¶90–165; week 2).** Consciousness takes its object to be something independent of it, simply there to be known. Its first shape, *sense-certainty*, thinks the bare *this*, *here* and *now* given to the senses is the richest knowledge there is: its content "makes this prima facie appear to be the richest kind of knowledge" (PhG ¶91; Baillie p. 89). It turns out to be "the abstractest and the poorest kind of truth" (PhG ¶91; Baillie p. 89), because the moment it tries to say what it knows, *this*, *here* and *now* turn out to be universals that fit anything at all (Argument 3). *Perception* takes the object to be a thing with many properties, and cannot make the thing's unity and its many properties hang together (Argument 4). *Force and the understanding* posit a world of forces and laws behind appearance, a "supersensible" world, and end in the "inverted world", in which the inner world turns out to be the appearance's own structure stated twice over (Argument 5). The lesson of the whole group is that the object consciousness thought independent is shaped by the concepts consciousness brings to it. Consciousness has, without knowing it, been knowing itself.

**Self-consciousness (chapter IV, ¶¶166–230; weeks 3–4).** With that discovery the road reaches what Hegel calls the "turning-point" (PhG ¶177; Baillie p. 174). Self-consciousness first appears as *desire*: it is "only assured of itself through sublating this other, which is presented to self-consciousness as an independent life ; self-consciousness is Desire" (PhG ¶174; Baillie p. 172). But desire defeats itself, since consuming its object leaves it needing another one. Only an object that negates itself while staying independent can satisfy it, and that is another self: "Self-consciousness attains its satisfaction only in another self-consciousness" (PhG ¶175; Baillie p. 173) (Argument 6). Here the concept of spirit first appears, as "Ego that is 'we,' a plurality of Egos, and 'we' that is a single Ego" (PhG ¶177; Baillie p. 174).

The next paragraph opens the most famous section of the book. "Self-consciousness exists in itself and for itself, in that, and by the fact that it exists for another self-consciousness ; that is to say, it is only by being acknowledged or 'recognised'" (PhG ¶178; Baillie p. 175). *Anerkennung*, recognition, is not a courtesy added to an already complete self. It is part of what it is to be a self at all. The first form recognition takes is a struggle, because each self tries to prove that it is not tied to mere life: "it is solely by risking life, that freedom is obtained" (PhG ¶187; Baillie p. 180) (Argument 7). The struggle cannot end in death, which would destroy the recognizer, so it ends in submission: one becomes lord, the other bondsman. The outcome reverses itself. The lord is recognized only by someone he does not recognize, and "The truth of the independent consciousness is accordingly the consciousness of the bondsman" (PhG ¶193; Baillie p. 184). The bondsman has felt the fear of death, and "albeit the fear of his lord is the beginning of wisdom", consciousness is not yet aware in it of being for itself; "Through work and labour, however, this consciousness of the bondsman comes to itself" (PhG ¶195; Baillie p. 186), because in forming the thing it sees its own activity made lasting (Argument 8). Baillie translates *Herrschaft und Knechtschaft* as "Lordship and Bondage"; "master and slave" is a common later rendering that the library entry also uses.

The bondsman's freedom is still only inward, and the remainder of chapter IV follows three attempts to live it (Argument 9). *Stoicism* holds that the thinking self is free "on the throne as well as in fetters" (PhG ¶199; Baillie p. 193), by withdrawing from the world it cannot change. *Scepticism* carries that withdrawal through, dissolving every determinate claim, but contradicts itself in living by the claims it denies. The *unhappy consciousness* takes the contradiction into itself, "the consciousness of self as a divided nature, a doubled and merely contradictory being" (PhG ¶207), torn between a changeable self and an unchangeable beyond it can neither reach nor give up. Baillie's headnote reads it against medieval Christian devotion; that reading is his and many later commentators' gloss, not Hegel's label. The section ends when the self gives up its claim to be the single source of meaning and comes to see itself as one with reality, which is reason.

**Reason (chapter V; read only in outline).** "Reason is the conscious certainty of being all reality" (PhG ¶233; Baillie p. 224), and Hegel adds at once that this is how idealism announces its principle. The chapter tests that certainty in the observing sciences of nature and mind (including a celebrated attack on phrenology), in practical attempts to realize the self in the world, and in two shapes that bear on Kant: reason as lawgiver and reason as tester of laws. The last two are the ground of Hegel's charge that the moral law of pure practical reason is empty (Argument 12, drawn from PhG ¶¶419–437 together with PhR §135).

**Spirit (chapter VI; week 5).** "Reason is spirit, when its certainty of being all reality has been raised to the level of truth, and reason is consciously aware of itself as its own world, and of the world as itself" (PhG ¶438; Baillie p. 429). From here on the shapes are forms of social life. Chapter VI begins with the "ethical world" of the Greek city, read through Sophocles' *Antigone*: the human law of the city and the divine law of the family collide in Creon and Antigone, and the collision destroys the immediate harmony of Greek ethical life (PhG VI.A, ¶¶444–476, paraphrased; *Antigone* is named at ¶463). It passes through the Roman world of legal persons, the culture of self-alienation (*Entfremdung*) of the early modern world, and the Enlightenment's struggle with faith, to the French Revolution. *Absolute freedom* tries to make the general will immediately real in every individual, and so can tolerate no institution and no difference. Its "sole and only work and deed" is death, "with no more significance than cleaving a head of cabbage or swallowing a draught of water" (PhG ¶590; Baillie p. 599) (Argument 10). The chapter ends with German moral philosophy, conscience and the "beautiful soul", and with forgiveness as the mutual recognition of two consciences that each confess their one-sidedness.

**Religion (chapter VII).** Spirit's knowledge of itself, so far held in the form of social life, is now held as an object of worship and imagination: natural religion, the "art-religion" of Greece, and revealed religion, Christianity, whose content Hegel takes to be the truth of spirit, though held in the form of representation (*Vorstellung*), which Baillie renders "picture-thoughts" (the word recurs at the opening of PhG VIII, where Hegel looks back on religion's "picture-thoughts"; paragraph not pinned). The dossier reads none of this chapter.

**Absolute knowing (chapter VIII).** Absolute knowing is not knowledge of a new object. It is spirit's comprehension of the whole road as its own, "Absolute Knowledge or Spirit knowing itself as Spirit" (PhG ¶808; Baillie p. 822). The book ends by calling history and the science of appearing knowledge together "the recollection and the golgotha of Absolute Spirit" (PhG ¶808; Baillie p. 823): the remembered sequence of the shapes is both their preservation and their place of death.

## The Logic: its beginning and its divisions

This is where the dossier goes furthest past the learning path, and where it stops shortest. The learning path says never to read the *Science of Logic* (1812–16) solo, and the dossier keeps that rule: week 6 reads the shorter *Encyclopaedia Logic* (EL §§19–25, 79–82, 84–88), which Hegel wrote as a compendium, with Wallace's or the Hackett translation and a guide.

"Logic is the science of the pure Idea ; pure, that is, because the Idea is in the abstract medium of Thought" (EL §19). Logic in this sense is not formal logic, the study of valid inference, though it contains a treatment of judgment and syllogism. It is the study of the basic concepts, or "thought-determinations", through which anything at all is thought: being, quality, quantity, measure, essence, appearance, actuality, the concept itself. Kant had drawn up a table of such categories and held that they apply only to appearances. Hegel's Logic tries to derive them from one another, without borrowing anything from experience, and holds that in doing so it states the structure of what is. Whether "what is" there means the structure of being itself or the structure of any thinking that could count as knowledge is precisely the dispute between the readings described below under the tensions.

**The beginning.** A presuppositionless science must start with something that presupposes nothing, and that can only be the most indeterminate thought there is. "Pure Being makes the beginning : because it is on one hand pure thought, and on the other immediacy itself, simple and indeterminate ; and the first beginning cannot be mediated by anything, or be further determined" (EL §86). But pure being, with no determination at all, has no content to distinguish it from its opposite: "this mere Being, as it is mere abstraction, is therefore the absolutely negative : which, in a similarly immediate aspect, is just Nothing" (EL §87). Thinking being turns out to be thinking nothing, and thinking nothing turns out to be thinking being, since nothing too is pure indeterminacy. "The truth of Being and of Nothing is accordingly the unity of the two : and this unity is Becoming" (EL §88). Hegel admits how strange this sounds: the claim "seems so paradoxical to the imagination or understanding, that it is perhaps taken for a joke" (EL §88 R). Becoming, the vanishing of each into the other, is the first thought with a determinate content, and the *Zusatz* says so: "Becoming is the first concrete thought, and therefore the first notion : whereas Being and Nought are empty abstractions" (EL §88 Z). Argument 11 reconstructs the passage and the standard objection that it trades on an equivocation.

The beginning also shows why the Logic is circular in the good sense. The first category is the poorest, and every later category is a richer statement of what the first was trying to be. The Preface's formula for the Absolute applies here: "Of the Absolute it must be said that it is essentially a result" (PhG ¶20; Baillie p. 17), and the beginning is justified only by the end it leads to (Argument 1).

**The divisions.** "Logic is subdivided into three parts :— I. The Doctrine of Being: II. The Doctrine of Essence : III. The Doctrine of Notion and Idea" (EL §83). The *Doctrine of Being* treats thoughts of things as immediately there: quality (being, determinate being, being-for-self), quantity, and measure, the unity of the two. The *Doctrine of Essence* treats thoughts of things as having a ground behind their surface: identity and difference, ground, appearance, and finally actuality (*Wirklichkeit*), which is where the famous distinction between existence and actuality belongs. The *Doctrine of the Concept* (Wallace's "Notion", *Begriff*) treats thought that knows itself as the source of its own determinations: the subjective concept, judgment and syllogism; objectivity (mechanism, chemism, teleology); and the Idea, "the absolute unity of the notion and objectivity" (EL §213). The Logic closes with the absolute Idea, which is not one more category but the method that has been at work through all of them.

## Nature in outline

The dossier does not read the *Philosophy of Nature*, and this section is only a map. Nature is "the science of the Idea in its otherness" (EL §18). In the *Encyclopaedia*'s own section on it, nature is the Idea in the form of otherness, the Idea external to itself (Enc. §247, as reported in SEP, "Hegel," §3.2.1). It runs from mechanics (space, time, matter, motion, gravitation) through physics to organics (the earth, the plant, the animal organism), which leads over to spirit.

Two points matter for the rest of the system. First, nature is not deduced *a priori* in its details. Hegel says that nature's "impotence" sets limits to philosophy, so that it is pointless to demand that the concept explain or deduce nature's contingent products, the point he made against W. T. Krug's challenge to deduce the pen he was writing with (Enc. §250 R, paraphrased from the SEP's account; SEP, "Hegel," §3.2.1). Philosophy of nature takes its material from the empirical sciences and arranges it. Second, the SEP reports that recent work has pushed back against the old dismissal of Hegel's philosophy of nature, while granting that its scientific content is clearly a product of its time (SEP, "Hegel," §3.2.1).

## The *Philosophy of Right*: will, freedom and their worlds

The *Elements of the Philosophy of Right* (published late 1820, title page dated 1821) expands the objective spirit section of the *Encyclopaedia* into a book. Week 7 reads it in selection: the Preface, the Introduction on the will, abstract right on property, morality on Kant, and ethical life on the family, civil society and the state.

**Will and freedom.** The subject matter is the free will and the world it builds. "The territory of right is in general the spiritual, and its more definite place and origin is the will, which is free. Thus freedom constitutes the substance and essential character of the will, and the system of right is the kingdom of actualized freedom. It is the world of spirit, which is produced out of itself, and is a second nature" (PhR §4). "Right" (*Recht*) here is wider than law: "That a reality is the realization of the free will, this is what is meant by a right. Right, therefore, is, in general, freedom as idea" (PhR §29). The Introduction argues that freedom is neither the bare ability to abstract from every content (which, made into a politics, becomes the fury of destruction Hegel saw in the Terror) nor the ability to choose whatever one happens to want (which depends on contents one has not chosen). The will is fully free only when what it wills is freedom itself, embodied in a world: "the abstract conception of the idea of the will is in general the free will which wills the free will" (PhR §27).

The work's plan follows from that. "According to the stages in the development of the idea of the absolutely free will", the will is first immediate, as personality embodied in an external thing, "the sphere of abstract or formal right"; then turned back into itself as subjective individuality, "the sphere of morality"; and finally the unity of the two, in which "freedom exists as real substance, which is quite as much actuality and necessity as it is subjective will", which is ethical life (PhR §33).

**Abstract right.** The first sphere treats persons as bare bearers of rights, abstracting from everything particular about them. Its command is "Be a person and respect others as persons" (PhR §36). A person must give his freedom an external existence: "A person must give to his freedom an external sphere, in order that he may reach the completeness implied in the idea" (PhR §41). That sphere is property (Argument 13). Property is justified not by need, as the Addition notes, but by freedom: in the thing I own, my will becomes objective and recognizable to others. Abstract right then runs through contract, in which two wills recognize each other in exchanging property, and wrong, in which a particular will sets itself against right and punishment restores it.

**Morality.** *Moralität* is the will turned inward. "The moral standpoint is the standpoint of the will, not in its abstract or implicit existence, but in its existence for itself" (PhR §105); it "converts person into subject" (PhR §105). Its themes are purpose and responsibility, intention and welfare, and the good and conscience. Here Hegel places Kant. The moral law of pure practical reason is a real achievement, since it makes the will's own autonomy the root of duty. But held alone, "this philosophical acquisition is reduced to empty formalism, and moral science is converted into mere rhetoric about duty for duty's sake. From such a position can be derived no inherent doctrine of duties" (PhR §135 R). The test of non-contradiction cannot tell me which maxims to adopt unless content is brought in "from without" (PhR §135 R). That is Argument 12, and its other source is PhG ¶¶419–437.

**Ethical life.** *Sittlichkeit* (Dyde: "the ethical system", and in his contents "ethical observance") is the third sphere and the heart of the book. "The ethical system is the idea of freedom. It is the living good, which has in self-consciousness its knowing and willing, and through the action of self-consciousness its actuality" (PhR §142). In ethical life, duties are not abstract demands set against inclination but the customs, roles and institutions in which people already live and which they can recognize as expressions of their own freedom. Its three moments are, in Dyde's terms, "The direct or natural ethical spirit, the family", which loses its unity and becomes "The civic community, an association of members or independent individuals" (PhR §157), and finally the state.

*The family* is ethical life in the form of feeling, love. *Civil society* (*bürgerliche Gesellschaft*, Dyde's "civic community") is the realm of particularity: "The concrete person, who as particular is an end to himself, is a totality of wants and a mixture of necessity and caprice", but "each establishes and satisfies himself by means of others" (PhR §182). The Addition calls it "the realm of difference, intermediate between the family and the state" (PhR §182 A). It contains the system of needs (the market economy, which Hegel read in the political economists), the administration of justice, and the police (in the old sense of public authority over welfare) and the corporations. Hegel was among the first philosophers to see that a free market generates poverty it cannot cure. When "a large number of people sink below the standard of living regarded as essential for the members of society, and lose that sense of right, rectitude, and honour which is derived from self-support, a pauper class arises, and wealth accumulates disproportionately in the hands of a few" (PhR §244). Dyde's "pauper class" translates Hegel's *Pöbel*, the rabble; the Addition says that the rabble is defined not by poverty alone but by "a frame of mind, associated often with poverty, consisting in inner rebellion against the wealthy, against society, and against constituted authority" (PhR §244 A). Neither charity nor public works can solve it, and Hegel states "the seeming paradox that the civic community when excessively wealthy is not rich enough" (PhR §245). Civil society is then driven outward, to foreign markets and colonies (PhR §§246–248, paraphrased). Argument 14 reconstructs the analysis and asks whether Hegel solves the problem he states; the SEP notes that Marx later took this analysis as evidence for socializing the means of production, a conclusion Hegel did not draw (SEP, "Hegel," §3.2.2).

*The state* is ethical life in its fully self-conscious form. "The state, which is the realized substantive will, having its reality in the particular self-consciousness raised to the plane of the universal, is absolutely rational", and its end "has the highest right over the individual, whose highest duty in turn is to be a member of the state" (PhR §258; the OCR text misprints the section number as 268). The Remark at once distinguishes the state from civil society: if the state is confused with an arrangement for protecting property and personal freedom, membership becomes "at one's option" (PhR §258 R), and the Remark goes on to criticize the view, which it associates with Rousseau, that makes the state a contract resting on individual wills (PhR §258 R, paraphrased). The balance comes in §260: "The state is the embodiment of concrete freedom. In this concrete freedom, personal individuality and its particular interests, as found in the family and civic community, have their complete development" (PhR §260). The modern state's strength, Hegel argues, is that it lets subjectivity develop fully and still brings it back to the universal. Hegel's state is a constitutional monarchy with an estates assembly and a professional civil service; [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md) takes up how far it reflects the Prussia of 1820.

**The Preface.** The Preface wraps all this in its most quoted sentences (Argument 15). "What is rational is real ; And what is real is rational" (PhR Preface; Dyde's "real" translates *wirklich*, which the *Encyclopaedia* translator renders "actual"). In the *Encyclopaedia* Hegel answered the outcry: "existence is in part mere appearance, and only in part actuality" (EL §6 R), so the sentence is not a blessing on everything that happens to exist. The Preface also gives the task of philosophy: "every one is a son of his time ; so philosophy also is its time apprehended in thoughts" (PhR Preface), and it is no more able to leap beyond its world than an individual can "jump over Rhodes" (PhR Preface). To recognize "reason as the rose in the cross of the present" is reconciliation with reality (PhR Preface). And philosophy comes late: "When philosophy paints its grey in grey, one form of life has become old", and "The owl of Minerva takes its flight only when the shades of night are gathering" (PhR Preface).

## World history

The *Philosophy of Right* ends with world history, which is also the point of passage from objective to absolute spirit (SEP, "Hegel," §3.2.2). The fuller treatment is in the *Lectures on the Philosophy of History*, given five times in Berlin and assembled after Hegel's death from his manuscripts and students' notes. Eduard Gans edited the first edition; his preface, dated Berlin, June 8, 1837, is printed in Sibree's translation and names the student notebooks he used, among them those of Hegel's son Karl ("M. Charles Hegel"), who later prepared his own edition (SEP, "Hegel," §3.2.2, on Karl's text; [02-works-and-texts.md](02-works-and-texts.md) on the editions). That origin is a reason for caution: the text is a reconstruction of lectures, not a book Hegel wrote, and week 8 reads only its Introduction.

The thesis is one sentence. "The History of the world is none other than the progress of the consciousness of Freedom" (PH Introduction; Sibree pp. 19–20) (Argument 16). The progress has a famous schema: "The Oriental World knows only that One is Free : The Greeks and Romans recognize Some as free. The German Nations under the influence of Christianity, have attained the knowledge that All are Free" (PH, Sibree's analytical contents to the Introduction, keyed to p. 19; the running text puts it as "the Eastern nations knew only that one is free ; the Greek and Roman world only that some are free ; whilst we know that all men absolutely (man as man) are free", Sibree p. 20). Sibree's "German Nations" stands for Hegel's Christian peoples of post-Roman Europe, which the body of the lectures calls "the German World" and treats as including the Romanic and Germanic peoples together (PH, Sibree's contents to Part IV), not modern Germany alone.

Three concepts organize the account. The first is *spirit of a people* (*Volksgeist*): each historical community lives by a principle that shapes its law, religion, art and ethics, and its reflection on that principle eventually undermines it and gives rise to a new one, so that history is a dialectic of community types (SEP, "Hegel," §3.2.2). The second is the role of passion. "We may affirm absolutely that nothing great in the World has been accomplished without passion" (PH Introduction; Sibree p. 24): the Idea and the passions are "the warp" and "the woof of the vast arras-web of Universal History" (PH Introduction). World-historical individuals, of whom Caesar is the standard example, pursue their own ends and carry out a larger one. The third is the *cunning of reason*: "that it sets the passions to work for itself, while that which develops its existence through such impulsion pays the penalty, and suffers loss" (PH Introduction; Sibree p. 34). Hegel does not hide the cost. He describes history as "the slaughter-bench at which the happiness of peoples, the wisdom of States, and the virtue of individuals have been victimised" (PH Introduction; Sibree p. 22), and asks to what final aim those sacrifices were offered. His answer is freedom realized in the state: "The State is the Divine Idea as it exists on Earth" (PH Introduction; Sibree p. 41).

Hegel's account is, as the SEP says flatly, "a clearly Eurocentric account of history" (SEP, "Hegel," §3.2.2), and some of the Introduction's remarks on Africa and Asia are worse than Eurocentric; [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md) discusses them. The SEP also notes that the philosophy of history, like the philosophy of nature, does not deduce empirical events; it takes the results of empirical history as its material (SEP, "Hegel," §3.2.2).

## Absolute spirit: art, religion and philosophy

Absolute spirit is spirit knowing itself, no longer in a social world that never quite matches its concept, but as its own object. In the *Encyclopaedia*'s version, subjective and objective spirit "are to be looked on as the road on which this aspect of *reality* or existence rises to maturity" (Enc. §553). Absolute spirit has one content in three forms of knowing.

*Art* knows the absolute in sensuous form. As the consciousness of the absolute "first takes shape, its immediacy produces the factor of finitude in Art"; art is "the concrete *contemplation* and mental picture of implicitly absolute spirit as the *Ideal*" (Enc. §556). The *Lectures on Aesthetics* divide it into symbolic, classical and romantic art, with Greek sculpture as the point at which sensuous form and spiritual content fit perfectly. The so-called end-of-art thesis follows: for the modern world, art no longer carries the highest truth. The SEP calls this "Hegel's often misunderstood thesis of the death of art" and reads it as a claim about art's loss of ultimate cultural authority, not a prediction that art will stop being made (SEP, "Hegel," §3.2.3).

*Religion* knows the same content in representation (*Vorstellung*): in images, stories and doctrines. Christianity is the "revealed" religion because its content is the absolute as spirit: "It lies essentially in the notion of religion,—the religion i.e. whose content is absolute mind—that it be *revealed*, and, what is more, revealed *by God*" (Enc. §564). The incarnation, death and resurrection of God and the community of the spirit are, for Hegel, the truth of spirit told as a story.

*Philosophy* knows it in the form of the concept. "This science is the unity of Art and Religion" (Enc. §572): it holds together art's concreteness and religion's universal content, and grasps in thought what the other two present in intuition and image. That is why the *Encyclopaedia* ends by returning to the Logic (Enc. §574), and why Hegel can say that philosophy and religion have the same content in different forms. Whether this makes philosophy the truth of religion, or quietly replaces it, split his school within a decade of his death. The SEP reports that his followers divided into left, center and right factions over religion, after the publication in 1835–36 of D. F. Strauss's *Life of Jesus* (SEP, "Hegel," §3.2.3).

## How the pieces interlock

The truth is the whole, and the whole is subject: an activity that becomes what it is by differentiating itself and returning to itself. The *Phenomenology* leads ordinary consciousness to that standpoint by determinate negation, each shape failing by its own standard and its failure yielding the next, until consciousness finds that its object was itself, that a self exists only as recognized by another, and that reason is a shared social world, spirit. The Logic then thinks the forms of that whole purely, starting from empty being and ending in the Idea; nature is the Idea outside itself; spirit is the Idea come back to itself. Within spirit, the free will builds a world: property, contract and punishment; the moral subject; and ethical life, where family, civil society and state give freedom a content that morality alone cannot supply. World history is the progress of the consciousness of that freedom, and art, religion and philosophy are spirit's knowledge of itself, the last of them returning to the Logic. A good self-test is to take any single claim in Hegel and ask three questions: what is it the negation of, what does it preserve, and what would make it, in Hegel's sense, actual?

## Key terms

In the format of the repo [glossary](../../glossary.md), restricted to Hegel's usage, with the old translations' renderings and the common misreadings flagged.

- **Geist (spirit; Baillie and Wallace: "mind"):** reason that exists as a shared, self-knowing world, "Ego that is 'we'" (PhG ¶177, 438). Not a ghost or a separate cosmic person, whatever one concludes about its metaphysics; Sibree notes that it includes will as well as intelligence.
- **Subjective, objective and absolute spirit:** spirit as individual mind; as a world of law, morality and institutions; and as self-knowledge in art, religion and philosophy (Enc. §385).
- **Begriff (concept; Wallace and Baillie: "notion"):** a thought that develops its own determinations and unites universality, particularity and individuality (EL §163, paraphrased). Not a general idea abstracted from cases.
- **Idee (Idea):** "the absolute unity of the notion and objectivity" (EL §213). Not a mental representation.
- **Aufhebung (sublation; Baillie: "supersede", "cancel"):** "at once to negate and to preserve" (PhG ¶113), and also to raise. What is sublated survives as a moment, not as a self-standing truth.
- **An sich / für sich / an und für sich:** in itself or implicitly; for itself or explicitly, set against an other; in and for itself, explicit and at home with itself (EL §83). A pattern of development, not a mechanical triad.
- **Bestimmte Negation (determinate negation):** the result of a refutation taken as "the nothing of that out of which it comes" (PhG ¶79), so that it has a content and yields a new position.
- **Dialectic:** the moment in which finite determinations "supersede themselves, and pass into their opposites" (EL §81), one of three sides of every logical thought with understanding and speculation (EL §79). Not "thesis, antithesis, synthesis", which is not Hegel's formula.
- **Understanding (Verstand) and reason (Vernunft):** the understanding fixes and separates determinations; reason grasps them in their movement and unity (EL §§79–82). Not two separate faculties of a mind.
- **Speculative:** the "positive" side of reason, which "apprehends the unity of terms (propositions) in their opposition" (EL §82). Not idle guessing.
- **Vermittlung (mediation):** the way something is what it is through its relation to something else; opposed to immediacy (*Unmittelbarkeit*). Even being's immediacy proves mediated (EL §§86–88).
- **Immediate / abstract / concrete:** immediate is what is simply given; abstract is what is held apart from its relations ("Being and Nought are empty abstractions", EL §88 Z); concrete is what has grown together with its relations. "Abstract" is not "theoretical".
- **Das Absolute (the Absolute):** reality as a whole understood as self-developing; "essentially a result" (PhG ¶20). Not Schelling's undifferentiated night "in which ... all cows are black" (PhG ¶16).
- **Substance and subject:** the claim that the true is "not as Substance but as Subject as well" (PhG ¶17): the whole is an activity of self-differentiation and return, not an inert foundation.
- **Erfahrung (experience):** in the *Phenomenology*, the movement in which consciousness's knowledge fails by its own standard and a new object arises (PhG ¶¶86–88). Not sense experience.
- **Shape of consciousness (Gestalt des Bewusstseins):** a whole stance toward what counts as true and as knowing it, such as sense-certainty or stoicism.
- **Begierde (desire):** the first form of self-consciousness, which assures itself by negating its object (PhG ¶174) and so needs another self (¶175).
- **Anerkennung (recognition; Baillie: "acknowledged or 'recognised'"):** the relation in which a self-consciousness exists "by the fact that it exists for another self-consciousness" (PhG ¶178). Constitutive of selfhood, not a courtesy.
- **Herrschaft und Knechtschaft (lordship and bondage):** the unequal settlement of the struggle for recognition, which reverses itself through the bondsman's fear and work (PhG ¶¶189–196). Often rendered "master and slave".
- **Unhappy consciousness:** "the consciousness of self as a divided nature, a doubled and merely contradictory being" (PhG ¶207), split between a changeable self and an unreachable beyond.
- **Entfremdung (alienation; also *Entäußerung*, externalization):** spirit's condition in a world it has made but experiences as foreign, the theme of PhG VI.B (Baillie: "Spirit in self-estrangement"). Not yet Marx's economic concept.
- **Sittlichkeit (ethical life; Dyde: "ethical system", "ethical observance"):** "the idea of freedom", "the living good" embodied in institutions and customs (PhR §142).
- **Moralität (morality):** the standpoint of the will in its "existence for itself", which "converts person into subject" (PhR §105). Hegel's name for the Kantian standpoint, true but abstract.
- **Abstract right:** the sphere of persons, property, contract and wrong, whose command is "Be a person and respect others as persons" (PhR §36).
- **Recht (right):** "the realization of the free will"; "freedom as idea" (PhR §29). Wider than law or rights.
- **Freedom:** "the substance and essential character of the will" (PhR §4), fully realized when "the free will ... wills the free will" (PhR §27). Neither bare choice nor the absence of constraint.
- **Bürgerliche Gesellschaft (civil society; Dyde: "civic community"):** the sphere of particular needs and their mutual satisfaction, "the realm of difference, intermediate between the family and the state" (PhR §182 A). Not the state.
- **Pöbel (rabble; Dyde: "pauper class"):** those who sink below the standard of subsistence and lose the honor of self-support, defined also by "inner rebellion" against society (PhR §§244, 244 A).
- **Staat (state):** "the embodiment of concrete freedom" (PhR §260), "absolutely rational" (PhR §258). Ethical life fully conscious of itself, not a mere apparatus of government.
- **Wirklichkeit (actuality; Dyde: "real"):** existence that embodies its rational structure; "existence is in part mere appearance, and only in part actuality" (EL §6 R).
- **Volksgeist (spirit of a people):** the principle that informs a community's whole way of life in the philosophy of history (SEP, "Hegel," §3.2.2).
- **List der Vernunft (cunning of reason):** reason's working through passions that pursue other ends, "while that which develops its existence through such impulsion pays the penalty" (PH Introduction).
- **Vorstellung (representation; Baillie: "picture-thought"):** thought in the form of image and narrative, the form of religion, as opposed to the concept, the form of philosophy (Enc. §§564–572).
- **Absolute knowing:** "Spirit knowing itself as Spirit" (PhG ¶808), the comprehension of the whole road as spirit's own. Not omniscience about empirical facts.

## Known internal tensions

**(1) Metaphysical or post-Kantian?** Is Hegel's spirit a cosmic subject that comes to know itself through us, or is "spirit" Hegel's name for the self-correcting, socially shared practice of giving and asking for reasons? The SEP sets out three positions (SEP, "Hegel," §§2.2–2.4). The traditional metaphysical view reads Hegel as offering a metaphysico-religious account of God as absolute spirit, known through pure thought; Frederick Beiser (and Horstmann) defend a version of it against the newer readings. The post-Kantian view, developed from the late 1980s by Robert Pippin and Terry Pinkard and, in more explicitly analytic form, by Robert Brandom and John McDowell, reads Hegel as completing Kant's critique of metaphysics by socializing and historicizing Kant's idea of rational self-determination. The SEP adds that the older label "non-metaphysical" was dropped because it wrongly suggested that Hegel had no metaphysics at all, and that Pippin now describes Hegel's logic as a metaphysics "in a new sense". A third group, the revised metaphysical view, accepts Kant's critique of dogmatic metaphysics but holds that Hegel's logic is still an account of the structures of being itself: Stephen Houlgate and Robert Stern are the SEP's examples, with Kenneth Westphal, James Kreines and others. The dossier presents this as a live debate. It bears on almost every concept above, above all *Geist*, the Absolute and the claim that logic is ontology. [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md) gives the positions in full.

**(2) The end of history.** If world history is the progress of the consciousness of freedom, and "we know that all men absolutely (man as man) are free" (PH Introduction; Sibree p. 20), has history in principle ended? Hegel calls Europe "absolutely the end of History, Asia the beginning" (PH Introduction, "Classification of Historic Data"; Sibree p. 109), but in a geographical sense, and earlier in the Introduction he calls America "the land of the future" (Sibree p. 90), declining to prophesy about it because philosophy deals with what is. The owl of Minerva makes the same point from the other side: philosophy can comprehend a form of life only once it has matured, and cannot say what comes next. Alexandre Kojève's lectures in 1930s Paris shaped a generation of French readers, Hyppolite, Sartre and Lacan among them (SEP, "Hegel," §1), and made the end of history a famous Hegelian thesis; whether Hegel held it, in anything like Kojève's form, is disputed.

**(3) The Prussian state.** "What is rational is real" and "The state is the march of God in the world" (PhR §258 A, Dyde) read, out of context, like a philosophy of submission. Critics from Hegel's own century to Karl Popper, whose *The Open Society and Its Enemies* (1945) linked Hegel to twentieth-century totalitarianism, have read them so (SEP, "Hegel," §§2.2–2.3). Defenders reply on three fronts. Actuality is not mere existence (EL §6 R). The "march of God" sentence comes from an Addition compiled from students' lecture notes, not from Hegel's printed text, and modern translators construe the German differently, roughly as saying that it is the way of God in the world that there should be a state (a paraphrase of the modern reading, not checked here against the German; see [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md)). And the state Hegel describes, a constitutional monarchy with representative estates, a professional civil service and protected spheres for civil society and conscience, was by the library entry's account more liberal than the Prussia of 1820. The SEP treats Popper's reading as a polemic that later scholarship, beginning with J. N. Findlay's 1958 book, set out to answer (SEP, "Hegel," §2.3).

**(4) The place of the individual.** The state's end "has the highest right over the individual, whose highest duty in turn is to be a member of the state" (PhR §258), and the cunning of reason lets individuals be "sacrificed and abandoned" (PH Introduction; Sibree p. 34). Yet the same book insists that in the modern state "personal individuality and its particular interests ... have their complete development" (PhR §260), and the morality section defends the "right of the subjective will" (PhR §§107, 132, paraphrased). Kierkegaard's objection, in the library entry's words, is that the system leaves out the existing individual: a person deciding how to live is not helped by learning his place in the progress of spirit. Whether Hegel's reconciliation of individual and whole is real, or a whole that absorbs its members, is the question every reading in (1) must also answer.

**(5) Necessity and contingency.** The *Phenomenology* claims that its sequence of shapes is necessary, and the Logic claims to derive its categories without presupposition. Yet the *Phenomenology*'s later chapters follow a particular European history, and Hegel himself concedes that nature's "impotence" leaves contingent detail beyond philosophy's reach (Enc. §250 R, via the SEP). The rabble problem shows the same strain inside the *Philosophy of Right*: Hegel states a contradiction of civil society (PhR §245) and offers colonization and the corporations rather than a resolution (Argument 14). How much of the system's necessity survives these concessions is part of the debate in (1).
