# Bibliography — G. W. F. Hegel
**English** | [简体中文](../../zh/focus-studies/g-w-f-hegel/08-bibliography.md)

Annotated and tiered: read downward only as far as your questions require. No prices are given; none was read on a publisher page for this list. Years and publishers come from a publisher or library page, from the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy's bibliographies, or from the copyright page of a saved scan, and each entry says which where sources disagree (searched and read 13 November 2026). Whatever could not be read on a page is marked unchecked. One fact governs the whole list: the *Phenomenology* is cited by **paragraph** in the Miller and Pinkard numbering (PhG ¶178), the *Encyclopaedia* and the *Philosophy of Right* by Hegel's own section numbers (EL §86; PhR §41), and the philosophy of history by Sibree's part and heading. Any reading edition below finds every reference in this dossier. [02-works-and-texts.md](02-works-and-texts.md) explains the corpus and gives the edition rulings this list follows. This file quotes no Hegel; the rest of the dossier quotes him in the spelling of the four public-domain translations in §1, and nothing from a modern translation.

## 1. Primary texts — the editions the plan uses

The dossier separates **reading editions** (modern, in copyright, recommended, never quoted) from **quoted editions** (old, public domain, free, and quoted in their own spelling). Use the first for study and the second to check a quotation word for word.

### The *Phenomenology of Spirit*

**Terry Pinkard (ed. and trans.), *The Phenomenology of Spirit* (Cambridge Hegel Translations; Cambridge University Press, 2018; translated with Michael Baur).** The ruling for modern English in Weeks 1–5. The year is the print date on its Cambridge Core page; the SEP's bibliography gives 2017, so a library catalogue may show either. It keeps Miller's paragraph numbers, so every ¶ in this dossier finds its place.

**A. V. Miller (trans.), *Phenomenology of Spirit* (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1977; paperback with J. N. Findlay's analysis, 1979).** The alternative ruling, and the better solo companion because of Findlay's paragraph-by-paragraph analysis. Most scholarship before 2018 quotes Miller, and he is the source of the ¶ numbering. The 1977 date is from a review record in the *American Political Science Review* (Cambridge Core), the 1979 paperback from Oxford University Press's product page; the SEP gives 1979.

**Michael Inwood (trans.), *Hegel: The Phenomenology of Spirit* (Oxford University Press, 2018).** A third modern translation, with an extensive commentary by the translator; the year and publisher are from an Open Library record only. The dossier does not rule on it. Inwood is better known to Hegel readers for *A Hegel Dictionary* (Blackwell, 1992, per the SEP), which is the quickest way to look up a term.

### The *Science of Logic* and the *Encyclopaedia Logic*

**George di Giovanni (trans.), *The Science of Logic* (Cambridge Hegel Translations; Cambridge University Press, 2010).** The modern standard, per the SEP bibliography and the publisher's Cambridge Core page, which says it contains the revised Book I (1832) with Book II (1813) and Book III (1816). Not set reading. Take it up after Houlgate's guide (§3) and the *Encyclopaedia Logic*, not before.

**A. V. Miller (trans.), *Hegel's Science of Logic* (London: George Allen & Unwin).** The older English standard, which most secondary literature cites by page. An Open Library record dates it 1966 and lists it in two volumes; I remembered 1969, and the date is unchecked here. Check the copyright page of your copy before citing its page numbers.

**T. F. Geraets, W. A. Suchting and H. S. Harris (trans.), *The Encyclopaedia Logic: Part I of the Encyclopaedia of Philosophical Sciences with the Zusätze* (Indianapolis: Hackett, 1991).** The ruling for Week 6 (EL §§19–25, 79–82, 84–88). It translates the prefaces to all three editions, carries the Additions, and labels them as Additions, which is why it comes first. Publisher and year are from a PhilPapers review record. Its glossary-style introduction on Hegel's German helps with terms that [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md) lists.

**Klaus Brinkmann and Daniel O. Dahlstrom (trans. and eds.), *Encyclopaedia of the Philosophical Sciences in Basic Outline, Part 1: Logic* (Cambridge University Press, 2010).** The Cambridge alternative; the SEP's bibliography lists it with this year. Use it when you want the Logic in the same series as di Giovanni's *Science of Logic*, so the translators' terms match.

### The *Philosophy of Right*

**H. B. Nisbet (trans.), Allen W. Wood (ed.), *Elements of the Philosophy of Right* (Cambridge Texts in the History of Political Thought; Cambridge University Press, 1991; lii + 514 pp.).** The ruling for Week 7. Wood's notes are the best short guide to the politics and mark which passages are Additions. The year and page count are from a *Hegel Bulletin* review record.

**T. M. Knox (trans.), *Hegel's Philosophy of Right* (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1952), and Stephen Houlgate (ed.), *Outlines of the Philosophy of Right*, Knox's translation revised by Julian Schmidt (Oxford World's Classics, 2008).** The older English, still cited in the literature on Hegel and the state. An Open Library record gives Knox's first printing as 1952 and a catalogue record of the Houlgate edition as 2008, but I did not see the series name or the reviser on a publisher page, so those two details are unchecked. Houlgate's introduction is the reason to pick the World's Classics edition up.

### Philosophy of history, aesthetics and the early writings

**H. B. Nisbet (trans.), *Lectures on the Philosophy of World History: Introduction: Reason in History*, from Johannes Hoffmeister's German edition, with an introduction by Duncan Forbes (Cambridge University Press, 1975).** The ruling for Week 8 and Argument 16. The year comes from the Library of Congress sample page (card number 74-79137) and the BBC *In Our Time* reading list, which names the same Cambridge edition as 1975.

**Robert F. Brown and Peter C. Hodgson (eds. and trans.), *Lectures on the Philosophy of World History*, vol. 1: *Manuscripts of the Introduction and the Lectures of 1822–3* (Oxford University Press, 2011).** The critical edition of the same lectures. The BBC programme page names it; use it to see how the 1822–23 course differs from the text Gans and Karl Hegel printed.

**T. M. Knox (trans.), *Aesthetics: Lectures on Fine Art*, 2 vols. (Oxford University Press, 1975).** Week 8, in summary. Read it with the SEP's warning about Hotho's editing (§4). The SEP's "Hegel" bibliography lists a 1998 Oxford printing, "first published 1975"; the Clarendon imprint, if you meet it, is unchecked.

**T. M. Knox (trans.), *Early Theological Writings* (Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 1948), and H. S. Harris and Walter Cerf (trans.), *The Difference Between Fichte's and Schelling's System of Philosophy* and *Faith and Knowledge* (Albany: SUNY Press, 1977).** Background to Week 1, not set reading; [02](02-works-and-texts.md) gives what each contains and what Knox leaves out.

### The free texts the dossier quotes

Every one is a public-domain scan on the Internet Archive; the archive's own catalogue record gave the title, year and publisher listed here.

- ***The Phenomenology of Mind*, trans. J. B. Baillie (London: Sonnenschein; New York: Macmillan, 1910):** [vol. I](https://archive.org/details/cu31924091023832) and [vol. II](https://archive.org/details/phenomenologyofm02hege). Baillie prints no paragraph numbers, so the dossier gives the ¶ and Baillie's page together. For orientation only, marxists.org has [a Baillie text with paragraph numbers added](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/hegel/works/ph/); the dossier uses it to match paragraphs and quotes it nowhere. Baillie's one-volume revision of 1931 may still be in copyright in some countries, so the dossier quotes the 1910 scan only.
- ***The Logic of Hegel*, trans. William Wallace, 2nd ed. (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1892):** [archive.org scan](https://archive.org/details/thelogicofhegel00hegeuoft); a second scan has the same title page. Its copyright page reads second edition 1892, reprinted 1904. Wallace keeps Hegel's section numbers and prints the Additions without labels, which is why the dossier cites an Addition as "EL §n Z" and checks it against Geraets, Suchting and Harris. Project Gutenberg #55108 is also a transcription (seen in a search result, not downloaded).
- ***Hegel's Philosophy of Right*, trans. S. W. Dyde (London: G. Bell, 1896):** [archive.org scan](https://archive.org/details/cu31924014578979). Dyde keeps Hegel's section numbers, calls the Remarks "Notes", and renders *wirklich* as "real"; his wording is stiff, and the dossier flags it where an argument turns on a word.
- ***Lectures on the Philosophy of History*, trans. J. Sibree (London: Henry G. Bohn, 1857):** [archive.org scan](https://archive.org/details/lecturesonphilos00hegeiala). This translates Karl Hegel's 1840 text, not the Hoffmeister text Nisbet translates.
- **Gustav E. Mueller, "The Hegel Legend of 'Thesis-Antithesis-Synthesis'," *Journal of the History of Ideas* 19 (1958):** the article behind the dossier's treatment of the triad as a misreading (see §4 and [03](03-system-and-concepts.md)). Where it is hosted was not checked here; look it up by the citation.

**Avoid** anthologies that print famous sentences (the rational and the actual, the owl of Minerva, world history as the court of judgment) without saying whether they come from a paragraph, a Remark or an Addition; any *Science of Logic* read solo; and the Baillie one-volume revision as a quotation source.


## 2. First secondary books, in order

Hegel is the author the learning path warns about most, so the order matters. Read one short book before Week 1, a clear one-volume exposition during Weeks 1–5, and a commentary on the *Phenomenology* beside the primary text. Every item below was checked as far as its note says.

1. **Peter Singer, *Hegel: A Very Short Introduction* (Oxford University Press, 2001).** One evening before Week 1. The Oxford page describes it as a broad discussion of Hegel's ideas and an account of his major works. The SEP's bibliography lists the original, *Hegel* (Oxford University Press, 1983), so the VSI is that book reissued (inferred from the two dates; unchecked). It is short and, as I remember it, sceptical (unchecked), so its Hegel is not the one the metaphysical readers defend: treat it as a map and test it in Weeks 2–3. The learning path's rule that a slim volume per hard figure comes before the primary text is this book's job.
2. **Stephen Houlgate, *An Introduction to Hegel: Freedom, Truth and History* (2nd ed.; Oxford: Blackwell, 2005).** The first-edition date and publisher are the SEP's (London: Routledge, 1991). The main exposition for Weeks 1–8: it follows Hegel's own order, chapter by chapter, and Houlgate argues for a reading on which the *Logic* is a real ontology and the *Phenomenology* its introduction (both from memory; unchecked). That is a side in the debate in [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md), not neutral ground, and the BBC page lists this edition as the programme's reading on Hegel and history. The Philosophize This! pages for episodes 66, 74 and 76 also name it as further reading.
3. **Terry Pinkard, *Hegel: A Biography* (Cambridge University Press, 2000).** The life, for [01-life-and-times.md](01-life-and-times.md) and Week 1. The copyright page of the Library of Congress sample reads "First published 2000"; the Internet Archive's catalogue record gives xx + 780 pages. It also summarizes the key works for a general reader, but Pinkard is a leading voice of the non-metaphysical Hegel (a reviewer's phrase on a university review site), so his summaries lean that way.
4. **Frederick C. Beiser, *Hegel* (Routledge, 2005).** Weeks 6–8. Beiser defends a metaphysical Hegel, and this is the shortest book that does so with the historical background; the year and publisher are from the SEP and an Open Library record, which agree. Put it beside Pippin (§3) to see the whole debate in two short books.
5. **Charles Taylor, *Hegel* (Cambridge University Press, 1975).** Weeks 7–9. The SEP lists it, and an Open Library record gives the same year and publisher. It is long and heavily historical, and it reads Hegel as the philosopher of expressive unity and the modern state (both from memory; unchecked), which is why it supplies the background to the Marx and Kierkegaard selections in Week 9. Taylor's shorter *Hegel and Modern Society* (Cambridge, 1979) is the usual one-sitting version (from memory; unchecked).

If you want a reference book rather than a narrative, keep Michael Inwood, *A Hegel Dictionary* (Blackwell, 1992, per the SEP) at your elbow.

## 3. Advanced and specialist, by area

Open these when a week's checkpoint question has narrowed to a debate. Most are academic books, best borrowed. A scholar's position not read at the SEP, the IEP or a publisher's description is marked unchecked.

- **A commentary on the *Phenomenology* (Weeks 1–5).** Robert Stern, *Hegel and the Phenomenology of Spirit* (Routledge GuideBook; Routledge, 2002 per the SEP, though an Open Library record says 2001), revised as *The Routledge Guidebook to Hegel's Phenomenology of Spirit* (Routledge, March 2013 per the publisher's page). Routledge's page lists it as covering the context, each part of the text and its reception; the dossier uses the 2013 edition as the guide to take beside Pinkard or Miller. Its author is counted on the metaphysical side of the debate in the dossier's sorting (the SEP's own sorting was not checked). For a fuller reading, Terry Pinkard, *Hegel's Phenomenology: The Sociality of Reason* (Cambridge University Press, 1994, per the SEP), which reads the book as a social theory of reason. For the opposing, epistemological reading, Kenneth R. Westphal, *Hegel's Epistemology: A Philosophical Introduction to the Phenomenology of Spirit* (Hackett, 2003, per the SEP; the characterization is from memory, unchecked). Michael N. Forster, *Hegel's Idea of a Phenomenology of Spirit* (University of Chicago Press, 1998, per the SEP), is, I believe, the book on the method (unchecked).
- **The two sides of the metaphysical debate.** *Non-metaphysical* or "post-Kantian": Robert B. Pippin, *Hegel's Idealism: The Satisfactions of Self-Consciousness* (Cambridge University Press, 1989; the date is from an Internet Archive catalogue record and the SEP). Pippin reads Hegel's idealism as a development of Kant's theory of the self-legislating subject, and the book is the foundation of the non-metaphysical reading; a snippet of Cambridge's own description, seen in a search result (its page would not load), calls it the most important book on Hegel to have appeared in the past ten years, which dates the claim to about 1989. His *Hegel's Practical Philosophy: Rational Agency as Ethical Life* (Cambridge University Press, 2008) carries the same view into ethics and politics (SEP; Open Library). *Metaphysical*: Beiser (§2), Houlgate (§2) and Stern, which is how the dossier sorts them (the SEP's own sorting was not checked). The rest of the literature turns on this question, so read one book from each side before the Week 9 capstone.
- **Brandom and the inferentialist reading (Weeks 3–5, optional).** Robert B. Brandom, *A Spirit of Trust: A Reading of Hegel's Phenomenology* (Belknap Press of Harvard University Press, 2019; the SEP gives Harvard University Press, 2019). Brandom reads the *Phenomenology* semantically, as a theory of how concepts get their content through recognition and recollection. It is very long and written for readers who know analytic philosophy of language, so it is not a first commentary. Brandom's own video lectures on the book are free (§5).
- **The *Science of Logic* (Week 6 and after).** Stephen Houlgate, *The Opening of Hegel's Logic: From Being to Infinity* (Purdue University Press), the usual guide to the Doctrine of Being. The SEP gives 2005; an Open Library record gives 2006, so check your copy. Beiser's *Hegel* (§2) and Pippin's *Hegel's Realm of Shadows: Logic as Metaphysics in "The Science of Logic"* (University of Chicago Press, 2019; from memory, unchecked) are the two sides on the *Logic*. Stanley Rosen, *The Idea of Hegel's "Science of Logic"* (University of Chicago Press; the SEP's bibliography gives 2013 in one place and 2014 in another), is a third voice.
- **Ethics, right and the state (Week 7; Arguments 12–15).** Allen W. Wood, *Hegel's Ethical Thought* (Cambridge University Press, 1990; the SEP's two bibliographies and an Open Library record agree). The standard book on Hegel's moral and social philosophy, and the best commentary on the empty-formalism charge of Argument 12. Frederick Neuhouser, *Foundations of Hegel's Social Theory: Actualizing Freedom* (Harvard University Press, 2000; SEP and Open Library agree), on the system of right as a theory of freedom realized in institutions, with a long treatment of civil society for Argument 14. Shlomo Avineri, *Hegel's Theory of the Modern State* (Cambridge University Press, 1972, per the SEP), the book that made the case that Hegel is not the Prussian apologist his critics made him (my characterization; unchecked; the IEP's Hegel entry names the charge it answers). Pippin (above) and Axel Honneth, *Freedom's Right: The Social Foundations of Democratic Life* (Columbia University Press and Polity, 2014; Open Library), give two contemporary appropriations. On recognition (Argument 7), Honneth, *The Struggle for Recognition: The Moral Grammar of Social Conflicts* (MIT Press; the SEP says 1995, an Open Library record 1996), and Pippin's *Hegel on Self-Consciousness* (Princeton University Press, 2011; Open Library, which also lists 2010).
- **History and art (Week 8; Argument 16).** For history, the BBC *In Our Time* page on Hegel's philosophy of history prints a reading list; from it, and on its authority only: Joe McCarney, *Hegel on History* (Routledge Philosophy Guidebook, 2000); Terry Pinkard, *Does History Make Sense? Hegel on the Historical Shapes of Justice* (Harvard University Press, 2017); Peter C. Hodgson, *Shapes of Freedom: Hegel's Philosophy of World History in Theological Perspective* (Oxford University Press, 2012); and Jean Hyppolite, *Introduction to Hegel's Philosophy of History*, trans. Bond Harris and Jacqueline Bouchard Spurlock (University Press of Florida, 1996). For art, Stephen Houlgate's SEP entry (§4) is the place to start, and its bibliography leads on.
- **Early Hegel and the development of the system.** H. S. Harris, *Hegel's Development: Toward the Sunlight 1770–1801* (Clarendon Press, 1972) and *Hegel's Development II: Night Thoughts (Jena 1801–6)* (Oxford University Press, 1983), then *Hegel's Ladder*, 2 vols. (Hackett, 1997), a line-by-line commentary on the *Phenomenology* (all per the SEP). The standard English account of how Hegel's thought grew, and the specialist's book for ¶¶1–72 (both descriptions from memory; unchecked). Heavy; use it as a reference beside the primary text, not as reading.
- **Reception and the twentieth century (Week 9).** Alexandre Kojève, *Introduction to the Reading of Hegel*, ed. Allan Bloom, trans. J. H. Nichols, Jr. (Basic Books, 1969, per the SEP), and Jean Hyppolite, *Genesis and Structure of Hegel's "Phenomenology of Spirit"* (Northwestern University Press, 1974, per the SEP), the two French readings that shaped existentialism and Marxism (the influence claim is from memory; unchecked). For the English-language rediscovery of Hegel, Walter Kaufmann, *Hegel: Reinterpretation, Texts, and Commentary* (Doubleday, 1965; the SEP and Open Library agree), which the learning path names as a model of a guided-excerpts volume. The reception debates are in [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md).
- **Companions and handbooks.** Frederick C. Beiser (ed.), *The Cambridge Companion to Hegel* (Cambridge University Press, 1993; Open Library has a 2006 reprint). Michael N. Forster's chapter on Hegel's dialectical method is in it (SEP). Dean Moyar (ed.), *The Oxford Handbook of Hegel* (Oxford University Press, 2017, per the SEP's citations), and Stephen Houlgate and Michael Baur (eds.), *A Companion to Hegel* (John Wiley & Sons, 2011, per the BBC reading list). Use them to find a specialist article on one argument.

## 4. Reference

- **[SEP, "Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/hegel/)** — by Paul Redding (first published 13 February 1997; substantive revision 19 September 2025). Life, the works and their editions, the system, and a long bibliography with an "English Translations of Key Texts" list that this file checks against. The source of most edition facts in §1. Read it before Week 1 and again after Week 4.
- **[SEP, "Hegel's Dialectics"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/hegel-dialectics/)** — by Julie E. Maybee (first published 3 June 2016; substantive revision 2 October 2020). Read it for Week 6: it is the dossier's check on the thesis–antithesis–synthesis formula; it notes that the pattern fits some transitions (being, nothing, becoming among them) but many more badly, as [02](02-works-and-texts.md) reports.
- **[SEP, "Hegel's Aesthetics"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/hegel-aesthetics/)** — by Stephen Houlgate (first published 20 January 2009; substantive revision 3 April 2024). Week 8. It gives the publication history of the lectures, the Hotho question and the 2022 discovery of Heidelberg transcripts.
- **[SEP, "Hegel's Social and Political Philosophy"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/hegel-social-political/)** — by Thom Brooks (first published 3 June 2021; substantive revision 31 March 2025). Week 7 and Arguments 12–15, with a short bibliography of its own.
- **[IEP, "Hegel: Social and Political Thought"](https://iep.utm.edu/hegelsoc/)** — by David A. Duquette (St. Norbert College). The plainer first pass for Week 7. It opens by naming the charge that Hegel was a nationalist apologist for the Prussian state. The page shows no publication date. **There is no general IEP entry on Hegel at the obvious address**: `iep.utm.edu/hegel/` returns a bare image page with no article, and the guesses I tried for Hegel's logic, aesthetics and dialectic entries returned "not found". The learning path advises using the IEP first for Hegel; for the whole philosopher that advice cannot be followed today (see the corrections log).
- **[PhilPapers, "Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel"](https://philpapers.org/browse/georg-wilhelm-friedrich-hegel)** — for finding articles on one argument (address unchecked; reached by following the site's usual pattern).
- **The journals.** *Hegel Bulletin* (Cambridge), whose review records supplied the dates for the Nisbet–Wood and Geraets–Suchting–Harris editions in §1, and *The Owl of Minerva*, which the SEP's bibliography cites repeatedly.

## 5. Media (verified 13 November 2026; assignments in [07-study-plan.md](07-study-plan.md))

Each item was checked on its own page or in its show's feed. Where a page does not state a date or length, none is given here. The learning path warns that audio is for preview and review, never a replacement for the reconstruction in your own words.

- **Gregory B. Sadler, *Half Hour Hegel* (free on YouTube; the series index is on [Sadler's Substack](https://gregorybsadler.substack.com/p/the-half-hour-hegel-series)).** A line-by-line commentary on the whole *Phenomenology*, each lecture covering one to four paragraphs. The index page says Sadler worked on it for nine years and made **376** commentary videos, listed as Lectures 1–376, and it gives each lecture's paragraph range; its last entry is Lecture 376 on ¶808. (The learning path says 379 videos and about 190 hours; see the corrections log. The page states no running times, so the hours figure is unchecked here, although the lecture name suggests half an hour each.) The index lets you match lectures to the dossier's weeks exactly:
  - Week 1, Preface ¶¶1–37: Lectures 1–16.
  - Week 2, ¶¶90–165: Lectures 39–65.
  - Week 3, ¶¶166–196: Lectures 66–79.
  - Week 4, ¶¶197–230: Lectures 80–95.
  - Week 5, ¶¶419–437 for Argument 12: Lectures 168–176; ¶¶444–476: Lectures 180–194; ¶¶582–595: Lectures 244–251.

  The learning path restricts this series to the Preface and the master–slave block (about 3–4 hours). The dossier goes further, in the same weeks and the same paragraphs, and never beyond them: this is the best free companion for exactly those sections. I read the index page, not the videos, so I cannot vouch for the quality of any lecture.
- **Robert Brandom, video lectures on *A Spirit of Trust* (free).** Brandom's Pittsburgh page ([Video and Multimedia](https://sites.pitt.edu/~rbrandom/Video%20Mark%203%20d.html)) lists "18 Lectures from A Spirit of Trust" (the Leipzig Humboldt Lectures, 2015–2019, in five parts that follow the *Phenomenology*'s order: Introduction, consciousness chapters, self-consciousness and recognition, agency, and the historicity of normativity), the video of all 14 sessions of his 2021 graduate seminar on the *Phenomenology*, and a three-lecture series, "A Semantic Sonata in Kant and Hegel" (Pittsburgh, 2008). The page says his channel holds 94 lecture and seminar videos ([channel](https://www.youtube.com/@BobBrandomPitt)). The [2021 seminar syllabus](https://sites.pitt.edu/~rbrandom/Courses/Hegel%20(2021)/ST%202021%20Main.html) assigns *Phenomenology* chapters 1 and 2 (sense-certainty and perception) with video of each meeting. Running times are not stated on either page. These are lectures for readers who have read Brandom's argument or are about to; they are Week 2–3 optional extras for readers who want the inferentialist side of the debate, not an introduction.
- ***Philosophize This!*** (Stephen West; free). Four episodes name Hegel in their titles, read on the show's own pages. These pages state neither a date nor a length, so none is given.
  - ep. 66, ["Hegel Pt. 1 – Introduction"](https://www.philosophizethis.org/podcast/episode-066-hegel-pt-1-introduction): its page says it explores two views of freedom and introduces Hegel's idea that everything, even consciousness, evolves through a process called the dialectic. Further reading named on the page: Michael Allen Fox, *The Accessible Hegel* (2005) and Houlgate. Before Week 1.
  - ep. 67, "What Hegel Was Saying!": a search result's description reads "We continue our unfortunately abridged discussion on Hegel from last episode." I could not load its own page (the third-party mirror refused access, and my guess at the show's address returned "not found"), so the title and number come from that search result only.
  - ep. 74, ["Hegel Returns!"](https://www.philosophizethis.org/podcast/hegel-returns): freedom as understanding how beliefs and values are shaped by culture and change. Its page describes the dialectic as "thesis, antithesis, synthesis", which is the formula this dossier treats as a misreading; hear it with [03](03-system-and-concepts.md) open. Its further reading adds Klaus Vieweg, *Hegel: The Philosopher of Freedom* (2023; publisher unchecked).
  - ep. 76, ["Hegel's God"](https://www.philosophizethis.org/podcast/hegels-god): God as an infinite process of self-determination rather than a being; further reading is Singer's VSI (2001), Houlgate (2005) and Howard P. Kainz, *An Introduction to Hegel: The Stages of Modern Philosophy* (1996). Week 8.

  West is a skilled popularizer, not an academic; the learning path says he simplifies, occasionally to distortion on Kant and Hegel. Preview and review with him, never reconstruct from him.
- **BBC Radio 4, *In Our Time*** (free on BBC Sounds). **["Hegel's Philosophy of History"](https://www.bbc.co.uk/programmes/m0017k8w)** — 52 minutes; Melvyn Bragg with Sally Sedgwick (Boston University), Robert Stern (Sheffield) and Stephen Houlgate (Warwick). The page's "Last on" entry is Thursday 26 May 2022, a repeat date, not necessarily the first broadcast. It describes Hegel's history as the progress in the consciousness of freedom and covers the "end of history" thesis. Week 8, with Argument 16. The page's reading list is the source of the history titles in §3. **I found no *In Our Time* programme on the *Phenomenology* of Spirit** in the searches I ran: the programme titled "Phenomenology" is about the Husserlian movement, not Hegel.
- ***History of Philosophy Without Any Gaps*** (Peter Adamson; free). **The series has not reached Hegel.** Its [Podbean feed](https://feed.podbean.com/hopwag/feed.xml), read 13 November 2026, ends at ep. 503, ["Free Market: Dutch Capitalism"](https://hopwag.podbean.com/e/hop-503-free-market-dutch-capitalism/) (8 November 2026). The only mention of Hegel anywhere in the feed is in a description of an interview about Shakespeare, which says it discusses his influence "on such thinkers as Hegel". Check the feed before you start; a Hegel run, when it comes, belongs after Week 5.
- **Wi-Phi (Wireless Philosophy).** A search of [wi-phi.com](https://www.wi-phi.com/) for "hegel" on 13 November 2026 returned "Sorry, no results were found." Nothing is assigned from it.
- **Free university courses.** No free academic lecture course on Hegel alone was verified for this list, apart from Brandom's seminar above.
