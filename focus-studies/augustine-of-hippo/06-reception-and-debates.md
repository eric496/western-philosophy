# Augustine Received: Doctor of Grace, Father of Quarrels, and the Fights Still Open

*Focus study: Augustine of Hippo · [Dossier home](README.md) · [Library entry](../../eras/02-medieval/augustine-of-hippo.md) · [Study guide](../../study/augustine-confessions.md)*

Augustine's readers were arguing with him before he died. Within a century a church council had trimmed his doctrine of grace. For a thousand years the Latin West quoted him more than any other Father, and in the sixteenth century Catholics and Protestants each claimed him. The Enlightenment blamed him for original sin and persecution, and the twentieth century read him as a psychologist of time, a political realist and the first modern self. This file tells that story by era, states seven live debates with their named positions, and closes with the standard misconceptions in full form and the myths about the life. The *Confessions* is quoted from E. B. Pusey's translation (1838), the *City of God* from Marcus Dods's (1871), and other works from the Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers translations.

## The fifth and sixth centuries: semi-Pelagianism and the Council of Orange

Augustine's last works answered his own readers. Around 426 monks at Hadrumetum in North Africa asked why anyone should be rebuked for sin if only grace makes the will good, and he wrote *On Grace and Free Will* and *On Rebuke and Grace*. In 428–29 Prosper of Aquitaine reported that monks in southern Gaul accepted his doctrine of grace and balked at predestination. Augustine replied with *On the Predestination of the Saints* and *On the Gift of Perseverance*.

The Gallic monks had their own theologian. John Cassian's thirteenth *Conference* (c. 426–28) held that grace is necessary for salvation and allowed that the first movement toward God can come from the will. Vincent of Lérins's *Commonitorium* (434) proposed the test of what has been believed everywhere, always and by all, and many scholars read it as aimed at Augustine's novelties. The label "semi-Pelagian" is anachronistic. It was coined in the late sixteenth century, and none of these men thought of himself as half a Pelagian.

The Second Council of Orange (529), under Caesarius of Arles and confirmed by Pope Boniface II in 531, settled the question for the Latin church. Its canons, drawn largely from Augustine and Prosper, teach that even the beginning of faith is a gift of grace and that Adam's sin passed both death and sin to his descendants. Its conclusion anathematizes anyone who holds that God predestines some to evil, and it says nothing of irresistible grace. The result was Augustine's grace without his hardest predestinarian conclusions. The council's acts were then little known in the Middle Ages and were rediscovered in the sixteenth century, just as the Reformation reopened the question.

## The medieval Augustine

The medieval West read Augustine more than any other Christian author. Two textbooks fixed his authority. Gratian's *Decretum* (c. 1140), the basis of canon law, drew on him for marriage, penance and war. Peter Lombard's *Sentences* (c. 1155–58) arranged patristic quotations by topic, with Augustine by a wide margin the most quoted, and from the thirteenth century every master of theology lectured on it.

[Boethius](../../eras/02-medieval/boethius.md) had already carried his account of eternity into the *Consolation*. [Anselm](../../eras/02-medieval/anselm-of-canterbury.md)'s preface to the *Monologion* (1076) says it contains nothing that disagrees with the Fathers, above all Augustine's *On the Trinity*. [Aquinas](../../eras/02-medieval/thomas-aquinas.md) cites him on nearly every page while recasting him in Aristotelian terms. Predestination split the ninth century. Gottschalk of Orbais taught a double predestination, of the elect to life and the damned to death, and was condemned at Quierzy in 849. [John Scotus Eriugena](../../eras/02-medieval/john-scotus-eriugena.md), asked to answer him, argued in *On Predestination* (851) that evil is nothing and so cannot be predestined, and was himself condemned at Valence in 855. In the thirteenth century Franciscans led by Bonaventure defended an "Augustinian" theory of divine illumination against Aquinas.

## The Augustinian order

Augustine founded communities and no order. In 1256 Pope Alexander IV's Grand Union joined groups of Italian hermits into the Order of the Hermits of Saint Augustine, a mendicant order. Gregory of Rimini (d. 1358) revived the strict anti-Pelagian Augustine and held that unbaptized infants suffer pain of the senses as well as the loss of God, which earned him the nickname *tortor infantium*, "torturer of infants." Martin Luther entered the order's house at Erfurt in 1505.

## Petrarch and the humanists

[Petrarch](../../eras/03-early-modern/renaissance-humanists.md) carried a pocket copy of the *Confessions* given to him by the Augustinian friar Dionigi da Borgo San Sepolcro. His letter on the ascent of Mont Ventoux (*Familiares* IV.1, dated 26 April 1336) has him open it at the summit at *Conf.* X.8.15: "And men go abroad to admire the heights of mountains, the mighty billows of the sea, the broad tides of rivers, the compass of the ocean, and the circuits of the stars, and pass themselves by." In the *Secretum* (1347–53) Petrarch staged a dialogue between himself and "Augustinus" on his love for Laura and his hunger for fame.

## The Reformation: Luther, Calvin and Trent

Luther lectured on Romans in 1515–16 with Augustine's *On the Spirit and the Letter* beside Paul. His *Disputation against Scholastic Theology* (1517) set Augustine against the late scholastics, and *The Bondage of the Will* (1525) answered Erasmus's *On Free Choice* (1524) with an Augustinian denial that fallen free choice contributes anything to salvation. Luther also judged that Augustine had not fully grasped justification by faith. Augustine held that grace makes the sinner just inwardly; Luther held that Christ's righteousness is credited to the sinner.

Calvin's *Institutes* (1536; final edition 1559) quotes Augustine more than any other Father, as A. N. S. Lane's *John Calvin: Student of the Church Fathers* (1999) documents. Calvin claimed him for double predestination and could cite the *Enchiridion*: "to the condemnation of those whom in His justice He has predestined to punishment, and to the salvation of those whom in His mercy He has predestined to grace" (*Enchiridion* 100). The Council of Trent's decree on justification (1547) answered with an Augustine read through Orange: grace precedes every good act, and the will moved by grace freely cooperates.

## Jansenism and the *Augustinus*

Cornelius Jansen, bishop of Ypres, read Augustine's anti-Pelagian works for some twenty years and died in 1638 with his summary complete. The *Augustinus* appeared at Louvain in 1640. It argued that the fallen will cannot resist the grace God gives the elect and that Jesuit theology had revived the errors of the Gallic monks. Urban VIII condemned it in 1642, and Innocent X's *Cum occasione* (1653) condemned five propositions said to be in it. Antoine Arnauld, theologian of the convent of Port-Royal, accepted that the propositions were heretical and denied that Jansen held them. Arnauld had also written the Fourth Objections to Descartes's *Meditations* (1641), which point out that Augustine used the *cogito* argument first (see the [Descartes dossier](../rene-descartes/README.md)). [Pascal](../../eras/03-early-modern/blaise-pascal.md)'s *Provincial Letters* (1656–57) defended Arnauld, and the *Pensées* rest on an Augustinian account of fallen nature. [Malebranche](../../eras/03-early-modern/nicolas-malebranche.md) built his doctrine of seeing all things in God on Augustine's theory of illumination.

## The Enlightenment critique

[Pierre Bayle](../../eras/03-early-modern/bayle-and-voltaire.md) attacked Augustine twice. His *Philosophical Commentary* (1686–88) answered the persecution of the Huguenots after 1685, whose defenders had reprinted Augustine's letters on coercing the Donatists, and argued that "compel them to come in" (Luke 14:23) cannot license forced belief. His *Dictionary* (1697) argued that the Manichaean two-principle view explains experience better than the orthodox one. Voltaire treated original sin as Augustine's invention and a scandal to reason. [Rousseau](../../eras/03-early-modern/jean-jacques-rousseau.md) borrowed the title *Confessions* and denied, in the *Letter to Beaumont* (1763), any original perversity in the human heart. Edward Gibbon's *Decline and Fall* (chapter 33, 1781) judged that Augustine's learning was too often borrowed and his arguments too often his own, and that his rigid system of grace had become the law of the Latin church.

## Nietzsche

[Nietzsche](../../eras/04-19th-century/friedrich-nietzsche.md) read the *Confessions* in 1885. In a letter to Franz Overbeck of 31 March 1885 he called Augustine a false old rhetorician and his Christianity a Platonism vulgarized for slaves. *Beyond Good and Evil* §50 (1886) cites him for a passion for God that lacks nobility. Christian humility, which Augustine made the cure for pride, is the kind of revaluation that the *Genealogy of Morals* (1887) traces to slave morality. See the [Nietzsche dossier](../friedrich-nietzsche/README.md).

## The twentieth century

**The convert and his sources.** In 1888 Gaston Boissier and Adolf von Harnack noted that the dialogues written at Cassiciacum in 386–87 sound like a Neoplatonist's, while the *Confessions*, a decade later, tells a penitent's conversion. Harnack also called Augustine the first modern man, for his inwardness. Prosper Alfaric (1918) concluded that Augustine converted to Neoplatonism in 386 and to Catholic Christianity only gradually. Pierre Courcelle's *Recherches sur les Confessions de saint Augustin* (1950) reconstructed the Milan circle of Ambrose and the Christian Platonists and showed that Augustine's Platonism and his Christianity reached him through the same people.

**Brown's biography.** Peter Brown's *Augustine of Hippo* (1967) read the life through the late Roman world, and its portrait of a bishop hardening into a severe controversialist shaped a generation. In 1981 Johannes Divjak published twenty-nine unknown letters, and in the 1990s François Dolbeau found twenty-six unknown sermons in a Mainz manuscript. Brown's new edition (2000) added two epilogues based on them and softened his picture: the late Augustine appears as a hard-pressed pastor more than a remote authoritarian.

**Political Augustinianism.** H.-X. Arquillière's *L'augustinisme politique* (1934) argued that medieval readers had flattened the two cities into church and empire and so absorbed the state into the church. R. A. Markus's *Saeculum* (1970) argued that the mature Augustine leaves a *secular* realm, neither holy nor demonic, where the two cities share an interest in earthly peace, which made him an ancestor of liberal pluralism. John Milbank's *Theology and Social Theory* (1990) denied that he leaves any neutral sphere, since all peace is ordered by one love or the other. Reinhold Niebuhr's essay "Augustine's Political Realism" (1953) made him the patron of Christian realism: political order restrains sin and never abolishes it. The text all of them read is the definition of the two cities, formed "by the love of self, even to the contempt of God; the heavenly by the love of God, even to the contempt of self" (*City of God* XIV.28).

**Philosophers.** [Husserl](../../eras/05-20th-century/edmund-husserl.md)'s lectures on internal time-consciousness (1905, published 1928) open with *Confessions* XI. [Heidegger](../../eras/05-20th-century/martin-heidegger.md) lectured on "Augustine and Neoplatonism" at Freiburg in 1921. [Hannah Arendt](../../eras/05-20th-century/hannah-arendt.md) wrote her dissertation on love in Augustine (Heidelberg, 1929, under [Karl Jaspers](../../eras/05-20th-century/karl-jaspers.md)), and her idea of natality rests on the sentence *initium ut esset homo creatus est*: "That this beginning, therefore, might be, the first man was created" (*City of God* XII.20 in Dods; XII.21 in most modern editions). [Camus](../../eras/05-20th-century/albert-camus.md), another North African, wrote his 1936 thesis on Christian metaphysics and Neoplatonism, with Plotinus and Augustine as its poles. Wittgenstein's *Philosophical Investigations* (1953) opens with *Confessions* I.8 (see the [Wittgenstein dossier](../ludwig-wittgenstein/README.md)). Charles Taylor's *Sources of the Self* (1989) made Augustine's turn inward the origin of the modern self.

**Feminist critique and recovery.** Rosemary Radford Ruether (1974) made Augustine a main source of the church's subordination of women and its fear of sexuality. The texts supply material. *On the Trinity* XII.7.10 holds that "the woman together with her own husband is the image of God," and that she alone, considered as helper, is not, although her mind, considered as mind, bears the image. *The Literal Meaning of Genesis* IX.5 concludes that woman was made as a helper for childbearing, since for other help another man would have served better. Elaine Pagels's *Adam, Eve, and the Serpent* (1988) argued that his doctrine of concupiscence suited a church allied with imperial power. A second line recovered the women in the texts. Catherine Conybeare's *The Irrational Augustine* (2006) showed Monica taking part as a philosopher in the Cassiciacum dialogues. Danuta Shanzer (2002) studied the concubine of some fifteen years, who, Augustine writes, "returned to Afric, vowing unto Thee never to know any other man, leaving with me my son by her" (*Conf.* VI.15.25).

**Augustine as an African.** Brown insisted that Augustine was a Numidian provincial who spent all but about five years of his life in Africa. James O'Donnell's *Augustine: A New Biography* (2005) went further. He read the Donatists as the majority church of Africa and Augustine as the leader of a minority party allied with imperial power, and he treated the *Confessions* as a bishop's self-justification. In April 2001 the Algerian state co-sponsored a colloquium, "Augustin, africain et universel," in Algiers and Annaba (ancient Hippo), and Algerian writers now claim him as a national figure. His letters show a Latin-speaking church in a Punic-speaking countryside; he sought a Punic-speaking bishop for the town of Fussala. Whether his family was Berber is unknown. The suggestion rests mainly on Monica's name, which some derive from a Libyan deity.

## Live debates

**(1) Did the garden scene happen as told?** The *Confessions* reports a child's voice from a neighbouring house "chanting, and oft repeating, 'Take up and read; Take up and read'" (*Conf.* VIII.12.29). The **literary reading** begins with Courcelle (1950). He noted that the fig tree recalls Nathanael under the fig tree (John 1:48), that the scene copies Antony's conversion by a chance reading, which Augustine cites in the same paragraph, and that the voice belongs to a stock of literary oracles. Courcelle did not deny a real conversion; he held that the bishop of 397 shaped its telling. O'Donnell's commentary (1992) and biography (2005) press further and treat the narrative as a constructed theological argument whose surface facts cannot be checked. The **historical reading** of John J. O'Meara (*The Young Augustine*, 1954), and broadly of Brown, holds that Augustine described a remembered event, since Alypius, who was present, was alive and a bishop when the book appeared. Most scholars now accept that something decisive happened in Milan in 386 and that its narration is shaped.

**(2) Did Augustine change his mind on free will?** *On Free Choice of the Will* (begun 388) defends free choice against the Manichees, and the Pelagians later quoted it against him. In *Retractations* I.9 he replied that he had been arguing a different question there. He dated his own turn to *To Simplician* (396): "I laboured indeed on behalf of the free choice of the human will, but God's grace overcame" (*On the Predestination of the Saints* 4.8, quoting *Retractations* II.1). The **break reading** treats 396 as a rupture; Kurt Flasch's *Logik des Schreckens* (1990) called it the founding of a theology of terror. The **continuity reading** of Carol Harrison's *Rethinking Augustine's Early Theology* (2006) finds grace and divine initiative in the earliest works. James Wetzel's *Augustine and the Limits of Virtue* (1992) argues that the late Augustine keeps a coherent freedom: the will acts from its own loves, and grace reorders the loves. John Rist (1969) argued that the late doctrine leaves the fallen will free only to sin, with every good will caused by God, and so cannot support responsibility in the ordinary sense.

**(3) Original sin and the damnation of infants.** Augustine read Romans 5:12 in a Latin version that says all sinned *in quo*, meaning in Adam; the Greek is usually rendered *because* all sinned. On his reading every human shares Adam's guilt, and an infant who dies unbaptized is condemned, though "the mildest punishment of all will fall upon those who have added no actual sin, to the original sin they brought with them" (*Enchiridion* 93). Julian of Eclanum, the ablest Pelagian, charged that a God who damns infants for another's sin is unjust and that the doctrine is Manichaeism renamed. Augustine answered that God's judgments are hidden and just, and that the church's baptism of infants for the remission of sins presupposes their guilt. Later theology softened the result. Abelard and Aquinas held that unbaptized infants suffer only the loss of the vision of God, the doctrine called Limbo, and the Catholic International Theological Commission (2007) called hope for their salvation well founded. The Orthodox churches never adopted inherited guilt.

**(4) Just war and the coercion of the Donatists.** *City of God* XIX.7 admits the objection, "But, say they, the wise man will wage just wars," and replies: "As if he would not all the rather lament the necessity of just wars, if he remembers that he is a man." *Against Faustus* XXII.74 names what is evil in war: "The real evils in war are love of violence, revengeful cruelty, fierce and implacable enmity, wild resistance, and the lust of power." Gratian and Aquinas (*Summa* II-II q. 40) built these texts into the just war tradition. Roland Bainton (1960) read Augustine as the point where the church left early Christian pacifism; Paul Ramsey (1961) read his position as love using force to protect the neighbor. Coercion is the harder case. Until about 405 Augustine opposed forcing the Donatists. After the Edict of Unity (405) and the violence of the Circumcellion bands he changed his mind, and his Letter 93 (c. 408) defends the laws with Luke 14:23 and the converts at Thagaste who thanked him. Brown's "St. Augustine's Attitude to Religious Coercion" (1964) explained the change as a pastor's response to local conditions. Bayle's objection, that he handed every later persecutor a text, is the standard one, and most modern readers accept it.

**(5) Did Augustine anticipate the *cogito*?** *City of God* XI.26 answers the skeptics: "For if I am deceived, I am." Related arguments appear in *On Free Choice* II.3.7 and *On the Trinity* X.10.14. Descartes replied to the comparison in November 1640 that Augustine used the argument to prove our existence and then to find an image of the Trinity, while he used it to show that the thinking "I" is an immaterial substance (see the [Descartes dossier](../rene-descartes/06-reception-and-debates.md)). Gareth Matthews's *Thought's Ego in Augustine and Descartes* (1992) argued that both reason in the first person from certainty about their own minds, and that *On the Trinity* X also moves from self-knowledge to the mind's immateriality. Stephen Menn's *Descartes and Augustine* (1998) argued that Descartes took over an Augustinian program of finding God by turning inward. The opposing view, common among Descartes scholars, is that Augustine never makes self-certainty the foundation of all knowledge, so the resemblance is local.

**(6) Is the theory of time subjective?** *Confessions* XI.14.17 asks the question philosophers kept quoting: "What then is time? If no one asks me, I know: if I wish to explain it to one that asketh, I know not." [Russell](../../eras/05-20th-century/bertrand-russell.md)'s *History of Western Philosophy* (1945) praised the account as an advance on Greek philosophy and read it as a subjective theory, like Kant's, on which time exists only in the mind. Wittgenstein (*Blue Book*; *Philosophical Investigations* §89) quoted the same sentence as a puzzle that comes from misunderstanding the grammar of a word, to be dissolved and not solved. [Paul Ricoeur](../../eras/05-20th-century/paul-ricoeur.md)'s *Time and Narrative* (1983) took the *distentio animi* as an aporia that narrative, beginning with Aristotle's *Poetics*, answers in practice. Realist readers reply that his claim concerns what we measure, not whether events occur in order. Roland Teske (*Paradoxes of Time*, 1996) suggested that the mind in question may be the world soul, as in Plotinus. The library entry and study guide leave open whether time requires a mind.

**(7) Predestination.** From *To Simplician* onward Augustine taught that God chooses the elect without regard to merit, gives them grace that infallibly converts them, and gives them perseverance; the rest remain in the condemned mass of humanity. The *Enchiridion* speaks of predestination to punishment as well as to grace (*Enchiridion* 100). The **double predestination reading** of Gottschalk, Calvin and Jansen takes such texts at face value. The **single predestination reading** of Orange and most Catholic theology holds that God predestines to grace only and permits the damnation sinners earn. Augustine's language supports both: he speaks of predestination to punishment and insists that God causes no one's sin. The objection, from Julian to the present, is that justice cannot coexist with an election the damned could do nothing to change. Augustine's reply, from Romans 9, is that no one is owed grace, so withholding it wrongs no one. Jesse Couenhoven (2013) defends a compatibilist reading on which responsibility survives.

## Misconceptions, in full form

- **"The *Confessions* is just an autobiography."** Books I–IX narrate the life, and the narrative is selected to argue a thesis about restless love and grace. Books X–XIII leave narrative for memory, time and creation. The book is a prayer from its first page, "Thou madest us for Thyself, and our heart is restless, until it repose in Thee" (*Conf.* I.1.1), and the reader overhears it.

- **"Augustine hated the body, sex, and the world."** That was the Manichaean position, and he spent years attacking it: every nature, as made by God, is good. His later doctrine of concupiscence did make sexual desire the plainest sign of a will out of control, and whether it burdened Western attitudes to the body is a real debate. The claim that he thought matter evil is a mistake.

- **"Original sin means Augustine thought infants are wicked."** The infant of *Confessions* I.7 shows self-love before teaching, not chosen sin. The doctrine concerns inherited guilt and a wounded will. It did lead him to the damnation of unbaptized infants, which he softened only to "the mildest punishment" and which later theology largely gave up (debate 3).

- **"Augustine's theory of time means time is unreal, or only in your head."** Book XI asks what measurement measures and answers with the soul's distension across memory, attention and expectation. It does not deny change or the order of events. Whether time could exist with no mind is debate (6).

- **"Augustine abandoned reason when he converted."** He held that faith seeks understanding. After 386 he wrote the Cassiciacum dialogues, *On Free Choice*, *On the Trinity* and *Confessions* XI. What he gave up was the belief that reason alone can heal the will.

- **"'Love, and do what you will' licenses anything."** The line (*Homilies on 1 John* 7.8) comes from a sermon on correction. Its point is that an act's worth depends on the love behind it, so a loving father may punish and a flatterer may caress. Augustine used the same principle to defend coercing the Donatists.

## Myths about the life

- **The child's game.** Retellings say Augustine heard children at play. He says only that he wondered "whether children were wont in any kind of play to sing such words: nor could I remember ever to have heard the like" (*Conf.* VIII.12.29), and that he took the voice as a command from God. Scholars have proposed a game, a work song, a misheard phrase or a literary device. Courcelle also drew attention to a manuscript variant reading *divina domo*, a divine house, for *vicina domo*, a neighbouring house; standard editions print the neighbouring house. The source of the voice is unknown, and Augustine leaves it so.

- **"Only not yet."** The prayer "Give me chastity and continency, only not yet" (*Conf.* VIII.7.17) is usually quoted as the motto of the man in Milan on the eve of conversion. Augustine dates it to the start of his youth, in a sentence that counts twelve years since he read Cicero's *Hortensius* at nineteen: "I wretched, most wretched, in the very commencement of my early youth, had begged chastity of Thee." In Milan he recalls it as evidence of how long he had been deferring.

- **The deathbed.** Possidius's *Life* (chapter 31) reports that in his last illness, in the third month of the Vandal siege of Hippo, Augustine had the penitential psalms copied and fixed to the wall where he could read them, that he asked to be left alone for his last ten days except for the doctors' visits and meals, and that he died on 28 August 430 without a will, having nothing to leave. That account comes from a friend close to the event. Later embellishments, including miracles at the bedside, have no early source.
