# Augustine: The Arguments
**English** | [简体中文](../../zh/focus-studies/augustine-of-hippo/04-arguments.md)

*Focus study · [Dossier home](README.md) · Library entry: [Augustine of Hippo](../../eras/02-medieval/augustine-of-hippo.md) · The system these arguments build: [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md) · Study guide: [*Confessions*](../../study/augustine-confessions.md)*

Each argument below is reconstructed in numbered premises in the house style: what the argument proves, the reconstruction, its textual basis, the strongest standard objection, and the best reply, Augustine's own where he gives one and the literature's where he does not, labeled as such. Quotations from the *Confessions* come from E. B. Pusey's translation (1838), which keeps its own *Thou* and *hast*, capitals and punctuation. Pusey prints no chapter numbers, so references use the book, chapter and section numbers that every modern edition prints, including Boulding and Sheed: (*Conf.* VIII.12.29) means Book VIII, chapter 12, section 29. Quotations from the *City of God* come from Marcus Dods's translation (1871) and cite book and chapter, so (*City of God* XI.9) means Book XI, chapter 9. The *Enchiridion* and *On Christian Doctrine* are quoted from the Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers translations; the *Enchiridion* is cited by its continuous chapter number, *On Christian Doctrine* by book, chapter and section. Works with no public-domain translation used here, such as *On Free Choice of the Will*, are paraphrased and cited by book and chapter.

Arguments 1 to 7 follow the *Confessions* in order, from the opening prayer through the pear theft, the break with the Manichees, the divided will and the analysis of memory, to Book XI on time. They match weeks 1 to 4 of the study plan. Arguments 8 to 14 turn to the treatises and the *City of God*.

**Argument 1. The restless heart: God as the soul's good and its rest (*Conf.* I.1, X.20–23)**

*What it proves:* that every human desire is at bottom a desire for happiness, that only an unchanging good can satisfy that desire, and that this good is God. The argument gives the *Confessions* its plot. Augustine tells his life as a search for rest in things that could not give it.

The book opens with its thesis in one sentence: "Thou madest us for Thyself, and our heart is restless, until it repose in Thee" (*Conf.* I.1.1). Book X supplies the argument behind it.

1. Everyone wants to be happy. Augustine treats this as beyond dispute: "is not a happy life what all will, and no one altogether wills it not?" (*Conf.* X.20.29).
2. Happiness is rest in the good one loves. To enjoy a thing, he writes elsewhere, "is to rest with satisfaction in it for its own sake" (*On Christian Doctrine* I.4.4).
3. Anything that can be lost cannot give secure rest, because the fear of losing it disturbs the possession. The death of an unnamed friend in Thagaste teaches him this. He had "poured out my soul upon the dust, in loving one that must die, as if he would never die" (*Conf.* IV.8.13).
4. Everyone also wants truth and no one wants to be deceived: "I have met with many that would deceive; who would be deceived, no one" (*Conf.* X.23.33). So "a happy life is joy in the truth" (*Conf.* X.23.33).
5. The truth that the mind finds above itself is unchanging and cannot be lost against one's will (*Conf.* VII.17.23, X.26.37).
Therefore only God, who is that truth, can give the rest that every desire seeks: "when I seek Thee, my God, I seek a happy life" (*Conf.* X.20.29).

The argument governs how creatures are loved, not whether. "Blessed whoso loveth Thee, and his friend in Thee, and his enemy for Thee. For he alone loses none dear to him, to whom all are dear in Him who cannot be lost" (*Conf.* IV.9.14). The image Augustine uses late in the book is weight: each body seeks its own place, and "My weight, is my love" (*Conf.* XIII.9.10). Love carries the soul where it goes, and disordered love carries it downward.

**Textual basis.** *Conf.* I.1.1; IV.4.7–IV.9.14 (the friend's death); X.20.29–X.23.34 (the happy life); XIII.9.10 (love as weight); *On Christian Doctrine* I.3.3–I.5.5 (use and enjoyment). The eudaimonist premise comes from Cicero's lost *Hortensius*, which Augustine read in his nineteenth year (*Conf.* III.4.7); see [Cicero](../../eras/01-ancient/cicero-and-lucretius.md).

**Objection.** Two objections are standard. The first is logical. A desire does not prove that its object exists, and an unsatisfied longing may simply be part of the human condition, as [Schopenhauer](../../eras/04-19th-century/arthur-schopenhauer.md) held. The second is ethical and comes from Hannah Arendt's dissertation *Love and Saint Augustine* (*Der Liebesbegriff bei Augustin*, 1929). Augustine divides things into those to be enjoyed and those to be used (*On Christian Doctrine* I.3.3). If only God may be enjoyed, the neighbour can only be used, or loved *in God*, which means loved for something other than himself. Arendt argued that this makes the neighbour, and the shared world, a stop on the way to somewhere else. See [Hannah Arendt](../../eras/05-20th-century/hannah-arendt.md).

**Reply.** On the first objection, Augustine's argument is not the bare inference from desire to object. Premise 5 rests on a separate argument, the ascent from changeable judgements to the unchangeable truth that grounds them (*Conf.* VII.17.23), which the dossier reconstructs as Argument 10. The restless heart reads that conclusion back into experience; it does not prove God on its own. On the second objection, Augustine's reply is IV.9.14: loving the friend *in Thee* is the only way not to lose him, since the friend is then loved as a creature with an eternal destiny and not as a substitute for God. The same book concedes that one may enjoy a person, with a qualification: "when you have joy of a man in God, it is God rather than man that you enjoy" (*On Christian Doctrine* I.33.37). Oliver O'Donovan (*The Problem of Self-Love in St. Augustine*, 1980) notes that Augustine let the use-enjoyment pair drop from his later works. Arendt's point about the world survives these replies in part, and the dispute is still open. [Pascal](../../eras/03-early-modern/blaise-pascal.md) restated the argument from restlessness in the *Pensées*.

**Argument 2. The pear theft: why do wrong for its own sake? (*Conf.* II.4–10)**

*What it proves:* that the will can choose wrong without any outside good drawing it, and so that the root of sin lies in the will's own turning. Augustine uses a trivial crime to isolate this root, because a trivial crime has no motive large enough to hide it.

In his sixteenth year, in Thagaste, Augustine and a gang of friends stripped a neighbour's pear tree at night. He stole "compelled by no hunger, nor poverty, but through a cloyedness of well-doing, and a pamperedness of iniquity" (*Conf.* II.4.9). They took the fruit "not for our eating, but to fling to the very hogs, having only tasted them" (*Conf.* II.4.9).

1. Every ordinary wrong is done for the sake of some lower good: wealth, revenge, pleasure, safety. "So then, not even Catiline himself loved his own villainies, but something else, for whose sake he did them" (*Conf.* II.5.11).
2. The pears were no such good to him. He had better at home and threw them away. "For if aught of those pears came within my mouth, what sweetened it was the sin" (*Conf.* II.6.12).
3. So the object of his love was the wrongness itself: "It was foul, and I loved it; I loved to perish, I loved mine own fault, not that for which I was faulty, but my fault itself" (*Conf.* II.4.9).
4. But the wrongness of an act is not a thing. It is a lack of order, and a lack has no beauty to attract anyone (*Conf.* II.6.12).
5. So the will was not drawn by any real good. It moved itself, and its movement imitated in a twisted way the freedom of God: "I might mimic a maimed liberty by doing with impunity things unpermitted me, a darkened likeness of Thy Omnipotency" (*Conf.* II.6.14).
Therefore the source of the act lay in the will's own turning away from the highest good, and every vice is a bent copy of something only God has. "Thus all pervertedly imitate Thee, who remove far from Thee, and lift themselves up against Thee" (*Conf.* II.6.14).

Then comes a complication: he would never have done it alone. "Alone, I had never committed that theft" (*Conf.* II.9.17). What he enjoyed was partly the company and the joke on the owner. When someone says "let's go, let's do it", he writes, "we are ashamed not to be shameless" (*Conf.* II.9.17). The book ends with a confession of failure: "Who can disentangle that twisted and intricate knottiness?" (*Conf.* II.10.18).

**Textual basis.** *Conf.* II.4.9–II.10.18. The list of vices as counterfeits of divine attributes is II.6.13. The study guide reconstructs a related argument in its section (a).

**Objection.** The analysis contradicts itself, and the contradiction points to a deeper one. Augustine says he loved the theft for its own sake (premise 3) and then finds motives after all: imitating God's freedom, the pleasure of the gang, the joke (II.6.14, II.8.16, II.9.17). If those were the motives, the theft was done for an apparent good like every other crime, and premise 3 is false. If they were not, the will chose nothing at all, which no eudaimonist psychology can describe. The ancient position Augustine inherits, the Socratic claim that no one does wrong willingly, which [Plato](../../eras/01-ancient/plato.md) puts in Socrates' mouth in the *Protagoras* and the *Meno*, says the second option is impossible. Kant's account of radical evil in *Religion within the Boundaries of Mere Reason* (1793) later accepted the first and denied that anyone wills evil as evil. See [Immanuel Kant](../../eras/03-early-modern/immanuel-kant.md).

**Reply.** Augustine's own text supplies the reply, and the literature has drawn it out. Scott MacDonald ("Petit Larceny, the Beginning of All Sin", 2003) argues that the two halves fit. Augustine never says the will chose nothing. He says it chose no real good and loved a shadow of one, the shadow of God's unrestricted freedom (II.6.14). That is consistent with his rule that every choice aims at something with the look of good, and it locates the fault exactly: the theft aims at a good that belongs to God alone and so can only be had in a counterfeit form. The companions do not remove this. They show that pride, the wish to be answerable to no one, is easily social. Premise 3 should be read as *for no good the pears could give*, which the text supports. Against the Socratic view, the case is built so that ignorance cannot explain it: he knew theft was wrong and did not want the fruit. Argument 9 develops the same view of the evil will in the *City of God*.

**Argument 3. Against Manichaean materialism: God and evil need not be bodies (*Conf.* V.10, VII.1–5)**

*What it proves:* that the Manichaean picture of God and evil as two opposed bodies is incoherent, and that the mind has evidence within itself of a reality that is not spread out in space. This clears the ground for the privation theory in Argument 4.

Augustine was a Manichaean hearer for about nine years, from Carthage to Rome (*Conf.* III.6.10–III.11.20, IV.1.1). He says his whole error came from one habit of thought: "when I wished to think on my God, I knew not what to think of, but a mass of bodies (for what was not such did not seem to me to be anything)" (*Conf.* V.10.19). Evil had to be a body too, so "I conceived two masses, contrary to one another, both unbounded, but the evil narrower, the good more expansive" (*Conf.* V.10.20).

Book VII gives two arguments against the picture. The first is Nebridius's dilemma, which Augustine says had silenced his circle in Carthage years before (*Conf.* VII.2.3).

1. On the Manichaean myth, God fought the "nation of darkness" and lost part of his own substance, the souls of the light, to it.
2. Ask what the darkness could have done to God had he refused to fight: "That said nation of darkness, which the Manichees are wont to set as an opposing mass over against Thee, what could it have done unto Thee, hadst Thou refused to fight with it?" (*Conf.* VII.2.3).
3. If it could have harmed him, God is corruptible, and a corruptible thing is not the supreme good.
4. If it could not have harmed him, he had no reason to fight, and the myth of a captured divine substance is pointless.
Therefore the Manichaean God is either not God or the myth is false.

The second argument works on Augustine's own thinking about God.

1. Whatever can be corrupted is worse than what cannot: "that which may be corrupted must be inferior to that which cannot" (*Conf.* VII.1.1). God, the best, is incorruptible.
2. A God spread through space would have more of himself where there is more space, so that "the body of an elephant should contain more of Thee, than that of a sparrow" (*Conf.* VII.1.2). A being divided into larger and smaller portions is changeable and divisible, so not incorruptible.
3. Augustine already had evidence that something real is not spatial. The mind forms images of bodies, but the act of forming them is not itself an image: "nor yet did I see that this same notion of the mind, whereby I formed those very images, was not of this sort, and yet it could not have formed them, had not itself been some great thing" (*Conf.* VII.1.2).
Therefore God need not, and cannot, be a body, and the question "whence is evil?" (*Conf.* VII.5.7) can be asked without assuming that evil is a body either.

Augustine says that he grasped the third premise fully only after he read "certain books of the Platonists" (*Conf.* VII.9.13), Latin versions made by Marius Victorinus (*Conf.* VIII.2.3), in Milan in 386 (*Conf.* VII.10.16). Which books they were is disputed: Paul Henry argued for Plotinus, Willy Theiler for Porphyry, and most readers since (O'Donnell among them) accept some of both. See [Plotinus](../../eras/01-ancient/plotinus.md) and [the later Neoplatonists](../../eras/01-ancient/late-neoplatonists.md).

**Textual basis.** *Conf.* III.7.12 (the Manichaean questions); V.10.19–20; VII.1.1–VII.5.7; VII.10.16 (the incorporeal light). The disappointment with the Manichaean bishop Faustus is V.3.3–V.7.13. The case continues in the debate with Fortunatus (392) and *Against Faustus* (c. 400).

**Objection.** The Manichees had a reply to Nebridius: the myth is a story about how a good God protects the light without becoming the author of darkness, and its point is moral. Evil is real and opposed to God, and Catholic monism makes God responsible for it. That objection is taken up in Argument 4. The deeper objection is to premise 3 of the second argument. [Thomas Hobbes](../../eras/03-early-modern/thomas-hobbes.md) held that "incorporeal substance" is a contradiction, words that signify nothing (*Leviathan*, 1651, chs. 4 and 34). On this view Augustine has not found a non-spatial reality; he has found that he cannot picture his own picturing, which shows only the limits of imagination.

**Reply.** The Nebridius dilemma does not depend on incorporeal being at all. It uses only the premise, which the Manichees accepted, that God is the supreme good and so cannot be harmed. That half of the case stands even if Hobbes is right. For the second half, the literature's reply is that Augustine's point is narrower than a proof of immaterial substance. He claims that the power to judge bodies by standards of better and worse is not itself measured by size or place (*Conf.* VII.17.23), so being real cannot mean being spatial. He later argues that the mind knows itself with a certainty it has about no body (*On the Trinity* X.10.13–16), an argument that Andreas Colvius (1640) and Antoine Arnauld (Fourth Objections, 1641) pointed out to [Descartes](../../eras/03-early-modern/rene-descartes.md), and which the dossier takes up in Argument 8. Whether that self-knowledge shows the mind to be a non-bodily thing is the question Descartes and Hobbes argued over in the Third Objections (1641); see [the Descartes dossier](../rene-descartes/README.md).

**Argument 4. Evil as privation of good (*Conf.* VII.12–16; *Enchiridion* 10–14; *City of God* XI.9, XII.3)**

*What it proves:* that evil is not a substance or a nature but a lack of good in something that is good, so that a good God can have made everything that exists without making evil.

1. Whatever exists was made by God, and God, who is supremely good, makes only good things (*Enchiridion* 10; *Conf.* VII.12.18).
2. Some things can be corrupted. Corruption harms a thing, and harm is a loss of good: "all which is corrupted is deprived of good" (*Conf.* VII.12.18).
3. If a thing lost all its good, it would cease to exist, since a thing that kept existing beyond all corruption would be incorruptible and so better than before, which is absurd (*Conf.* VII.12.18).
4. So whatever exists is good: "So long therefore as they are, they are good: therefore whatsoever is, is good" (*Conf.* VII.12.18).
5. Evil, then, cannot be a substance: "That evil then which I sought, whence it is, is not any substance: for were it a substance, it should be good" (*Conf.* VII.12.18).
Therefore evil is a privation, the absence of a good that should be present. "For what is that which we call evil but the absence of good?" (*Enchiridion* 11).

The *Enchiridion*, a handbook Augustine wrote around 421, gives the medical analogy. In a body "the wound or disease is not a substance, but a defect in the fleshly substance", and when health returns the disease does not go somewhere else; it ceases (*Enchiridion* 11). So "there can be no evil where there is no good" (*Enchiridion* 13), and an evil man is, strictly, "an evil good" (*Enchiridion* 13). The *City of God* says the same in one line: "evil has no positive nature; but the loss of good has received the name" of evil (*City of God* XI.9). Vice depends on the good it damages: "For it is impossible that there should be a harmless vice" (*City of God* XII.3), and so "Things solely good, therefore, can in some circumstances exist; things solely evil, never" (*City of God* XII.3).

Two further claims complete the view. Moral evil, the evil of a will, is also a privation. Augustine enquired "what iniquity was, and found it to be no substance, but the perversion of the will, turned aside from Thee, O God, the Supreme, towards these lower things" (*Conf.* VII.16.22). And the whole universe contains no evil when it is seen entire: "And to Thee is nothing whatsoever evil" (*Conf.* VII.13.19), because what clashes with one part fits another (VII.13.19, VII.16.22).

**Textual basis.** *Conf.* VII.12.18–VII.16.22; *Enchiridion* 10–14; *City of God* XI.9, XI.17, XII.3. The Latin label *privatio boni* comes from the tradition; Augustine himself speaks of *privatio* and of things deprived of good. Plotinus had called matter evil as absolute lack (*Enneads* I.8); Augustine refuses to call any nature, matter included, evil, which is the main difference between them. See [Plotinus](../../eras/01-ancient/plotinus.md).

**Objection.** There are two kinds of objection. The first is phenomenological. Pain and a torturer's delight feel as positive as pleasure, so calling them lacks redescribes evil instead of explaining it. The second is the problem of evil itself, which the privation theory does not touch. J. L. Mackie ("Evil and Omnipotence", *Mind*, 1955) argued that an omnipotent, wholly good God would eliminate evil as far as he could, so the existence of evil is inconsistent with such a God. An omnipotent God could fill any lack. William Rowe ("The Problem of Evil and Some Varieties of Atheism", 1979) restated the case as evidence. Pointless suffering, such as a fawn burning slowly to death in a forest fire, makes such a God improbable.

**Reply.** Augustine does not offer the privation theory as a complete answer to the problem of evil, and the literature (G. R. Evans, *Augustine on Evil*, 1982) stresses this. The theory answers the Manichaean question, whether God must be the author of an evil substance. The answer to Mackie's question, why God permits the lack, comes from two other doctrines: that evil wills are self-caused (Argument 9), and that God permits evil only because he "can bring good even out of evil" (*Enchiridion* 11). Alvin Plantinga's free will defence (*God, Freedom, and Evil*, 1974) is the modern descendant of the first. On the phenomenological objection, the standard reply from [Aquinas](../../eras/02-medieval/thomas-aquinas.md) onward is that privation is a claim about what kind of being evil has, not about how it feels. Pain is a real experience, and its badness consists in the damage or threat to a good it signals. A torturer's delight is a real act of a real will, and its evil consists in what the act lacks: due order toward the victim and toward God. Whether this saves the theory from Rowe's evidential argument is a separate question, and most philosophers of religion today think it does not by itself. [Leibniz](../../eras/03-early-modern/gottfried-wilhelm-leibniz.md) took over the aesthetic half of the view in the *Theodicy* (1710).

**Argument 5. The divided will (*Conf.* VIII.5, 9–10)**

*What it proves:* that a person can will something and fail to will it in the same act, that this division lies inside one self and is not a conflict between two souls, and that it comes from habit formed by earlier free choices.

In Milan in 386 Augustine was intellectually convinced and still could not change his life. Simplicianus told him how the famous rhetorician Victorinus had confessed his faith in public (*Conf.* VIII.2.3–5). Augustine wanted to do the same and could not: he was "bound as I was, not with another's irons, but by my own iron will" (*Conf.* VIII.5.10). He describes the mechanism as a chain: "For of a forward will, was a lust made; and a lust served, became custom; and custom not resisted, became necessity" (*Conf.* VIII.5.10). His adolescent prayer had been "Give me chastity and continency, only not yet" (*Conf.* VIII.7.17).

1. The mind commands the body and is obeyed at once. "The mind commands the body, and it obeys instantly; the mind commands itself, and is resisted" (*Conf.* VIII.9.21).
2. In the body's case, willing and being able are separate. In the will's own case "the ability was one with the will, and to will was to do" (*Conf.* VIII.8.20). So a will that commands itself and is not obeyed must not be commanding wholly.
3. A will that wills wholly needs no command, since it already is what it commands (*Conf.* VIII.9.21).
4. So the resistance is a will that wills only in part: "It is therefore no monstrousness partly to will, partly to nill, but a disease of the mind, that it doth not wholly rise, by truth upborne, borne down by custom" (*Conf.* VIII.9.21).
Therefore "And therefore are there two wills, for that one of them is not entire: and what the one lacketh, the other hath" (*Conf.* VIII.9.21), and both belong to a single self: "it was I who willed, I who nilled, I, I myself" (*Conf.* VIII.10.22).

Against the Manichees, who read deliberation as a contest between a good soul and an evil one, he adds a reductio. A man may hesitate between killing "by poison or by the sword" (*Conf.* VIII.10.24), both bad, or between reading the Apostle and singing a Psalm, both good. If each will needed its own soul, there would be as many souls as options, which the Manichees themselves deny. So "one soul fluctuates between contrary wills" (*Conf.* VIII.10.23).

The new will was "not yet able to overcome my former wilfulness, strengthened by age. Thus did my two wills, one new, and the other old, one carnal, the other spiritual, struggle within me; and by their discord, undid my soul" (*Conf.* VIII.5.10). The struggle ends only in the garden at Milan (*Conf.* VIII.12.29), which Augustine presents as a gift of grace and not as a final act of effort. Whether the garden scene happened as told is a separate dispute: Pierre Courcelle argued (1950) that it is largely literary, built from Scripture and Ponticianus's story; James O'Donnell's commentary (1992) and most recent readers hold that the scene is shaped by its telling but not invented.

**Textual basis.** *Conf.* VIII.5.10–12; VIII.7.17; VIII.8.19–20; VIII.9.21; VIII.10.22–24; VIII.11.25–27; Romans 7, which Augustine quotes at VIII.5.11.

**Objection.** The strongest objection is moral and was pressed in Augustine's lifetime by Pelagius and, in the 420s, by Julian of Eclanum. If custom has become necessity, the person cannot do otherwise, and someone who cannot do otherwise is not responsible. Julian argued that this either destroys responsibility or makes God unjust for punishing what cannot be helped. A second, conceptual objection comes from Gilbert Ryle (*The Concept of Mind*, 1949). Talk of the will commanding itself treats volitions as acts that need further acts to start them, which produces a regress. If willing to will requires another willing, nothing is ever willed. See [Gilbert Ryle](../../eras/05-20th-century/gilbert-ryle.md).

**Reply.** Augustine's reply to the moral objection is in the same chapter. The necessity is of his own making: "it was through me that custom had obtained this power of warring against me, because I had come willingly, whither I willed not" (*Conf.* VIII.5.11). Responsibility passes back to the choices that forged the chain, as a drunk answers for what he does drunk. He adds a second, theological layer at VIII.10.22: the division is "the punishment of a sin more freely committed, in that I was a son of Adam". Julian rejected that layer, and the dossier takes up the dispute in Argument 12. To Ryle, the literature's reply (James Wetzel, *Augustine and the Limits of Virtue*, 1992) is that Augustine's talk of commands is a figure for a will that is not unified, and that no regress arises because a whole will acts without a prior command (premise 3). Harry Frankfurt's account of first- and second-order desires ("Freedom of the Will and the Concept of a Person", 1971) gives a modern analogue: the addict who wants not to want his drug, and whose second-order desire does not move him, is Augustine's case.

**Argument 6. Memory and the search for God (*Conf.* X.8–27)**

*What it proves:* that the mind contains far more than it can survey, that seeking anything requires already knowing it in some way, and that God, whom the mind seeks, is found through memory and yet is not contained in it.

Book X turns from the past to the present: what Augustine is now, as he writes (*Conf.* X.3.4). He looks for God first in the world, which answers that it is not God (X.6.9), then in the soul, and comes to memory: "the fields and spacious palaces of my memory, where are the treasures of innumerable images" (*Conf.* X.8.12).

1. Memory holds images of things sensed, but also things that are not images: the truths of number and the liberal arts, which he did not learn through the senses and recognised as already in him when taught (*Conf.* X.9.16–X.12.19), and his own past feelings, recalled without being felt again (*Conf.* X.14.21–22).
2. Memory is larger than the mind can grasp. "Great is this force of memory, excessive great, O my God; a large and boundless chamber!" and "nor do I myself comprehend all that I am" (*Conf.* X.8.15).
3. To seek something is to remember it in part. "For the woman that had lost her groat, and sought it with a light; unless she had remembered it, she had never found it" (*Conf.* X.18.27). Without memory she could not tell when she had found it.
4. Everyone seeks a happy life. So everyone must know it in some way, though no one has seen it with bodily eyes: "Truly we have it, how, I know not" (*Conf.* X.20.29).
5. The happy life is "joy in the truth" (*Conf.* X.23.33), so everyone seeking happiness already knows something of the truth.
Therefore the search for God begins inside memory, but it does not end there. God was not in memory before Augustine learned of him: "Where then did I find Thee, that I might learn Thee, but in Thee above me?" (*Conf.* X.26.37).

The section closes with the most quoted lines of the book: "Too late loved I Thee, O Thou Beauty of ancient days, yet ever new!" and "Thou wert with me, but I was not with Thee" (*Conf.* X.27.38). The search has been inward and the finding has come from above. Augustine also notes a puzzle on the way. He can remember forgetfulness, yet "what is forgetfulness, but the privation of memory?" (*Conf.* X.16.24). He leaves it unsolved.

**Textual basis.** *Conf.* X.6.8–X.27.38. Petrarch says he opened the book on the summit of Mont Ventoux at X.8.15, where men "go abroad to admire the heights of mountains" and pass themselves by (letter dated 1336, probably written later).

**Objection.** Premises 3 to 5 repeat the paradox of inquiry in [Plato](../../eras/01-ancient/plato.md)'s *Meno*: one cannot seek what one does not know, and need not seek what one does. Plato's answer was recollection of a life before birth. Augustine wavers on the same answer at X.20.29, asking whether we were happy once "in that man who first sinned". The standard objection, which goes back to [Aristotle](../../eras/01-ancient/aristotle.md) (*Posterior Analytics* I.1), is that the paradox trades on two senses of knowing. We can know something under a description, or know a mark by which we would recognise it, without knowing it fully. The woman with the lost coin needs only to know what her coin looked like. Someone seeking happiness needs only the word and a rough idea of what it would be like to have it, which experience of small joys supplies. Nothing requires a hidden store of knowledge of God.

**Reply.** Augustine grants more of this than the objection supposes. He says plainly that the happy life is known "how, I know not" (X.20.29) and that God is not stored in memory at all (X.26.37). His conclusion is weaker than Plato's. Memory supplies a standard, the love of truth and the refusal to be deceived (X.23.33), against which the seeker can test candidates for happiness and find them wanting. That is a description in Aristotle's sense, and Augustine agrees it is enough to start the search. What he denies is that the description can be filled from experience of creatures, because every creature can be lost (Argument 1). Phillip Cary (*Augustine's Invention of the Inner Self*, 2000) reads Book X as the origin of the idea of the self as an inner space. [Heidegger](../../eras/05-20th-century/martin-heidegger.md) lectured on Book X in 1921 ("Augustine and Neoplatonism") and took from it the view that the self is a question to itself, after Augustine's "I became a great riddle to myself" (*Conf.* IV.4.9).

**Argument 7. What is time? The present of things past, present and future, and *distentio animi* (*Conf.* XI.14–28)**

*What it proves:* that time cannot be a property of things outside the mind alone, since the past and the future do not exist and the present has no length, and that what we measure when we measure time is a stretching of the mind across memory, attention and expectation.

Book XI starts from Genesis and a question pagans put to Christians: what was God doing before he made heaven and earth? (*Conf.* XI.10.12). Augustine answers that there was no before, since time was made with the world (XI.13.15–XI.14.17). Then he asks what time is, and the second question takes over the book. "What then is time? If no one asks me, I know: if I wish to explain it to one that asketh, I know not" (*Conf.* XI.14.17).

1. The past no longer exists and the future does not yet exist (*Conf.* XI.14.17).
2. The present, to be time and not eternity, must pass into the past, so "we cannot truly say that time is, but because it is tending not to be" (*Conf.* XI.14.17). And any stretch of present can be divided until no part of it has length (*Conf.* XI.15.20).
3. Yet we measure time. We say one syllable is twice as long as another (*Conf.* XI.16.21, XI.26.33).
4. We cannot measure what does not exist or what has no length. So we do not measure past, present or future as they are in things.
5. We do measure something that exists now: the impression that passing things leave in the mind. "It is in thee, my mind, that I measure times" (*Conf.* XI.27.36).
6. The mind holds the three times in three acts. "For it expects, it considers, it remembers" (*Conf.* XI.28.37). Speaking strictly there are "a present of things past, a present of things present, and a present of things future", that is, "present of things past, memory; present of things present, sight; present of things future, expectation" (*Conf.* XI.20.26).
Therefore time is a stretching of the mind, and Augustine says so with a question: "time is nothing else than protraction; but of what, I know not; and I marvel, if it be not of the mind itself?" (*Conf.* XI.26.33).

Pusey translates the Latin *distentio* as "protraction" in XI.26.33 and as "distraction" in XI.29.39: "my life is but a distraction". The double sense matters. *Distentio* is the mind's stretch across times, and it is also the scattering of a life among them, which Augustine contrasts with the gathered attention he hopes for in God (XI.29.39). The recited Psalm is his model. "I am about to repeat a Psalm that I know" (*Conf.* XI.28.38). Before he starts, expectation covers the whole; as he recites, it passes through attention into memory. "The same holds in the whole life of man, whereof all the actions of man are parts" (*Conf.* XI.28.38).

**Textual basis.** *Conf.* XI.10.12–XI.13.16 (no time before creation); XI.14.17–XI.28.38 (the analysis); XI.29.39–XI.31.41 (eternity and the gathered self). The study guide's section (c) gives a shorter reconstruction.

**Objection.** Two objections are standard. The first concerns measurement. If what I measure is an impression in memory, I need a fixed unit to measure it by, and memory impressions are not fixed: a dull hour remembered can seem short and a minute of pain long. To compare two impressions, I must already be measuring the time each took, which is the thing to be explained. Bertrand Russell (*A History of Western Philosophy*, 1945) praised the theory as an advance on Greek thought and still judged it subjectivist for this reason. See [Bertrand Russell](../../eras/05-20th-century/bertrand-russell.md). The second objection is Wittgenstein's. In *The Blue Book* (dictated 1933–34) and *Philosophical Investigations* §89–90 he cited XI.14.17 as a model philosophical puzzle. The puzzle arises because we picture measuring time as measuring a length laid out before us. On this view the right response is to describe how we actually use the word *time*, which raises no puzzle, and the theory of *distentio* is an answer to a question built on a bad picture. See [the Wittgenstein dossier](../ludwig-wittgenstein/README.md) and [later Wittgenstein](../../eras/05-20th-century/wittgenstein-late.md).

**Reply.** Augustine does not deny that bodies move or that events have an order outside the mind. He grants that the heavens' motion can mark days (XI.23.29–30) and rejects only the claim that time is that motion, since a potter's wheel would still turn in time if the stars stopped. His thesis concerns what measurement measures, and here the literature (Paul Ricoeur, *Time and Narrative*, vol. 1, 1983; see [Paul Ricoeur](../../eras/05-20th-century/paul-ricoeur.md)) treats the regress as a real result. Lived time cannot be reduced to clock time, because even reading a clock needs a mind that holds the earlier reading while it takes the later one. [Husserl](../../eras/05-20th-century/edmund-husserl.md) opened his lectures on inner time-consciousness (1905) with Augustine for this reason. Against Wittgenstein, the reply is partial. Augustine himself starts from use: we say "a long time" and understand each other (XI.15.18). He agrees that ordinary speech is improper but says he will not quarrel with it (XI.20.26). What he adds, and Wittgenstein's therapy does not address, is the question of how a self that lives across times holds together at all (XI.29.39). That question passes into the doctrine of divine eternity that [Boethius](../../eras/02-medieval/boethius.md) refined, and into Argument 11 on foreknowledge.

**Argument 8. *Si fallor, sum*: certainty against the sceptics (*City of God* XI.26; *On the Trinity* X.10.14; *Against the Academicians* in summary)**

*What it proves:* that some knowledge is proof against the Academic sceptic. I know that I exist, that I live, that I know these things, and that I love them. No hypothesis of error can touch this knowledge, because being in error already requires the existence it would cast in doubt. In *On the Trinity* the same certainty becomes a premise for a further claim: the mind is not a body.

The opponents are the sceptics of the later Platonic Academy, Arcesilaus and Carneades, whom Augustine knew through Cicero's *Academica*. They held that the wise man assents to nothing, because any impression we have could be matched by a false one we could not tell apart from it. Augustine was drawn to the view in Rome and Milan (*Conf.* V.10.19, V.14.25). His first work after his conversion, *Against the Academicians* (386), answers it with truths that survive the sceptic's test: disjunctions such as *there is one world or there is not*, the truths of mathematics, and reports of how things appear to me (*Against the Academicians* III.10.23–11.26, paraphrase). The argument from error comes later and goes deeper.

1. The Academic objects to any claim to knowledge: "What if you are deceived?" (*City of God* XI.26).
2. "For he who is not, cannot be deceived" (XI.26).
3. So "if I am deceived, by this same token I am" (XI.26).
4. "And since I am if I am deceived, how am I deceived in believing that I am?" (XI.26).
5. The same holds of my knowing that I know, and of my loving these two things: "neither am I deceived in this, that I love" (XI.26).
Therefore "I am most certain that I am, and that I know and delight in this," and in these truths "I am not at all afraid of the arguments of the Academicians" (XI.26).

*On the Trinity* X widens the list. People have disputed what the mind is made of, whether air, fire, brain, blood or atoms. "Yet who ever doubts that he himself lives, and remembers, and understands, and wills, and thinks, and knows, and judges? Seeing that even if he doubts, he lives; if he doubts, he remembers why he doubts; if he doubts, he understands that he doubts" (*On the Trinity* X.10.14). Doubt is itself one of the acts the doubter cannot doubt. Two sections later Augustine draws a conclusion about the mind's nature. The mind is certain of itself, "although it is not at all certain whether itself is air, or fire, or some body, or some function of body. Therefore it is not any of these" (X.10.16). Book XV answers the dream argument the same way. Whoever knows he lives does not say he knows he is awake, but "I know I am alive; therefore, whether he be asleep or awake, he is alive" (XV.12.21).

**Textual basis.** *City of God* XI.26, where the triad of being, knowing and loving is read as an image of the Trinity; *On the Trinity* X.10.13–16, XV.12.21; *Against the Academicians* III.10.23–11.26; earlier forms at *Soliloquies* II.1.1 and *On Free Choice* II.3.7, where Augustine asks Evodius whether he exists and notes that he could not be deceived if he did not (both paraphrase only). The *Confessions* applies the same certainty to the will: "I knew as well that I had a will, as that I lived" (*Conf.* VII.3.5).

**Objection.** There are three. First, the self is unearned. Lichtenberg's notebooks objected to Descartes that we should say *it thinks*, as we say *it lightens*. Bertrand Russell (*A History of Western Philosophy*, 1945) argued that experience gives us thoughts, and that the self who has them is a further assumption. Augustine's premise 3 makes the same move, from the fact of deception to the existence of someone deceived. Second, the result is too thin. Carneades attacked knowledge of the world through the senses, and certainty that I exist tells me nothing about the world. Third, the inference in *On the Trinity* X.10.16 looks invalid. Antoine Arnauld made the point against Descartes in the Fourth Objections (1641). That I can doubt whether I am a body, while I cannot doubt that I think, shows something about my knowledge and nothing about what I am. A man can be sure his father exists and unsure whether a masked stranger does, though the stranger is his father. See [Pyrrho and the sceptics](../../eras/01-ancient/pyrrho-of-elis-and-the-skeptics.md), [Cicero](../../eras/01-ancient/cicero-and-lucretius.md) and [Bertrand Russell](../../eras/05-20th-century/bertrand-russell.md).

**Reply.** To the first, the literature's. Augustine starts from error, and a mistake is someone's mistake. Gareth Matthews (*Thought's Ego in Augustine and Descartes*, 1992) argued that this first-person stance is the real innovation in both writers, and that *it thinks* leaves the act of doubting without a doubter. Whether that answers Russell or begs the question against him is disputed. To the second, Augustine's own. He never made the self the foundation of all knowledge. In *On the Trinity* XV.12.21 he goes on to accept much that we learn from the senses and from testimony. The certainty from error is a counterexample. The Academics said nothing can be known, and one case of knowledge refutes that. To the third, Augustine has a premise that Arnauld's case lacks: "when the mind knows itself, it knows its own substance" (X.10.16). If that premise holds, the masked-man pattern does not apply. The objection then falls on that premise, which Augustine supports only by the mind's presence to itself, "for nothing is more present to it than itself" (X.10.16). Descartes, shown the *City of God* passage in 1640, said Augustine had used the point for a different end (see [the Descartes dossier](../rene-descartes/README.md) and [René Descartes](../../eras/03-early-modern/rene-descartes.md)).

**Argument 9. Free will and the origin of evil (*On Free Choice* I; *City of God* XII.6–7)**

*What it proves:* that God is not the author of the evil people do. Moral evil comes from the free will of a created agent, which turns from the eternal good to lesser goods. The turning has no efficient cause outside the will, and Augustine argues that it can have none. It is a defect, and a defect has only a "deficient" cause.

Argument 4 showed that evil is no substance. That leaves the question of where the falling away comes from, if everything God made is good. *On Free Choice* opens with it: Evodius asks whether God is the author of evil (I.1.1, paraphrase). Augustine separates the evil a person does from the evil a person suffers. God causes the second, as just penalty, and does not cause the first (I.1.1). The rest of Book I looks for the source of evil-doing.

1. God is the just cause of the evils people suffer as penalty; he is not the cause of the evils they do (*On Free Choice* I.1.1).
2. Evil-doing consists in inordinate desire, the love of things that can be lost against one's will (I.3.8–I.4.10).
3. Nothing can make a mind ruled by reason the slave of desire. A virtuous mind, equal or higher, would not do it, being just; anything lower lacks the power (I.10.20–I.11.21).
4. So only the mind's own will can do it. Evil-doing is the will's turning from eternal things toward temporal ones (I.16.35).
5. This turning is a lack of due order, not a thing, so it has no efficient cause: "Let no one, therefore, look for an efficient cause of the evil will; for it is not efficient, but deficient, as the will itself is not an effecting of something, but a defect" (*City of God* XII.7).
Therefore moral evil originates in the free will of creatures, and God is not its author.

*City of God* XII.6 gives the regress behind premise 5. "The bad will is the cause of the bad action, but nothing is the efficient cause of the bad will" (XII.6). Suppose something caused it. A good will would not make another will bad. A bad will only moves the question back: "what made the _first_ evil will bad?" (XII.6). A thing without a will is a created nature, so it is good, and good cannot be the efficient cause of evil. The will goes wrong by its own turning: "when the will abandons what is above itself, and turns to what is lower, it becomes evil--not because that is evil to which it turns, but because the turning itself is wicked" (XII.6). Augustine then imagines two men, alike in body and soul, who see the same beautiful body. One consents to lust and the other does not. Nothing in their natures explains the difference; the one "is willing, the other unwilling, to fall away from chastity" (XII.6). To look further for a cause "is as if some one sought to see darkness, or hear silence" (XII.7). Because the defection is the will's own, "its failings are justly punished, being not necessary, but voluntary" (XII.8).

**Textual basis.** *On Free Choice* I.1.1, I.3.6–I.4.10, I.10.20–I.11.21, I.16.34–35; II.1.1–3 on why God gave a will that can sin; II.19.50–II.20.54 on free will as an intermediate good (all paraphrase only). *City of God* XII.6–8. In the *Confessions* Augustine recalls hearing "that free-will was the cause of our doing ill, and Thy just judgment of our suffering ill" without yet understanding it (*Conf.* VII.3.5).

**Objection.** J. L. Mackie ("Evil and Omnipotence," *Mind*, 1955) gave the strongest form. If one person can freely choose the good on one occasion, all persons can freely choose it on every occasion. An omnipotent and wholly good God could have made such persons. That he did not counts against his goodness or his power, so the appeal to free will fails. A second objection comes from inside Augustine's account. If nothing in the two men explains why one falls, the fall looks like chance, and chance is hard to blame anyone for. Scott MacDonald ("Primal Sin," in G. B. Matthews, ed., *The Augustinian Tradition*, 1999) and William Babcock (*Journal of Religious Ethics*, 1988) argued that Augustine's account threatens to make the first sin unintelligible.

**Reply.** To Mackie, the literature's. Alvin Plantinga's free will defence (*God, Freedom, and Evil*, 1974) granted that a world of creatures who always choose rightly is possible. He argued that God cannot bring about a free creature's choices, since then they would not be free. It is possible that every creature God could make would go wrong at least once. If so, Mackie has shown no contradiction. Augustine's own reply concerns the value of the will. A will that can turn away is still better than a nature that cannot turn at all, as a horse that strays is better than a stone that cannot (*On Free Choice* III.5.15, paraphrase). To the second objection, Augustine would say that the defection does not happen to the agent; it is the agent's act, and he can give no further cause because there is none. He says in XII.7 that this is the one thing we know only by not knowing it. MacDonald takes this as an honest limit. Others read it as a refusal to explain where explanation is owed. The question is still open.

**Argument 10. The proof of God from truth (*On Free Choice* II.3–15)**

*What it proves:* that God exists. There is something higher than the human mind, eternal and unchangeable, by which the mind judges. Augustine and Evodius agree in advance to call such a thing God, and Augustine argues that unchangeable truth is such a thing.

Book II begins with faith. Evodius believes that God exists, and Augustine proposes that they come to understand what he believes (*On Free Choice* II.2.5–6). The proof below is all paraphrase.

1. You exist, you live and you understand. Each is certain, and each is better than the one before, since what understands also lives and exists (II.3.7).
2. The outer senses perceive bodies. An inner sense perceives that we sense. Reason judges both, and what judges is higher than what it judges (II.3.8–II.6.13).
3. If we find something higher than reason, eternal and unchangeable, we will call it God. If there is something higher still, that will be God. It is enough to show that something is above reason (II.6.14).
4. The laws of number are unchangeable, and each person sees them by reason as true for all. The senses, which reach only changing bodies, cannot give them (II.8.20–24).
5. The rules of wisdom are of the same kind: for example, that the eternal is better than the temporal. They too are unchanging and common to all who reason (II.9.25–II.10.29).
6. This truth belongs to no one mind. All minds judge by it, and none judges it; a mind that judged truth would be above it (II.12.33–34).
7. So truth is higher than our minds (II.13.35–II.14.38).
Therefore God exists: either truth itself is God, or there is something higher than truth, and that is God (II.15.39).

Augustine identifies this truth with Wisdom, the Word (II.15.39). The proof starts from the mind's grasp of necessary truths, which the senses cannot explain, and not from motion or causes. The *Confessions* describes the same ascent, ending when the mind finds "the unchangeable and true Eternity of Truth above my changeable mind" (*Conf.* VII.17.23). Anselm's measure of God as that than which nothing greater can be thought has its ancestor in premise 3. Leibniz later argued that eternal truths need an eternal understanding in which they exist (*Monadology*, 1714, §§43–44). See [Anselm](../../eras/02-medieval/anselm-of-canterbury.md), [Leibniz](../../eras/03-early-modern/gottfried-wilhelm-leibniz.md) and, for the Forms behind the argument, [the Plato dossier](../plato/README.md).

**Textual basis.** *On Free Choice* II.2.5–II.15.39 (paraphrase only); *Conf.* VII.10.16, VII.17.23; *On the Trinity* XII.15.24 on the mind's seeing intelligible truth in a light from God, the doctrine usually called illumination.

**Objection.** Christopher Kirwan (*Augustine*, 1989) pressed the gap between premise 7 and the conclusion. Grant that there are unchanging truths no single mind owns. It does not follow that they are anything like a god. Frege held that numbers and logical laws exist, never change and are grasped by all minds alike, and he took them to be abstract objects with no mind, will or goodness. The phrase "higher than reason" also equivocates. A truth is higher in that reason must conform to it, which is a normative relation and need not make the truth a greater being. A second objection denies premise 6. Wittgenstein's later remarks on mathematics treat necessary truths as rules of our practice, which hold because of how we use signs (*Remarks on the Foundations of Mathematics*, written 1937–44). On that view there is nothing above the mind to find. See [later Wittgenstein](../../eras/05-20th-century/wittgenstein-late.md).

**Reply.** To the first, partly Augustine's own. His conclusion is a dilemma: truth is God, or something above truth is. He needs only that something stands above reason, which premise 3 already counts as God. He also denies that this truth is inert. The rules of wisdom direct the will as well as the intellect, since they say what ought to be preferred (II.10.28–29). Something unchanging, common to all, and the measure of right love has several marks of the God Augustine worships. Whether these marks add up to a person who creates and loves is the open question, and Augustine fills the gap from Scripture. To the second, the literature's. Mathematical realists since Frege argue that conventionalism cannot explain why the rules bind us or why they apply to the world. The dispute is live, and the proof stands or falls with it.

**Argument 11. Foreknowledge and freedom (*On Free Choice* III.2–4; *City of God* V.9–10)**

*What it proves:* that God's infallible foreknowledge of our acts is consistent with our doing them freely. Foreknowledge does not cause what it foreknows. The wills God foreknows are among the causes of what happens. The only necessity foreknowledge brings is one that leaves the will intact.

Evodius raises the worry in *On Free Choice* III.2.4. God foreknew that Adam would sin, and what God foreknows must happen, so Adam sinned of necessity (paraphrase). Augustine answers that to foreknow a will is to foreknow something in our power (III.3.6–8). Foreknowledge compels no more than memory does. You do not make the past happen by remembering it, and God does not make the future happen by knowing it (III.4.9–11, paraphrase).

*City of God* V.9 restates the problem as Cicero's. Cicero argued that foreknowledge implies a fixed order of causes, hence fate, hence no free will, and he gave up foreknowledge. Augustine says Cicero left "this alternative, to make choice between two things, either that something is in our own power, or that there is foreknowledge,--both of which cannot be true" (V.9). The religious mind "chooses both, confesses both, and maintains both by the faith of piety" (V.9).

1. God foreknows all things infallibly: "One who is not prescient of all future things is not God" (V.9).
2. Foreknowledge implies an order of causes certain to God (V.9, granting Cicero's step).
3. "Our wills themselves are included in that order of causes which is certain to God, and is embraced by His foreknowledge, for human wills are also causes of human actions" (V.9).
4. The necessity that destroys freedom brings something about whether we will it or not, as death does. Willing is not under that necessity, since "if we will, it _is_; if we will not, it _is_ not" (V.10).
5. Another necessity is harmless. To say "that it is necessary that, when we will, we will by free choice" is true, and it does not "subject our wills thereby to a necessity which destroys liberty" (V.10).
Therefore foreknowledge and free will are consistent: "A man does not therefore sin because God foreknew that he would sin" (V.10).

**Textual basis.** *On Free Choice* III.2.4–III.4.11 (paraphrase only); *City of God* V.9–11. Cicero's view is in *On Divination* II and *On Fate*; V.9 is Augustine's reconstruction of it. See [the Stoics](../../eras/01-ancient/zeno-of-citium-and-the-stoics.md), whose doctrine of fate Cicero was resisting.

**Objection.** Augustine answers the claim that foreknowledge causes our acts. The stronger argument makes no such claim. Nelson Pike ("Divine Omniscience and Voluntary Action," *Philosophical Review*, 1965) set it out. Suppose God believed eighty years ago that Jones would mow his lawn on Saturday. God's beliefs cannot be false, and the past cannot now be changed. So on Saturday Jones cannot refrain, since refraining would require that God had held a false belief or had never held it. William Rowe (*Review of Metaphysics*, 1964) argued that Augustine's reply turns on a scope ambiguity. *Necessarily, if God foreknows A, A will happen* is true. *If God foreknows A, A will necessarily happen* is the claim in dispute. The memory analogy fails for the same reason: the remembered past is fixed, and that is what the objector says about God's past belief. A last objection is that the freedom Augustine defends is too weak. In V.10 an act is free if it happens because we will it, which allows that our willing was settled in advance.

**Reply.** The literature's, since Augustine does not answer the fixed-past form. There are three main replies. [Boethius](../../eras/02-medieval/boethius.md) (*Consolation of Philosophy* V, c. 524) denied that God's knowledge lies in the past. God is eternal and sees all times in one present, as we see what happens before us without forcing it. Aquinas took this view. [Ockham](../../eras/02-medieval/william-of-ockham.md) (*Treatise on Predestination*) kept God in time and denied that past facts about God's knowledge of the future are fixed as ordinary past facts are; Alvin Plantinga revived the view in "On Ockham's Way Out" (*Faith and Philosophy*, 1986). The third reply accepts the last objection and defends a compatibilist Augustine, for whom freedom is the will acting as itself and does not require the ability to do otherwise. John Rist (*Journal of Theological Studies*, 1969) and James Wetzel (*Augustine and the Limits of Virtue*, 1992) read the later Augustine this way. Eleonore Stump ("Augustine on Free Will," in *The Cambridge Companion to Augustine*, 2001) dissents in part. Which reading fits V.10 is disputed.

**Argument 12. Grace and the will: the case against Pelagius (*On Nature and Grace*; *On Grace and Free Will*; *To Simplician* I.2 in summary)**

*What it proves:* that no human being, as human nature now is, can will and do the good without God's grace. Grace is a free gift that comes before any merit, including the first movement of faith. It heals the will and does not abolish free choice.

The view took shape before Pelagius. In *To Simplician* I.2 (c. 396), Augustine read Romans 9 on Jacob and Esau and concluded that even the beginning of faith is God's gift (paraphrase). Thirty years later he wrote that he had thought otherwise until then, and "that it was chiefly by this apostolic testimony that I myself had been convinced" (*On the Predestination of the Saints* 4.8). The testimony was 1 Corinthians 4:7, "what have you which you have not received?" The *Confessions* turns the doctrine into a prayer: "Give what Thou enjoinest, and enjoin what Thou wilt" (*Conf.* X.29.40). Augustine later reported that Pelagius objected sharply when he heard these words quoted in Rome (*On the Gift of Perseverance* 20.53, paraphrase). The controversy ran from 411 to Augustine's death. Pelagius was cleared by a synod at Diospolis in 415 and condemned by African councils and Pope Zosimus in 418. Julian of Eclanum carried on the argument until 430.

1. Human nature was created sound, but the nature everyone inherits from Adam "now wants the Physician, because it is not sound" (*On Nature and Grace* 3.3).
2. The damaged will can still choose. It cannot of itself love the good it sees, as Augustine's own will could not in Milan (*Conf.* VIII.5.10–12; Argument 5).
3. Whatever good a person has, that person received (1 Cor. 4:7; *On the Predestination of the Saints* 4.8).
4. So grace "is not rendered for any merits, but is given gratis, on account of which it is also called grace" (*On Nature and Grace* 4.4). When God rewards good works, "It is His own gifts that God crowns, not your merits" (*On Grace and Free Will* 6.15).
5. Grace works on the will from within: "He operates, therefore, without us, in order that we may will; but when we will, and so will that we may act, He co-operates with us" (*On Grace and Free Will* 17.33).
6. This leaves choice in place: "free will is not made void through grace, but is established, since grace cures the will whereby righteousness is freely loved" (*On the Spirit and the Letter* 30.52).
Therefore no one wills the good without grace, and the grace that makes the will good is unearned.

Pelagius granted that we need God, and he called our natural ability grace. Augustine quotes him: "The actual capacity of not sinning lies not so much in the power of will as in the necessity of nature" (*On Nature and Grace* 51.59). Since God made nature, the capacity is God's gift. Augustine's complaint is that this makes grace the same as creation and leaves no room for the healing that premise 1 says nature needs.

**Textual basis.** *On Nature and Grace* 3.3–5.5, 51.59 (415); *On the Spirit and the Letter* 30.52 (412); *On Grace and Free Will* 2.2, 6.15, 16.32, 17.33 (426–427); *On the Predestination of the Saints* 3.7–4.8 (428–429); *To Simplician* I.2 (paraphrase only). Pelagius's own case is best read in his *Letter to Demetrias* (413).

**Objection.** Pelagius's objection is the oldest and still the strongest. God commands us to be perfect, and a just God does not command the impossible, so what God commands we can do. If we cannot obey without a grace given to some and withheld from others, the commands are cruel and moral effort is pointless (*Letter to Demetrias*, paraphrase). Julian of Eclanum added three charges. Guilt cannot be inherited, since sin is an act of will and infants have willed nothing. A God who condemns unbaptized infants for Adam's sin is unjust. A nature evil from birth is the Manichaean doctrine Augustine claimed to have left. Augustine accepted the hard conclusion: "The entire mass, therefore, incurs penalty and if the deserved punishment of condemnation were rendered to all, it would without doubt be righteously rendered" (*On Nature and Grace* 5.5). Kant restated Pelagius's point: if we ought to become better, we must be able to (*Religion within the Boundaries of Mere Reason*, 1793). See [Immanuel Kant](../../eras/03-early-modern/immanuel-kant.md).

**Reply.** Augustine's own, from several works. Commands are not idle. They show us what we lack and send us to ask for it, and the will that obeys is really ours (*On Grace and Free Will* 2.2, 16.32). He separates the will's choice (*liberum arbitrium*), which everyone keeps, from freedom in the full sense (*libertas*), the healed will's delight in the good. Grace restores the second and works through the first (*On the Spirit and the Letter* 30.52). On infants and inherited guilt he gave no reply his critics accepted. He held that all are justly condemned in Adam, that mercy to some wrongs no one, and that the reasons for God's choice are hidden. Scholars dispute how far the late doctrine breaks with the early Augustine. Peter Brown (*Augustine of Hippo*, 1967; epilogue to the 2000 edition) places a turn in the years of *To Simplician*. Kurt Flasch (*Logik des Schreckens*, 1990) reads that work as a radical break. Carol Harrison (*Rethinking Augustine's Early Theology*, 2006) argues that the main elements are present from 386. The Second Council of Orange (529) affirmed prevenient grace and rejected predestination to evil. The dispute returned with Luther, Calvin, the Jansenists and [Pascal](../../eras/03-early-modern/blaise-pascal.md).

**Argument 13. Two loves, two cities, and kingdoms without justice (*City of God* IV.4, XIV.28, XIX.21, 24)**

*What it proves:* that no earthly state embodies true justice, so none can claim the full title Roman thought gave the republic. A state is still a real people with real, limited goods, defined by what its members love together. History is the story of two loves, formed into two cities that stay mixed until the end. The pilgrim city uses the earthly city's peace and obeys its laws without identifying with it.

The setting is the sack of Rome by Alaric's Goths in August 410 and the pagan charge that abandoning the old gods had caused it. Augustine wrote the *City of God* from 413 to 426. The preface to Book I names the theme: the earthly city, "though it be mistress of the nations, is itself ruled by its lust of rule" (*City of God* I, Preface). The Latin is *libido dominandi*.

1. Cicero, through Scipio in *On the Republic*, defined a people as "an assemblage associated by a common acknowledgment of right and by a community of interests," and a republic as the weal of the people (*City of God* XIX.21).
2. Right flows from justice: "Where, therefore, there is no true justice there can be no right" (XIX.21).
3. Justice gives each his due. A people that withholds itself from the true God and worships demons does not give God his due (XIX.21).
4. So, by Cicero's definition, "there never was a Roman republic" (XIX.21).
5. A kingdom without justice differs from a robber band only in size and impunity: "Justice being taken away, then, what are kingdoms but great robberies? For what are robberies themselves, but little kingdoms?" (IV.4).
6. A better definition is available: a people is "an assemblage of reasonable beings bound together by a common agreement as to the objects of their love" (XIX.24). On it Rome is a people, and a people is better "in proportion as it is bound together by higher interests" (XIX.24).
7. There are two basic loves: "Two cities have been formed by two loves: the earthly by the love of self, even to the contempt of God; the heavenly by the love of God, even to the contempt of self" (XIV.28).
Therefore every earthly state is a real people with real goods, ranked by its loves, and none has true justice. Citizens of the heavenly city live within such states as pilgrims.

IV.4 ends with Cicero's story of a pirate brought before Alexander the Great. Asked what he meant by seizing the sea, the pirate answered: "What thou meanest by seizing the whole earth; but because I do it with a petty ship, I am called a robber, whilst thou who dost it with a great fleet art styled emperor" (IV.4). The two cities are not church and state. The visible church holds citizens of both, and so does Rome. The earthly city seeks an earthly peace, the ordered concord of its citizens. The heavenly city, "or rather the part of it which sojourns on earth and lives by faith, makes use of this peace only because it must," and it "makes no scruple to obey the laws of the earthly city" in what concerns this life (XIX.17).

**Textual basis.** *City of God* I, Preface; IV.4; XIV.28; XIX.13 (peace as the tranquillity of order), XIX.15 (slavery), XIX.17, XIX.21, XIX.24, XIX.26. Book III of Cicero's *On the Republic*, which held the definition, survives only in fragments, many of them preserved by Augustine.

**Objection.** First, the argument proves too much or too little. If true justice requires true worship, every state outside the church is a great robbery. That points either to contempt for all political authority or to the view that only a Christian state is legitimate. Augustine moved toward the second. From about 405 he supported imperial laws coercing the Donatists, and he defended the policy in Letter 93 (c. 408) (paraphrase). Second, the argument makes injustice easier to accept. Augustine treated slavery as a result of sin: "The prime cause, then, of slavery is sin, which brings man under the dominion of his fellow" (XIX.15). He told slaves to serve faithfully (XIX.15). If no earthly order is just, little seems worth reforming. Third, Hannah Arendt (*The Human Condition*, 1958) argued that the Christian bond of charity, which she traced to Augustine, joins people while treating the world as a place to pass through, so it cannot found a public realm. See [Hannah Arendt](../../eras/05-20th-century/hannah-arendt.md).

**Reply.** To the first, Augustine's own. The second definition (XIX.24) blocks the slide into calling every state a robber band, since peoples are ranked by what they love together. The peace a state secures is a real good, which Christians share and pray for (XIX.17, XIX.26). On the Donatists there is no good reply in Augustine, and his sympathetic interpreters say so. To the second and third, the literature's, which is divided. Robert Markus (*Saeculum*, 1970) read Augustine as opening a secular space, neither holy nor demonic, that Christians and others can share on limited terms. Rowan Williams ("Politics and the Soul," *Milltown Studies*, 1987) and John Milbank (*Theology and Social Theory*, 1990) rejected this reading. For them the earthly city is defined by the lust of rule, and the real alternative is the church's own common life. All three agree that Augustine lowered the claims of the state and called no empire the kingdom of God. Compare the definition Augustine rejects in XIX.21, that right is what is useful to the stronger party, which Thrasymachus defends in [Plato](../../eras/01-ancient/plato.md)'s *Republic* I.

**Argument 14. Just war (*City of God* XIX.7; *Against Faustus* XXII.74–75)**

*What it proves:* that Christians can wage war justly. The conditions are a just cause, which is the other party's wrong; lawful authority; and a right disposition, aiming at peace and free of hatred and cruelty. Even a just war is a misery to be lamented.

Augustine wrote no treatise on war. The doctrine comes from passages written for other ends. *Against Faustus* (c. 400) defends the wars of the Old Testament against the Manichaean bishop Faustus, who cited them as proof that the Old Testament God was evil. *City of God* XIX catalogues the miseries of social life. Letters to Roman officials, above all Letter 189 to the general Boniface (c. 418), give pastoral advice (paraphrase).

1. Every war aims at peace: "it is obvious that peace is the end sought for by war. For every man seeks peace by waging war, but no man seeks war by making peace" (*City of God* XIX.12).
2. The evil in war is not chiefly death, which comes to all: "The real evils in war are love of violence, revengeful cruelty, fierce and implacable enmity, wild resistance, and the lust of power, and such like" (*Against Faustus* XXII.74).
3. Good men may make war to punish such evils, when force is needed, "in obedience to God or some lawful authority" (XXII.74).
4. "A great deal depends on the causes for which men undertake wars, and on the authority they have for doing so" (XXII.75). The ruler decides on war, and soldiers serve "in behalf of the peace and safety of the community" (XXII.75).
5. The just cause is the enemy's wrong: "it is the wrong-doing of the opposing party which compels the wise man to wage just wars" (*City of God* XIX.7).
6. Christ's command not to resist evil concerns "not a bodily action, but an inward disposition" (*Against Faustus* XXII.76).
Therefore a war waged under lawful authority, to answer wrong and restore peace, and fought without hatred or cruelty, can be just.

Augustine does not celebrate war. The wise man fights, but he will "all the rather lament the necessity of just wars, if he remembers that he is a man" (XIX.7). Whoever thinks of these evils without pain is in "a more miserable plight still, for he thinks himself happy because he has lost human feeling" (XIX.7). He also limits private violence. In *On Free Choice* I.5.11–13 he grants that the law may let a traveller kill a robber in self-defence, but doubts that the killer is free of sin, since he kills for things he could lose against his will (paraphrase). Soldiers and magistrates act for the public; private persons do not. *Against Faustus* XXII.75 adds that a righteous soldier under an ungodly king may obey an order to fight, because "his position makes obedience a duty."

**Textual basis.** *City of God* I.21 (killing by public authority or divine command is not murder), XIX.7, XIX.12–13; *Against Faustus* XXII.74–76; *On Free Choice* I.5.11–13 and Letters 138 and 189 (paraphrase only). Gratian's *Decretum* (c. 1140) collected these texts, and Aquinas built his three conditions of authority, just cause and right intention on them (*Summa theologiae* II-II q.40 a.1). See [Thomas Aquinas](../../eras/02-medieval/thomas-aquinas.md) and, for the later law of nations, [Suárez and Grotius](../../eras/03-early-modern/suarez-and-grotius.md).

**Objection.** First, the earlier Christian tradition. Tertullian (*On Idolatry* 19) and Origen (*Against Celsus* VIII.73) held that Christians should not fight, and the Sermon on the Mount says to turn the other cheek (paraphrase). Augustine's reply in XXII.76 moves the command from acts to dispositions. Robert Holmes (*On War and Morality*, 1989) argued that this lets any killing count as loving, so long as the killer's heart is right, and so cuts the tie between what a person does and what that person intends. Second, the conditions are too loose. A war "undertaken in obedience to God" is righteous by definition (XXII.75), which licenses holy war for anyone who claims God's command. The soldier's duty to obey even an unjust order anticipates the plea of superior orders that modern law rejects.

**Reply.** The literature's, built on Augustine's lament. R. A. Markus ("Saint Augustine's Views on the 'Just War'," *Studies in Church History* 20, 1983) argued that Augustine offered no theory of just war. He offered an account of how a Christian may act where coercion cannot be avoided, and he called even justified war a misery. On that reading the right disposition does not make killing good; it forbids the hatred and cruelty Augustine names as war's real evils (XXII.74). Paul Ramsey (*War and the Christian Conscience*, 1961) grounded the doctrine in love for a neighbour under attack, which can require force within limits set by the same love. On the second objection, the divine-command clause in *Against Faustus* concerns the wars of the Old Testament, where Augustine took the command to be given in Scripture, and he did not apply it to wars of his own day. The obedience clause covers orders whose injustice is not plain (XXII.75), and it speaks to the soldier's guilt, not to the war's justice. Jean Bethke Elshtain (*Augustine and the Limits of Politics*, 1995) argued that the lament of XIX.7 is the centre of the doctrine and works as a restraint on war. Whether a few occasional texts can bear the tradition later built on them is disputed.
