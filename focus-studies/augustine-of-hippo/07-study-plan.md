# Study Plan — Augustine in Seven Weeks

## What this plan is, and where it plugs in

The [learning path](../../learning-path.md) meets Augustine at the opening of **Stage 2 (Medieval & Renaissance Bridge)**, as the stage's first core figure (●) at row 1, before Boethius (row 2) and Anselm (row 3). Its block is *Confessions* I–VIII and XI, the last for time. Its editions are Boulding (New City Press) or Sheed (Hackett, with Foley's notes). It adds *On Free Choice of the Will* in selection "if the stage is going well", and it gives one warning: do not start with the *City of God*, which it allows only as Book XIX in selection, and later. This dossier keeps those editions and that order and widens the block to seven weeks. The *Confessions* fill Weeks 1–4, with Books IX and X added, because the vision at Ostia and the analysis of memory are what Book XI's account of time builds on. *On Free Choice* becomes a core text in Week 5, beside the short arguments on certainty from the *City of God* and *On the Trinity*, the *Enchiridion* on evil, and the Pelagian works in selection. The *City of God* comes last, in Weeks 6–7.

One ruling needs stating plainly. The path names only Book XIX of the *City of God*. This plan reads selections from Books I, IV, V, XI, XII, XIV and XV in Week 6 before Book XIX in Week 7, because Book XIX's definitions of peace and of a people rest on the two loves of XIV.28 and the robber kingdoms of IV.4. That is this dossier's widening of the path's rule, not the path's wording. The rule's point survives: you meet the *City of God* only after the whole *Confessions* and the arguments of Week 5, never first. [02-works-and-texts.md](02-works-and-texts.md), "The works in the dossier's reading order," gives the reasons week by week.

Run it as one block in place of Stage 2's Augustine row. Week 5's foreknowledge argument leads straight into Boethius at row 2, and Week 5's proof from truth into Anselm at row 3.

**Total cost, stated honestly: ≈42 hours all-in across seven weeks: 30 hours at the desk, ≈5.6 hours of assigned media, and ≈6.5 hours of the daily [Anki](https://apps.ankiweb.net) [FSRS](https://docs.ankiweb.net/deck-options.html) queue (about 8 minutes a day for 49 days, 392 minutes).** The table rounds each week's 56 minutes of cards to 0.9 hours, so its columns sum to 30 + 5.6 + 6.3 = 41.9. With the cards unrounded (6.53 hours) and the media in exact minutes (338 minutes, 5.63 hours), the total is 42.2. Weekly totals run from 4.8 to 6.5 hours. Optional items are not counted. Every running time comes from [08-bibliography.md](08-bibliography.md), which measured each one.

**The accounting against the path, stated plainly: these hours sit on top of Stage 2's budget (8 weeks, 36 desk hours, ≈62 all-in), they are not hidden inside it, and Stage 2 is the path's compressed stage.** The path says of it: do not skip it, and do not expand it. It fixes no week count for the Augustine row. Its 12-month trim (§10) keeps Augustine, Anselm and Aquinas in primary text inside 24 desk hours, about 8 each, so this dossier estimates the row at about 8 desk hours, roughly two weeks at the stage's rate of 4.5 a week; that is this dossier's estimate, not the path's figure. Half the media is already in the path. Adamson's Augustine block, eps. 110–116, is numbered in the late-antique run that Stage 1 draws on, but the path's Stage 2 "Listen & watch" hears it at Augustine's own row, inside that stage's curated ≈18 hours. Those seven episodes (168 minutes, 2.8 hours) are therefore not new cost. Eps. 88, 90, 109 and 117 (92 minutes) lie in Stage 1's range, in the Plotinus and ancient-Christianity tail that Stage 1 selects from; the path does not list which of its episodes it takes, so if you heard them there, this plan's hearing is a second one, and it is counted here. *Philosophize This!* ep. 16 and the *In Our Time* programme (78 minutes) are new. Take away the row's desk time, the 2.8 media hours already in Stage 2, and the row's share of the card queue (about 2 hours), and the net addition to Stage 2 is roughly 29 hours, again an estimate. Stage 2 would cost about 91 hours all-in instead of ≈62.

The weeks matter as much as the hours. The path's stage discipline (§1.4) lets you extend a stage by at most two weeks. Seven weeks in place of a two-week row extends Stage 2 by about five, which breaks that rule. Choose deliberately. Either run the whole plan in the comfortable version (Calendar B, §10), where extra weeks are expected, or run Weeks 1–4, the *Confessions* (17 desk hours, ≈24.5 all-in by the table), as the row, which keeps the extension near two weeks, and take Weeks 5–7 (13 desk hours, ≈17.4 all-in) in buffer weeks after Stage 2's gate. The plan splits cleanly there: Weeks 1–4 read one book, Weeks 5–7 the treatises.

**Reading texts.** Follow the path and [08-bibliography.md](08-bibliography.md), section 1. The *Confessions* in Boulding (New City Press) or in Sheed (Hackett, ed. Foley): pick one and do not alternate. The *Enchiridion*, *On Christian Doctrine*, *Against Faustus* and the Pelagian works in the New City Press volumes or, for selections this short, the Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers texts at [New Advent](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/). The dossier quotes Pusey's *Confessions* ([Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/3296)) and Dods's *City of God* because they are public domain and checkable; use them to check a reference, not as a reading copy, since their English is archaic and their Latin pre-critical.

**Cite by book, chapter and section, and budget by section.** Every edition above prints the numbers. *Conf.* VIII.12.29 is Book VIII, chapter 12, section 29; *City of God* XIX.13 is Book XIX, chapter 13; *On the Trinity* X.10.14 is Book X, chapter 10, section 14; the *Enchiridion* goes by chapter alone; the Pelagian works by chapter and section (*On the Predestination of the Saints* 4.8). Editions paginate differently, so each week's target is given as books and section ranges plus a session length. [02-works-and-texts.md](02-works-and-texts.md), "A note on citation," explains the system.

**The guide is your companion, by design.** The library's [study guide to the *Confessions*](../../study/augustine-confessions.md), with its book-by-book entries, four reconstructions and eighteen checkpoints with answers, is not duplicated here. Weeks 1–4 assign its book entries, reconstructions (a)–(d), checkpoints and misreadings by number; the checkpoints printed in those weeks are the dossier's own additions. The guide covers Books I–VIII and XI and notes Books IX and X briefly, so this plan supplies those two books itself. The guide cites book and chapter (VIII.12); this plan adds the section (VIII.12.29).

## Week 1 — The restless heart: *Confessions* I–IV (~6.2 hrs: 4 desk + 1.3 media + 0.9 cards)

**Primary.** *Confessions* I–IV, about three hours across Sessions A and B. Book I (I.1.1–20.31): the opening prayer and the restless heart (I.1.1), how God can be called into the self (I.2.2–5.6), infancy (I.6.7–7.12), how a child learns words (I.8.13), and school, Dido and the beatings (I.9.14–19.30). Read I.1.1 and I.8.13 twice; the second is the passage Wittgenstein quotes to open the *Investigations*. Book II (II.1.1–10.18): the pear theft (II.4.9) and its analysis (II.5.10–10.18), the plan's first argument about the will. Read II.4.9–8.16 slowly. Book III (III.1.1–12.21): Carthage and the theatre (III.1.1–2.4), Cicero's *Hortensius* (III.4.7–8), Scripture's style (III.5.9), the Manichees (III.6.10–10.18) and Monica's dream (III.11.19–20). Book IV (IV.1.1–16.31): the friend's death and the grief that follows (IV.4.7–9.14), love of mortal things and love in God (IV.10.15–12.19), *The Beautiful and the Fitting* (IV.13.20–15.27), and Aristotle's *Categories* (IV.16.28–31), which you may skim. Then *On Christian Doctrine* I.3.3–5.5, two pages, on use and enjoyment: the distinction Book IV puts to work and Argument 1's objection turns on.

**The guide.** Its Book I–IV entries; its reconstruction **(a)**, the pear theft, written book-closed before you open it; its **checkpoints 1–5**; its misreadings 1, 3 and 4.

**Alongside.** [01-life-and-times.md](01-life-and-times.md), from "Thagaste: Patricius and Monica, 354–366" through "Teaching at Thagaste and Carthage, 375–383," with "The partner and Adeodatus"; [02-works-and-texts.md](02-works-and-texts.md), "A note on citation" and the *Confessions* entry; [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md), "Faith seeking understanding" (its opening), "Love and the order of love" for use and enjoyment, and "Signs and language"; [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Arguments 1 and 2; [05-conversations.md](05-conversations.md), "Cicero and the Academic sceptics," "Mani and the Manichees" and "Wittgenstein"; [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md), the misconceptions on the *Confessions* as autobiography and on infants. The entries on [Cicero](../../eras/01-ancient/cicero-and-lucretius.md) and [Plato](../../eras/01-ancient/plato.md) as a refresher, the second for the Socratic claim Argument 2 tests. Before the week, Chadwick's *Augustine* (one or two evenings).

**Media.** *Preview, before the reading:* Peter Adamson, *History of Philosophy Without Any Gaps*, ep. 109, ["Spreading the Word - the Latin Church Fathers"](https://hopwag.podbean.com/e/hop-109-spreading-the-word-the-latin-church-fathers-1409207790/) (24 minutes), for Ambrose and the Latin world Augustine entered; and [*Philosophize This!*](https://philosophizethis.libsyn.com/episode-016-saint-augustine), ep. 16, "Saint Augustine" (30 minutes), the path's later spine voice heard once here as an orientation to the life. *Review, after your reconstruction of Argument 2:* Adamson, ep. 111, ["Papa Don't Teach - Augustine on Language"](https://hopwag.podbean.com/e/hop-111-papa-dont-teach-augustine-on-language-1409207790/) (22 minutes), for I.8.13 and *The Teacher*. *Optional, not counted:* ep. 101, ["Father Figures - Introduction to Ancient Christian Philosophy"](https://hopwag.podbean.com/e/hop-101-father-figures-introduction-to-ancient-christian-philosophy-1409207791/).

**Checkpoints (the dossier's own).**
1. *What does the opening prayer claim, and what in Book I is offered as evidence?* That the human heart is made for God and is restless until it rests in him (I.1.1). The evidence is a history of loves that satisfied briefly: the infant's rage at a thwarted will (I.7.11), the schoolboy's love of praise and of Dido's story (I.13.20–22). Then give the logical objection, that a desire does not prove its object exists, and say why Argument 1 does not need that inference. [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 1.
2. *Why is the pear theft the right example, and what is the standard objection to Augustine's analysis?* A theft for gain has an explanation in the gain. Augustine had better pears at home and threw these to the pigs (II.4.9), so the act isolates a will that loves the forbidden. Objection: he then finds motives after all, the gang (II.8.16–9.17) and a shadow of God's freedom (II.6.14), so either he did it for an apparent good or he willed nothing. Reply: MacDonald's, that he chose no real good and loved a counterfeit of one. [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 2.
3. *What did the Manichees offer the nineteen-year-old, and what did they ask him to believe?* A reasoned faith without blind belief, and an account of evil that blamed neither God nor the self: two principles, light and darkness, both bodies, at war (III.6.10–7.12; V.10.18–20). Say what this cosmology does to responsibility, and why Augustine stayed a hearer. [05-conversations.md](05-conversations.md), "Mani and the Manichees."
4. *What does Book IV teach about loving a mortal friend, and how does use and enjoyment state the lesson?* He loved the friend as if he would not die, so the friend's death left him hating every place (IV.4.9–6.11). The cure is to love others in God (IV.12.18). *On Christian Doctrine* I.3.3–4.4 puts it formally: enjoy only what makes you happy, use the rest. Then give Arendt's objection, that this makes the neighbour a means. [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 1; [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md), "Love and the order of love."

**Reconstructions.** The pear theft (Argument 2; guide (a) is the check, not the source), before Adamson ep. 111, and the restless heart from I.1.1 and Book IV (Argument 1), as a first pass that Week 4 completes with X.20–23.

**Cards.** The first 5 cards of the dossier's deck: the argument cards for Arguments 1 and 2, and concept cards on the restless heart (*cor inquietum*), the Manichaean two principles, and use and enjoyment (*uti*/*frui*). Import after the week's reading, never before. If you use the library's shipped decks in [`study/cards/`](../../study/cards/README.md), suspend any Augustine card these duplicate. Deck at 5.

## Week 2 — Milan and the Platonists: *Confessions* V–VII (~6.5 hrs: 4.5 desk + 1.1 media + 0.9 cards)

**Primary.** *Confessions* V–VII, about three and a half hours; Book VII is the hardest of the first nine and the most important for the rest of the plan, so read it twice. Book V (V.1.1–14.25): Faustus, the sect's famous teacher, who cannot answer the questions about the heavens (V.3.3–7.13); the move to Rome (V.8.14–15); the Manichaean picture of God and evil as two masses (V.10.18–20) and the pull of the Academic sceptics (V.10.19); Milan, Ambrose's preaching and the decision to doubt everything (V.13.23–14.25). Book VI (VI.1.1–16.26): Ambrose reading silently (VI.3.3–4); belief on authority, since most of what we know we take on trust (VI.5.7–8); the drunken beggar (VI.6.9–10); Alypius (VI.7.11–10.17); career, marriage and the partner sent away (VI.11.18–15.25). Book VII (VII.1.1–21.27): the struggle to think of a substance that is not a body (VII.1.1–2.3), the search for evil's source (VII.3.4–5.7), astrology refuted (VII.6.8–10), the "books of the Platonists" and what they did and did not contain (VII.9.13–15), the first ascent (VII.10.16), evil as privation (VII.11.17–16.22), the second ascent through the grades of judgement (VII.17.23), and what the Platonists lacked (VII.18.24–21.27).

If you heard eps. 88 and 90 in Stage 1, this is their review. *Optional, not counted:* *In Our Time*, ["Neoplatonism"](https://www.bbc.co.uk/programmes/b01g62w1) (19 April 2012, 42 minutes).

**Checkpoints (the dossier's own).**
1. *Why did Faustus matter more than a lost debate would have?* He was the teacher the Manichees promised would answer every question, and he admitted he could not explain the heavens, which the astronomers predicted better (V.3.3–7.13). The failure was the system's. Then say why Augustine turned to the Academic sceptics next (V.10.19, V.14.25), and what that phase left him with.
2. *State the argument against Manichaean materialism, with Nebridius's dilemma and Hobbes's objection.* If God is a body, he is divisible and can be diminished, which a supreme good cannot be (VII.1.1–2). Nebridius: if the darkness could harm God, he is not incorruptible; if it could not, there was no reason for the war (VII.2.3). The Platonists then supplied an incorporeal, unchanging being (VII.10.16). Hobbes: incorporeal substance is a contradiction in terms. Reply: the dilemma needs only the Manichees' own premise that God cannot be harmed. [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 3.
3. *State the privation theory, what it answers, and what it does not.* Whatever exists is good, as made by the good God; corruption harms only what is good, and a thing deprived of all good would cease to be; so evil is no substance but a lack of good in a good thing (VII.12.18–13.19). It answers the Manichaean question, whether God made an evil substance. It does not answer Mackie's, why an omnipotent God permits the lack, or Rowe's fawn. For that Augustine needs free will (Week 5). This is the path's Stage 2 exit checkpoint on evil; answer it aloud. [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 4.
4. *What did the books of the Platonists give Augustine, and what did they lack?* An incorporeal God, the Word through whom all things were made, and a method: turn inward, then upward (VII.9.13–10.16). They lacked the Word made flesh, and with it humility (VII.9.14, VII.18.24–20.26). The second ascent, through the grades of judgement to the unchanging truth above the mind (VII.17.23), returns as Argument 10. [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md), "The Platonists and their limits."

**Reconstructions.** Against Manichaean materialism (Argument 3; guide (b) is the check) and evil as privation (Argument 4), before Adamson ep. 90. Arguments 1 and 2 get their +2-day and +1-week touches.

**Cards.** +5: the argument cards for Arguments 3 and 4, and concept cards on evil as privation (*privatio boni*), the books of the Platonists and the ascent, and faith seeking understanding (*crede ut intelligas*), whose seed is VI.5.7. Suspend the shipped core card on evil as privation if you imported it. Deck at 10.

## Week 3 — The will and the garden: *Confessions* VIII–IX (~5.7 hrs: 4 desk + 0.8 media + 0.9 cards)

**Primary.** *Confessions* VIII–IX, about three hours. Book VIII (VIII.1.1–12.30), the hinge of the work. Simplicianus tells the story of Victorinus's public profession at Rome (VIII.2.3–5). Augustine names his two wills, the old carnal one and the new spiritual one, and the chain of habit (VIII.5.10–12). Ponticianus tells of Antony and of two officials at Trier who left their careers on reading his life (VIII.6.13–15). The prayer for continence, but not yet (VIII.7.16–18). Then the analysis: the mind commands the hand and is obeyed, commands itself and is resisted, because it does not will wholly (VIII.8.19–9.21), and the answer to the Manichaean reading of two wills as two natures (VIII.10.22–24). Continence appears (VIII.11.25–27), and the garden ends the book (VIII.12.28–30). Read VIII.8.19–10.24 twice. Book IX (IX.1.1–13.37): leaving the chair of rhetoric (IX.2.2–4), Cassiciacum and Psalm 4 (IX.4.7–12), baptism at Easter 387 with Alypius and Adeodatus (IX.6.14), Monica's life (IX.8.17–9.22), the vision at Ostia (IX.10.23–26), and her death and his grief (IX.11.27–13.37). Then *Conf.* XIII.9.10, one paragraph, where love is the weight that carries the soul to its place.

**The guide.** Its Book VIII entry and its note on Book IX; its reconstruction **(d)**, the soul's restlessness and God as interior, written book-closed as a check on Books I–VIII before you open it; its **checkpoints 11–13**; its misreadings 6 and 7.

**Alongside.** [01-life-and-times.md](01-life-and-times.md), "The garden, August 386" through "Rome and the return to Thagaste, 387–391," and "Weighing the sources"; [02-works-and-texts.md](02-works-and-texts.md), "The Cassiciacum dialogues (386–387)"; [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md), "The will" and "The weight of love"; [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 5; [05-conversations.md](05-conversations.md), "Paul" and "Ambrose, Simplicianus and Victorinus"; [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md), live debate (1) and the myths on "only not yet" and the child's game. The entry on [Gilbert Ryle](../../eras/05-20th-century/gilbert-ryle.md) for Argument 5's second objection. **Media.** *Review, at the end of the week:* BBC Radio 4, *In Our Time*, ["Augustine's Confessions"](https://www.bbc.co.uk/programmes/b09tyzvz) (15 March 2018, 48 minutes, with Kate Cooper, Morwenna Ludlow and Martin Palmer), after your reconstruction of Argument 5 and before live debate (1), as a review of Weeks 1–3.

**Checkpoints (the dossier's own).**
1. *What is the divided will, and why can Augustine command his hand but not his wanting?* When the mind commands the body, it is obeyed at once; when it commands itself to will, it is resisted, because it does not command wholly, and what it does not command wholly is not done (VIII.9.21). There are not two natures, as the Manichees said, but one soul torn between two incomplete wills (VIII.10.22–24). Then give Julian of Eclanum's objection, that a will bound by necessity is not responsible, and Augustine's reply from VIII.5.11, that he forged the chain himself. [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 5.
2. *Give Ryle's objection to the will commanding itself, and the best answer.* If willing needs a prior act of willing, a regress follows and nothing is ever willed. The answer the literature gives is that Augustine describes a conflict between desires already present, not a series of volitions; check it against VIII.9.21 and Wetzel's reading. [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 5.
3. *Did the garden scene happen as told, and what turns on it?* The text reports a child's voice, a book opened at Romans 13:13–14, and Alypius following (VIII.12.29–30). Courcelle (1950) read the fig tree, the chance reading copied from Antony and the voice as literary devices, while granting a real conversion; O'Donnell treats the narrative as a constructed argument whose surface facts cannot be checked; O'Meara and, broadly, Brown hold that Augustine described a remembered event, since Alypius was alive to read it. Most scholars now accept a decisive event in 386 whose telling is shaped. Then say why Augustine presents the event as the healing of a will rather than a change of belief (guide checkpoint 13). 1. *State the argument from deception, its target, and Arnauld's objection to its use in On the Trinity X.* The Academic sceptic says nothing can be known; but if I am deceived, I exist, since one who does not exist cannot be deceived (*City of God* XI.26). *On the Trinity* X.10.14 extends the list: whoever doubts lives, remembers, understands and wills; and X.10.16 infers that the mind is none of the things it can doubt it is. 2. *Where does evil come from, on Book I of* On Free Choice*, and what are Mackie's objection and Plantinga's reply?* Evil acts come from lust, the love of things one can lose against one's will, and the will turns to them freely, since nothing forces a mind (I.10.20–11.22, I.16.34–35). Free will is a good, and a good God may give it though it can be misused (II.1.1–3). Mackie: God could have made beings who always freely choose the good. Plantinga: God cannot bring about a free creature's choices, so a world without sin may not have been his to make. 3. *Run the proof from truth, and give Kirwan's objection.* Existing, living and understanding form a scale, and reason is highest in us (II.3.7). What is above our reason and unchanging is God, or if anything is higher, that is God (II.15.39). Kirwan: unchanging truths need not be a god, as Frege's numbers are not, and "higher than reason" equivocates between normative and ontological rank. 4. *Does foreknowledge take away freedom, and what does Pike add?* Augustine: God's foreknowing that I will sin does not make me sin, any more than my remembering a past act made it happen; what God foreknows is that I will sin willingly (*On Free Choice* III.3.6–4.11). [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 11.
5. *What was Pelagius's case, and what is Augustine's answer?* A just God commands nothing impossible, so what he commands we can do (*On Nature and Grace* 43.50 reports the principle and answers it). Augustine: the commands show us what we lack and send us to ask for it; free choice remains, but freedom to will the good reliably is a gift (*On Grace and Free Will* 15.31–17.33). Julian's charges: guilt cannot be inherited, and condemning infants is unjust. ## Capstone essay (800–1,200 words)

Write in Week 7, after the checkpoints, book closed for the first draft. **The gate relationship, stated exactly:** Stage 2's gate essay asks what reason can prove about God unaided, everything, something or nothing, with Anselm against Aquinas against one of the bridge figures. Anselm and Aquinas come later, at rows 3 and 5, so no prompt below is the gate essay. **Prompt 1 is built to pass the path's Stage 2 exit checkpoint on evil**, the triad, the privation answer and what it does not solve. **Prompt 2 is built for the exit checkpoint on time.** **Prompt 3 is the draft you extend into the gate essay** after Anselm and Aquinas. Each prompt draws on at least three weeks.

1. **Evil: what the privation theory solves and what it leaves (the exit-checkpoint prompt).** State the problem of evil as a triad. Reconstruct evil as privation (Argument 4) and free will as the origin of evil (Argument 9), with the pear theft (Argument 2) as the test case. Say which question each answers, the Manichees' or Mackie's, and judge Plantinga's reply against Rowe's evidential version. Then say whether the evil will with no cause (*City of God* XII.6–7) completes the account or opens a new hole.
2. **Is time in the mind?** Reconstruct Argument 7 from XI.14.17–28.38. Set Russell's charge of subjectivism and Wittgenstein's diagnosis against it, using live debate (6) of [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md). Then connect it to Argument 6: if memory holds the past, what does that do to the restless heart of Argument 1, stretched across time? Defend one reading.
3. **What can reason prove about God from truth? (the gate-essay draft).** Reconstruct the proof from truth (Argument 10) and its source in the ascent of VII.17.23. Judge Kirwan's objection that unchanging truths need not be a god. Say what *si fallor, sum* (Argument 8) contributes and what it does not. Leave marked spaces for Anselm's and Aquinas's routes.
4. **Did Augustine change his mind about the will?** Reconstruct the divided will (Argument 5), free will in *On Free Choice* (Argument 9) and grace against Pelagius (Argument 12). Use live debate (2) to weigh the sharp break at *To Simplician* (Brown's first edition, Flasch) against continuity (Carol Harrison). Was Pelagius right that commands imply ability, and does Augustine's distinction between free choice and freedom answer him?
5. **Can the earthly city be just?** Reconstruct Argument 13 from IV.4, XIV.28 and XIX.21–24, and Argument 14. Is Augustine's politics a realism that leaves a neutral space, as Markus argued, or a refusal of the world that cannot found a public realm, as Arendt argued? Test your answer on slavery (XIX.15) and on the coercion of the Donatists.

## Mastery checklist — you are done when you can…

- □ Explain Augustine on time (*Confessions* XI) in five sentences. *(The path's Stage 2 exit checkpoint, verbatim.)*
- □ State the problem of evil as a triad and give one Augustinian response (evil as privation) — and say what it doesn't solve. *(The path's Stage 2 exit checkpoint, verbatim.)*
- □ State the restless heart, say why it does not rest on an inference from desire to object, and give Arendt's objection from use and enjoyment.
- □ Analyse the pear theft, give the objection that Augustine finds motives after all, and the reply.
- □ Reconstruct the case against Manichaean materialism, with Nebridius's dilemma, and say what the books of the Platonists gave Augustine and what they lacked.
- □ Explain the divided will, give Julian's and Ryle's objections, and set out the dispute over the garden scene with its main names.
- □ Explain how the mind searches memory for God and the happy life, with the *Meno* objection and the reply.
- □ State *si fallor, sum*, its target, and Arnauld's objection, and say how far it anticipates Descartes.
- □ Reconstruct the free-will answer to the origin of evil and the proof from truth, with Mackie's and Kirwan's objections. *(Bridge to Stage 2's Anselm row.)*
- □ State Augustine's answer on foreknowledge, Pike's fixed-past objection, and Boethius's and Ockham's replies. *(Bridge to Stage 2's Boethius row.)*
- □ State Pelagius's case and Augustine's answer, the difference between free choice and freedom, and the debate over whether Augustine changed his mind.
- □ Explain the two loves and the two cities, the two definitions of a people, peace as the tranquillity of order, and the conditions of a just war, with one objection each. *(Bridge to Stage 2's Aquinas and Machiavelli rows.)*
