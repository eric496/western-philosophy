# Augustine: The System and Its Concepts

*Focus study · [Dossier home](README.md) · Library entry: [Augustine of Hippo](../../eras/02-medieval/augustine-of-hippo.md) · Arguments in full: [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md) · Study guide: [*Confessions*](../../study/augustine-confessions.md)*

## Faith seeking understanding

Augustine never wrote a system as Aquinas or Spinoza did. His roughly five million words are mostly sermons, letters, commentaries and polemics, and he changed his mind on important points; the *Retractations* (426–427) reviews and corrects his books one by one. Still, a single structure runs through the work. There is one God who is being, truth and good. Everything else exists by depending on God. Human beings are made to love God and find their rest there. Sin is love turned the wrong way, and grace turns it back. This file sets out that structure doctrine by doctrine, naming the text that states each one best.

The method comes first. Augustine read Isaiah 7:9 in the Old Latin version, taken from the Greek Septuagint, which makes belief a condition of understanding. He made it a rule of inquiry, often compressed as *crede ut intelligas*, believe so that you may understand (*Sermon* 43.7.9; *Tractates on John* 29.6). *Against Faustus* XII.46 cites the verse in the Nicene translation: "Unless ye believe, you shall not understand." *On the Trinity* gives the fullest statement: "Faith seeks, understanding finds; whence the prophet says, Unless ye believe, you shall not understand" (*On the Trinity* XV.2.2).

The rule describes an order. Belief comes first, as a pupil believes a teacher before seeing the proof, and understanding is the goal. Augustine had tried the other order: at nineteen he joined the Manichees because they promised reason without authority, and left when their reasons failed (*Conf.* III.6, V.3–7). Anselm later used *fides quaerens intellectum*, faith seeking understanding, as the first title of the *Proslogion*; see [Anselm](../../eras/02-medieval/anselm-of-canterbury.md). Against the objection that such a method cannot test its starting point, Augustine replies that all inquiry begins with trust in parents, teachers and historians (*Conf.* VI.5.7).

**Texts and quotation.** Quotations from the *Confessions* are from E. B. Pusey's translation (1838), keeping its "Thou" and capitals. *City of God* is quoted in Marcus Dods's translation (1871). Other works are quoted from the Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers translations as printed on newadvent.org. References give book, chapter and section where the work has them. *On Free Choice of the Will*, *The Teacher*, *Against the Academicians* and *Soliloquies* are paraphrased.

## God as being, truth and good

Augustine's God is the one who truly is. In the ascent of *Confessions* VII.10 he hears, as if from far off, the words of Exodus 3:14, "I AM that I AM," and after that, he writes, "I should sooner doubt that I live than that Truth is not" (*Conf.* VII.10.16). Being in the full sense means being unchangeable. Of creatures he says: "they neither altogether are, nor altogether are not, for they are, since they are from Thee, but are not, because they are not what Thou art. For that truly is which remains unchangeably" (*Conf.* VII.11.17).

The same God is truth. *On Free Choice* II argues that the mind finds truths it does not make and cannot change, such as the laws of number and the rule that wisdom should be sought. These truths are common to all minds and stand above any one mind, so they are higher than reason. If nothing is higher than them, they are God; if something is, that is God (*On Free Choice* II.6.14, II.12.33–15.39). Argument 10 in [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md) reconstructs the proof.

And God is the good that makes human beings happy. The *Confessions* opens with the claim the whole book tests: "Thou madest us for Thyself, and our heart is restless, until it repose in Thee" (*Conf.* I.1.1). Book X adds that "a happy life is joy in the truth" (*Conf.* X.23.33). Being, truth and good are three names for one object, so metaphysics, knowledge and ethics are one search.

## The Platonists and their limits

Augustine reached this picture in Milan in 386. As a Manichee he had pictured God as a vast luminous body and evil as a dark substance, and could think of nothing that did not take up space (*Conf.* V.10.19, VII.1.1–2). Then he was given "certain books of the Platonists, translated from Greek into Latin" (*Conf.* VII.9.13). *Confessions* VIII.2.3 names the translator, the rhetorician Marius Victorinus. Which books they were is disputed. Paul Henry argued mainly for Plotinus, Willy Theiler for Porphyry, and most scholars now assume some of both; see [Plotinus](../../eras/01-ancient/plotinus.md) and [the later Neoplatonists](../../eras/01-ancient/late-neoplatonists.md).

The books gave him incorporeal being, the method of turning inward and upward (*Conf.* VII.10.16, VII.17.23), and evil as a lack of being. He found in them, "not indeed in the very words, but to the very same purpose," the opening of John's Gospel: the Word with God, by whom all things were made (*Conf.* VII.9.13).

What he did not find was the Incarnation. "But that the Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us, I read not there" (*Conf.* VII.9.14). The Platonists saw the goal without the way, and their knowledge made them proud. Augustine himself reached a moment of vision and fell back at once, "borne down from Thee by mine own weight" (*Conf.* VII.17.23). Reading the Platonists before Scripture taught him to "distinguish between presumption and confession; between those who saw whither they were to go, yet saw not the way, and the way that leadeth not to behold only but to dwell in the beatific country" (*Conf.* VII.20.26).

His later verdict stayed respectful: "It is evident that none come nearer to us than the Platonists" (*City of God* VIII.5). Christians should "claim it for our own use from those who have unlawful possession of it," as Israel took the Egyptians' gold (*On Christian Doctrine* II.40.60). The limits are firm. Augustine rejects the Platonist eternity of the world, the pre-existence and transmigration of souls, the worship of lower gods, and the claim that the soul can purify itself by philosophy (*City of God* X, XII.21). See [Plato](../../eras/01-ancient/plato.md) and the [Plato dossier](../plato/README.md).

## Creation from nothing and the order of being

The Platonists held that the world flows from the One without a beginning. Augustine holds that God made it freely, with time, from neither himself nor any prior matter. "Thou wert, and nothing was there besides, out of which Thou createdst heaven and earth," and so "out of nothing didst Thou create heaven and earth" (*Conf.* XII.7.7).

Two consequences follow. Every creature depends on God for its existence at every moment (*Literal Meaning of Genesis* IV.12.22, paraphrased). And every creature is mutable: what comes from nothing tends back toward nothing. That mutability is the condition of evil, though not yet evil itself.

Creatures form a hierarchy: things that exist, things that also live, and things that also understand (*On Free Choice* II.3.7). Each bears three marks of God's making from Wisdom 11:20, measure, number and weight (*On the Trinity* XI.11.18). Every level is good. "So long therefore as they are, they are good: therefore whatsoever is, is good" (*Conf.* VII.12.18). In the *Literal Meaning of Genesis* (401–415) God makes all at once and plants "seminal reasons" (*rationes seminales*) that unfold over time (VI.10.17, paraphrased). Calling this an anticipation of evolution is anachronistic, since the kinds are fixed in the seeds.

## Evil as privation

The question that drove Augustine to the Manichees was where evil comes from, and their answer was a second principle. His answer is that evil is no substance. *Confessions* VII.12.18 argues from corruption. Things that can be corrupted are good, since corruption harms them, and harm is a loss of good. If they lost all their good they would cease to exist. So "all which is corrupted is deprived of good," and "That evil then which I sought, whence it is, is not any substance: for were it a substance, it should be good" (*Conf.* VII.12.18).

The *Enchiridion* states the doctrine as a definition. "For what is that which we call evil but the absence of good?" (*Enchiridion* 11). A wound is "not a substance, but a defect in the fleshly substance," and "what are called vices in the soul are nothing but privations of natural good." Later writers call this *privatio boni*, the privation of good; compare *City of God* XI.9.

Moral evil is a privation in the will. Augustine "enquired what iniquity was, and found it to be no substance, but the perversion of the will, turned aside from Thee, O God, the Supreme, towards these lower things" (*Conf.* VII.16.22). The evil will has no cause outside itself. "Let no one, therefore, look for an efficient cause of the evil will; for it is not efficient, but deficient, as the will itself is not an effecting of something, but a defect" (*City of God* XII.7). Seeking that cause is like trying "to see darkness, or hear silence." The objects the evil will chooses are not evil. "For avarice is not a fault inherent in gold, but in the man who inordinately loves gold" (*City of God* XII.8).

The doctrine defends God, who made no evil thing, and places responsibility in the creature's turning. Argument 4 gives the main objection, that pain and cruelty seem positively bad. The pear theft is the hard case (Argument 2). Augustine stole "having no temptation to ill, but the ill itself. It was foul, and I loved it" (*Conf.* II.4.9), which looks like evil chosen for its own sake. His analysis in II.5–10 finds counterfeit goods beneath it: friends' company and a perverse imitation of God's freedom.

## The will

Augustine gives the will a larger place than any earlier philosopher. Hannah Arendt (*The Life of the Mind*, vol. 2, 1978) called him the first philosopher of the will, and Albrecht Dihle (*The Theory of Will in Classical Antiquity*, 1982) credited him with the concept. Michael Frede (*A Free Will*, 2011) traces it to the Stoics; see [the Stoics](../../eras/01-ancient/zeno-of-citium-and-the-stoics.md) and [Arendt](../../eras/05-20th-century/hannah-arendt.md).

The will (*voluntas*) is the soul's power of consent and refusal, and so where praise and blame attach. Nothing can force the mind to serve desire except its own will, and an involuntary sin would be no sin (*On Free Choice* I.11.21, III.1.3, paraphrased). The will also directs attention in perception and is the third term of every trinity Augustine finds in the mind (*On the Trinity* XI.2.2–5, X.11.17).

The will can be divided against itself. In *Confessions* VIII Augustine wants to give up career and sex and cannot want it wholly. "Thus did my two wills, one new, and the other old, one carnal, the other spiritual, struggle within me; and by their discord, undid my soul" (*Conf.* VIII.5.10). To go into the new life "was nothing else but to will to go, but to will resolutely and thoroughly; not to turn and toss, this way and that, a maimed and half-divided will" (*Conf.* VIII.8.19). The mind commands the hand and is obeyed; it commands itself and is resisted (*Conf.* VIII.9.21). The cause is habit. Each yielding makes the next easier, until "a hard bondage held me enthralled" (*Conf.* VIII.5.10). Argument 5 reconstructs the analysis.

## Love and the order of love

The will acts by loving. Love (*amor*) names every movement of the will toward what it wants; love directed to God is *caritas*, love resting in creatures as ends is *cupiditas*. Virtue is love rightly ordered. "So that it seems to me that it is a brief but true definition of virtue to say, it is the order of love" (*City of God* XV.22). Hence *ordo amoris*.

*On Christian Doctrine* I builds the order out of a distinction between enjoying and using. "For to enjoy a thing is to rest with satisfaction in it for its own sake. To use, on the other hand, is to employ whatever means are at one's disposal to obtain what one desires" (*On Christian Doctrine* I.4.4). Only God is to be enjoyed (*frui*). Everything else is to be used (*uti*), that is, loved in relation to God. Augustine's image is a traveller far from home who falls in love with the journey and forgets to go home: "this world must be used, not enjoyed" (*On Christian Doctrine* I.4.4). The just person "neither loves what he ought not to love, nor fails to love what he ought to love, nor loves that more which ought to be loved less" (*On Christian Doctrine* I.27.28).

The worry is obvious: are friends and neighbours to be used? Anders Nygren (*Agape and Eros*, 1930–1936) read the scheme as a self-centred eros that makes neighbours into means. Augustine himself hesitates, answering that a human being is loved for God's sake (I.22.20–21) and speaking of enjoying one another in God (I.33.37). Oliver O'Donovan ("*Usus* and *Fruitio* in Augustine," 1982) argued that "use" means referring a love to God, and that Augustine later dropped the vocabulary for persons.

## The weight of love

In ancient physics every body has a weight (*pondus*) carrying it to its natural place. *Confessions* XIII applies this to the soul. "The body by its own weight strives towards its own place." Things out of their place are restless; "restored to order, they are at rest. My weight, is my love; thereby am I borne, whithersoever I am borne" (*Conf.* XIII.9.10). The Latin is *pondus meum amor meus*.

The image joins the system. The restless heart of I.1 is a weight out of place; the ascent of VII.17 fails under the weight of habit; grace sets the weight in a new direction: "We are inflamed, by Thy Gift we are kindled; and are carried upwards." A person is defined by what they love, and so is a people (*City of God* XIX.24). Peace is the rest a weight finds in its place (XIX.13).

## The inner turn and illumination

Augustine's search for God goes inward before it goes up. "And being thence admonished to return to myself, I entered even into my inward self, Thou being my Guide" (*Conf.* VII.10.16). There he saw "above my mind, the Light Unchangeable." "Behold, Thou wert within, and I abroad, and there I searched for Thee" (*Conf.* X.27.38). *On True Religion* 39.72 compresses the method into a precept: do not go outside, return into yourself, truth dwells in the inner man, and if you find your nature changeable, transcend yourself too (paraphrased).

The inner turn leads to illumination. The mind judges bodies by standards it did not get from bodies, such as unity, equality and number, and knows truths that are the same for every mind, such as that seven and three are ten. It sees them in a light not its own, as the eye sees in sunlight, and the light is God (*Soliloquies* I.8.15; *On the Trinity* XII.15.24, paraphrased).

*The Teacher* (*De magistro*, 389), a dialogue with his son Adeodatus, applies this to learning. A word names either something we know, so we learn nothing, or something we do not know, so it is an empty sound until we meet the thing. We learn by consulting the truth within, and the only teacher is Christ, the inner teacher (*The Teacher* 11.38–14.46, paraphrased).

How illumination works is disputed. Étienne Gilson read it as God supplying the certainty of judgments while content comes from the senses; Ronald Nash, more strongly, as knowledge of the forms in God; Lydia Schumacher (*Divine Illumination*, 2011), modestly, as the mind's God-given power of reasoning. See [Thomas Aquinas](../../eras/02-medieval/thomas-aquinas.md), who identified the light with the agent intellect, and [Malebranche](../../eras/03-early-modern/nicolas-malebranche.md), who revived the strong reading.

## Memory

*Confessions* X asks where God can be found in Augustine now. He passes beyond the senses. "And I come to the fields and spacious palaces of my memory, where are the treasures of innumerable images" (*Conf.* X.8.12). Memory (*memoria*) is wider than recall. It holds images of bodies, the liberal arts, which did not come through the senses (X.9.16–12.19), past feelings without their being felt again (X.14.21), and even forgetfulness, a paradox he leaves standing (X.16.24–25).

Memory is so large that it exceeds self-knowledge. "Great is this force of memory, excessive great, O my God; a large and boundless chamber! who ever sounded the bottom thereof? yet is this a power of mine, and belongs unto my nature; nor do I myself comprehend all that I am" (*Conf.* X.8.15). Augustine had already found this in grief: after his friend's death "I became a great riddle to myself" (*Conf.* IV.4.9).

Everyone wants the happy life, so everyone knows it somehow, though not as they remember Carthage; it is in memory as joy in the truth (X.20.29–23.33). God too is found in memory, though not as one of its images, and is above it (X.24.35–26.37). This echoes Platonic recollection without a past life, which Augustine rejected. See Argument 6.

## Time and eternity

*Confessions* XI begins with a sceptic's question: what was God doing before he made heaven and earth? Augustine refuses the joke reply, that he was preparing hell for people who ask (XI.12.14). God made time with the world, so there was no *before*, and God is not in time at all. "Thy years are one day; and Thy day is not daily, but To-day" and "Thy To-day, is Eternity" (*Conf.* XI.13.16). Eternity is an unchanging present that has no parts, past or future.

"What then is time? If no one asks me, I know: if I wish to explain it to one that asketh, I know not" (*Conf.* XI.14.17). The past is no longer, the future is not yet, and the present, if it lasted, would be eternity. Any stretch of present divides into past and future, so the present has no length. Yet we measure time.

Augustine's answer moves time into the mind. "What now is clear and plain is, that neither things to come nor past are." It would be better to say there are three times, "a present of things past, a present of things present, and a present of things future. For these three do exist in some sort, in the soul, but otherwhere do I not see them; present of things past, memory; present of things present, sight; present of things future, expectation" (*Conf.* XI.20.26). When we measure a syllable or a psalm, we measure an impression that stays in memory. So "time is nothing else than protraction; but of what, I know not; and I marvel, if it be not of the mind itself?" (*Conf.* XI.26.33). Pusey's "protraction" renders *distentio*. The usual Latin tag is *distentio animi*, a distension or stretching of the mind.

In the psalm example of XI.28.38, expectation stretches over the whole psalm, and recited verses pass through attention into memory; a life and the history of the race are stretched the same way. Edmund Husserl's *Lectures on the Phenomenology of Internal Time-Consciousness* (1928) open by praising these chapters; see [Husserl](../../eras/05-20th-century/edmund-husserl.md). Wittgenstein quotes XI.14.17 in *Philosophical Investigations* §89 as the model of a question that puzzles us because we already know the answer in use; see [Wittgenstein (late)](../../eras/05-20th-century/wittgenstein-late.md). Argument 7 asks whether the account makes time depend on minds.

## The soul and the mind's self-knowledge

The Stoics, Tertullian and the Manichees thought the soul a fine body. *On the Trinity* X answers from what the mind knows with certainty. Men have doubted whether the power of thinking belongs to air, fire, the brain or the blood. "Yet who ever doubts that he himself lives, and remembers, and understands, and wills, and thinks, and knows, and judges? Seeing that even if he doubts, he lives; if he doubts, he remembers why he doubts; if he doubts, he understands that he doubts" (*On the Trinity* X.10.14).

The mind is certain of itself and uncertain whether it is air or fire, and in knowing itself it knows its substance. So it "is certain that it is not any of those things of which it is uncertain, and is certain that it is that only, which only it is certain that it is" (*On the Trinity* X.10.16). The mind is not a body. Descartes argued from doubt to an immaterial thinking thing in the Second Meditation; Antoine Arnauld noted the parallel in the Fourth Objections (1641). The same certainty answers the Academic sceptics in *City of God* XI.26: "What if you are deceived? For if I am deceived, I am." The tag is *si fallor, sum*. Argument 8 sets out the three versions (*On Free Choice* II.3.7, *On the Trinity* X.10.14, *City of God* XI.26) and compares them with the cogito; see the [Descartes dossier](../rene-descartes/README.md) and [the sceptics](../../eras/01-ancient/pyrrho-of-elis-and-the-skeptics.md).

The mind is made in the image of a God who is three persons in one being, so Augustine looks in it for a trinity. Book IX finds mind, self-knowledge and self-love; Book X refines these into memory, understanding and will. "Since, then, these three, memory, understanding, will, are not three lives, but one life; nor three minds, but one mind; it follows certainly that neither are they three substances, but one substance" (*On the Trinity* X.11.18). Each contains the others: I remember that I understand and will, understand that I remember and will, and will that I remember and understand. Memory here is the mind's standing presence to itself. The image is perfected only when the mind remembers, understands and loves God (*On the Trinity* XIV.12.15). It remains an analogy, and Book XV lists where it fails.

## Sin, original sin and grace

Augustine's account of human failure begins with pride. "And what is the origin of our evil will but pride?" He answers with Ecclesiasticus 10:13, that pride is the beginning of sin, and then defines it: "And what is pride but the craving for undue exaltation?" (*City of God* XIV.13). Adam could easily have kept the command; his sin was the soul's wish to become "a kind of end to itself" (*City of God* XIV.13).

Augustine read Romans 5:12 in a Latin version that said all sinned *in him* (*in quo*), where the Greek *eph' hō* is better read *because* all sinned. So the whole race was present in Adam and shares his guilt as well as his punishment. "Hence the whole mass of the human race is condemned; for he who at first gave entrance to sin has been punished with all his posterity who were in him as in a root" (*City of God* XXI.12). The phrase *massa damnata*, the condemned mass, comes from this vocabulary (compare *Enchiridion* 27). Inherited guilt shows itself as concupiscence (*concupiscentia*), desire the will cannot master, plainest in sexual arousal (*City of God* XIV.16–24).

The damage is to the will, not to nature as created. "Man's nature, indeed, was created at first faultless and without any sin; but that nature of man in which every one is born from Adam, now wants the Physician, because it is not sound" (*On Nature and Grace* 3.3). The fallen person still has free choice (*liberum arbitrium*). What has been lost is freedom (*libertas*), the ability to choose the good reliably. Adam was able not to sin; fallen humanity is not able not to sin; the saints in heaven will not be able to sin (*On Rebuke and Grace* 12.33, paraphrased). Only grace restores freedom. "Do we then by grace make void free will? God forbid! Nay, rather we establish free will" (*On the Spirit and the Letter* 30.52).

The Pelagian controversy (411–430) forced the doctrine into its hardest form. Pelagius, a British ascetic teaching in Rome, read the prayer of *Confessions* X.29.40, "Give what Thou enjoinest, and enjoin what Thou wilt," and objected that it made human effort pointless (Augustine reports this in *On the Gift of Perseverance* 20.53). Pelagius held that God's commands imply our ability to keep them, and that grace is nature, law and Christ's example. Augustine replied that grace moves the will from within: "God works in the hearts of men to incline their wills wherever He wills" (*On Grace and Free Will* 21.43). Grace works in us so that we will, and with us once we will (17.33). A council at Carthage condemned Pelagianism in 418 and Pope Zosimus confirmed it; Julian of Eclanum fought on until Augustine's death (Argument 12).

## Predestination

If faith itself is God's gift, then God chooses who will believe. Augustine admits he once thought otherwise: he was "in a similar error, thinking that faith whereby we believe in God is not God's gift, but that it is in us from ourselves" (*On the Predestination of the Saints* 3.7). The change came in *To Simplician* I.2 (396), where he worked through Romans 9 and concluded that God's choice of Jacob over Esau did not rest on any merit, even foreseen faith. He says so in *Retractations* II.1 and *Predestination of the Saints* 4.8.

From the condemned mass God chooses some for salvation by unmerited grace and leaves the rest to just punishment. The number of the elect is fixed (*City of God* XIV.26). "Between grace and predestination there is only this difference, that predestination is the preparation for grace, while grace is the donation itself" (*On the Predestination of the Saints* 10.19). God does not predestine anyone to sin. He foresees sin, and he leaves sinners to their own deserts, so his justice is shown in the damned and his mercy in the saved (*Enchiridion* 98–99). Against Cicero, *City of God* V.9–10 argues that foreknowledge does not remove choice, since what God foreknows is that we will choose freely (Argument 11).

Monks in Hadrumetum and Marseille, later called semi-Pelagians (John Cassian among them), resisted the claim that even the beginning of faith is God's gift; *On the Predestination of the Saints* and *On the Gift of Perseverance* (428–429) answer them. The Second Council of Orange (529) affirmed the priority of grace and rejected predestination to evil without adopting Augustine's full doctrine. See [Boethius](../../eras/02-medieval/boethius.md) on foreknowledge and [William of Ockham](../../eras/02-medieval/william-of-ockham.md) on future contingents.

## The two cities and the lust for rule

*City of God* (413–426/7) answered pagans who blamed the Goths' sack of Rome in 410 on the abandonment of the old gods. Books I–X refute them; XI–XXII tell the story of two cities, defined by their loves. "Accordingly, two cities have been formed by two loves: the earthly by the love of self, even to the contempt of God; the heavenly by the love of God, even to the contempt of self" (*City of God* XIV.28). The heavenly city includes the good angels and the elect, the earthly the fallen angels and the reprobate. On earth they are mixed, and no institution coincides with either. Even the church contains members of both until the judgment (*City of God* I.35, XX.9).

The earthly city's ruling passion is the lust for rule, *libido dominandi*. The Preface to Book I says the earthly city, "though it be mistress of the nations, is itself ruled by its lust of rule." Book XIV.28 repeats the phrase: in the earthly city "the princes and the nations it subdues are ruled by the love of ruling". In Book V.12–20 the early Romans curb other vices for love of praise, which later gives way to love of domination; God rewarded their virtue with empire, though it was not true virtue, since it served glory.

## Peace and justice

Every creature seeks peace. Even war seeks a peace that suits the victor (XIX.12). *City of God* XIX.13 defines it as a ladder of orders. Each level is a well-ordered concord, from body and soul up to the city. At the top, "The peace of all things is the tranquillity of order. Order is the distribution which allots things equal and unequal, each to its own place." Political peace is one case of the metaphysical order that weight seeks (*Conf.* XIII.9.10). The heavenly city on pilgrimage uses earthly peace without enjoying it as its end, and obeys laws that secure life's necessities so long as they permit the worship of God (*City of God* XIX.17).

On justice, Augustine is harsh. "Justice being taken away, then, what are kingdoms but great robberies? For what are robberies themselves, but little kingdoms?" (*City of God* IV.4). A captured pirate tells Alexander that he is called a robber for doing with one ship what Alexander does with a fleet. In Book XIX, Cicero's *On the Republic* defines a people by agreement about right (*ius*) and common interest; true justice gives God his due, so Rome was never a commonwealth (*City of God* XIX.21). Augustine offers a definition that fits real states: "a people is an assemblage of reasonable beings bound together by a common agreement as to the objects of their love" (*City of God* XIX.24). A people is judged by what it loves.

Two applications are hard. Slavery is explained as a result of sin. "The prime cause, then, of slavery is sin, which brings man under the dominion of his fellow," and yet "by nature, as God first created us, no one is the slave either of man or of sin" (*City of God* XIX.15). Augustine does not call for abolition. War is lamented and permitted. "But, say they, the wise man will wage just wars. As if he would not all the rather lament the necessity of just wars, if he remembers that he is a man" (*City of God* XIX.7). *Against Faustus* XXII.74 names what makes war evil. "The real evils in war are love of violence, revengeful cruelty, fierce and implacable enmity, wild resistance, and the lust of power, and such like." XXII.75 adds that "A great deal depends on the causes for which men undertake wars, and on the authority they have for doing so." Through Gratian and Aquinas these passages began the just war tradition (Argument 14).

How far the two cities yield a theory of the state is disputed. Medieval "political Augustinianism" (H.-X. Arquillière's phrase, 1934) subordinated rulers to the church; Robert Markus (*Saeculum*, 1970) found a neutral secular space shared by both cities; Reinhold Niebuhr drew a Christian realism about power.

## Signs and language

*On Christian Doctrine* (Books I–III to III.25.35 in 396–397, the rest and Book IV in 426–427) is a handbook for reading and preaching Scripture. It opens with a theory of signs. "All instruction is either about things or about signs; but things are learned by means of signs" (*On Christian Doctrine* I.2.2). Book I treats things, Book II signs. "For a sign is a thing which, over and above the impression it makes on the senses, causes something else to come into the mind as a consequence of itself: as when we see a footprint, we conclude that an animal whose footprint this is has passed by" (*On Christian Doctrine* II.1.1).

Signs are natural or given. "Natural signs are those which, apart from any intention or desire of using them as signs, do yet lead to the knowledge of something else, as, for example, smoke when it indicates fire" (*On Christian Doctrine* II.1.2). "Conventional signs, on the other hand, are those which living beings mutually exchange for the purpose of showing, as well as they can, the feelings of their minds, or their perceptions, or their thoughts" (II.2.3). Words are the chief conventional signs. Scripture's signs are literal or figurative, and the reader needs languages, history and natural science to read them (II.10.15–42.63). The rule of interpretation is love: a reading that does not build up love of God and neighbour has gone wrong (I.36.40).

*Confessions* I.8.13 gives an account of how a child learns to speak. Adults named things and turned toward them, and the child learned from "the motion of their body, the natural language, as it were, of all nations." "And thus by constantly hearing words, as they occurred in various sentences, I collected gradually for what they stood; and having broken in my mouth to these signs, I thereby gave utterance to my will." Wittgenstein opens the *Philosophical Investigations* (1953) with this passage, as an example of a picture on which every word names an object; see the [Wittgenstein dossier](../ludwig-wittgenstein/README.md). Wittgenstein's complaint (§32) is that the child described could already think. Augustine's defenders reply that the passage is a memoir, not a theory of meaning, and that *The Teacher* already argues that words alone teach nothing.

## Lying

Augustine is the Western tradition's strictest opponent of lying. *On Lying* (c. 395) defines a lie by the speaker's mind. "Wherefore, that man lies, who has one thing in his mind and utters another in words, or by signs of whatever kind. Whence also the heart of him who lies is said to be double" (*On Lying* 3). A false statement believed true is no lie; a true one believed false is.

Every lie is a sin, since it uses speech against its purpose. "Now it is evident that speech was given to man, not that men might therewith deceive one another, but that one man might make known his thoughts to another" (*Enchiridion* 22). No lie is permitted even to save a life. Augustine discusses the case of hiding a man from those who would kill him and recommends silence or refusal over a lie (*On Lying* 22–24). He ranks lies in eight kinds, from lies in religious teaching, the worst, to harmless lies that protect someone from bodily harm, the least bad, but none is allowed (*On Lying* 25). *Against Lying* (420) forbids Christians to pose as heretics to expose them. A lie is a sign that misrepresents the mind behind it. Kant held a similar line in "On a Supposed Right to Lie from Philanthropy" (1797); see [Kant](../../eras/03-early-modern/immanuel-kant.md). The standard objection is that the rule sacrifices an innocent life to the speaker's purity.

## How the pieces interlock

God is being, truth and good, and creatures come from nothing and exist by depending on him. Being and goodness are the same, so evil is a privation, and moral evil is the will's deficient turning from higher to lower goods. The will acts by loving, love is the soul's weight, and virtue is the order of love: enjoy God, use everything else. The inner turn finds God within and above the mind, in memory and in the light of truth by which the mind judges. Time is the mind's stretching across memory, attention and expectation, while God is an unchanging present. The mind's certainty of itself refutes the sceptics and the materialists and shows an image of the Trinity in memory, understanding and will. Pride disordered Adam's love, the disorder passed to his descendants, and only grace reorders it, given to those God has chosen. A society is defined by what it loves, so the two loves make two cities, mixed in history and separated at the end. Signs carry love and knowledge between minds, which is why lying is a sin. A good self-test is to trace any doctrine back to the order of love.

## Key terms

In the format of the repo [glossary](../../glossary.md), restricted to Augustine's usage, with misleading senses flagged.

- **Faith seeking understanding (*crede ut intelligas*):** the rule that belief comes first and understanding is its goal (*On the Trinity* XV.2.2). Not a ban on reasoning.
- **Privation of good (*privatio boni*):** evil as a lack of good in something itself good (*Enchiridion* 11). Not the claim that evil is unreal or harmless.
- **Creation from nothing (*creatio ex nihilo*):** God's making of all things without pre-existing matter and with time (*Conf.* XII.7.7).
- **Free choice (*liberum arbitrium*) and freedom (*libertas*):** the power to choose, kept after the Fall, versus the power to choose the good reliably, restored only by grace.
- **Charity (*caritas*) and cupidity (*cupiditas*):** love directed to God and to others for God's sake, versus love that rests in creatures as ends.
- **Order of love (*ordo amoris*):** virtue as loving each thing as much as it deserves (*City of God* XV.22).
- **Use and enjoyment (*uti*, *frui*):** loving a thing for the sake of another, versus resting in it for its own sake (*On Christian Doctrine* I.4.4). "Use" does not mean exploitation.
- **Weight (*pondus*):** love as the force that carries the soul to its place (*Conf.* XIII.9.10).
- **Illumination (*illuminatio*):** the divine light in which the mind sees unchanging truths. Its exact mechanism is disputed.
- **Memory (*memoria*):** the mind's store and its presence to itself, holding images, knowledge, feelings and the idea of happiness. Wider than recall.
- **Distension of the mind (*distentio animi*):** time as the mind's stretching across memory, attention and expectation (*Conf.* XI.26.33; Pusey's "protraction").
- **Mind (*mens*) and the mental trinity:** memory, understanding and will (*memoria, intelligentia, voluntas*), one mind in three relations (*On the Trinity* X.11.18). An analogy, not a proof.
- ***Si fallor, sum*:** "if I am deceived, I am," the certainty of one's own existence against the sceptics (*City of God* XI.26).
- **Original sin (*peccatum originale*):** the guilt and wound inherited from Adam. Concerns the will's condition, not the goodness of the body.
- **Condemned mass (*massa damnata*, *massa perditionis*):** the human race after Adam, justly condemned as a whole (*Enchiridion* 27; *City of God* XXI.12).
- **Grace (*gratia*), operating and cooperating:** God's unmerited help that makes the will good and then works with it (*On Grace and Free Will* 17.33).
- **Predestination (*praedestinatio*):** God's preparation of grace for the elect (*On the Predestination of the Saints* 10.19). In Augustine, not a decree to sin.
- **City of God (*civitas Dei*) and earthly city (*civitas terrena*):** two societies formed by two loves, mixed in history (*City of God* XIV.28). Neither is the church or the state.
- **Lust for rule (*libido dominandi*):** the earthly city's desire to dominate (*City of God* I Preface).
- **Peace (*pax*), tranquillity of order (*tranquillitas ordinis*):** each thing in its place (*City of God* XIX.13).
- **Sign (*signum*), natural and given (*naturale, datum*):** something that brings another thing to mind (*On Christian Doctrine* II.1.1–2.3).
- **Lie (*mendacium*):** saying what one does not think, with a double heart (*On Lying* 3). Not the same as saying something false.

## Known internal tensions

The system strains at five joints. [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md) gives the history of each.

**(1) Free will against grace.** *On Free Choice* (388–395) defends the will's power so firmly that Pelagius cited it on his side, and Augustine replied that the book was not about grace (*Retractations* I.9). From *To Simplician* (396) on, grace decides who believes. Augustine says grace "establishes" free will (*On the Spirit and the Letter* 30.52), but if God inclines wills "wherever He wills" (*On Grace and Free Will* 21.43), what does the will contribute? Peter Brown (*Augustine of Hippo*, 1967, rev. 2000) and Kurt Flasch (*Logik des Schreckens*, 1990) describe a break in 396; James Wetzel (*Augustine and the Limits of Virtue*, 1992) traces a gradual change through the analysis of habit; Carol Harrison (*Rethinking Augustine's Early Theology*, 2006) argues for continuity from 386. See Arguments 9 and 12.

**(2) The goodness of creation against the condemned mass.** Against the Manichees, "whatsoever is, is good." Against the Pelagians, the whole race is a condemned mass, and unbaptised infants are damned, though to the mildest punishment (*Enchiridion* 93). Julian charged that this was Manichaeism again, with nature made evil. Augustine replied that guilt belongs to the will's inheritance, not to nature as created (*On Nature and Grace* 3.3). Whether an unchosen guilt can be just remains the objection, and Augustine admitted that the case of infants troubled him (*Letter* 166).

**(3) Inwardness against church authority.** Truth is found within, taught by the inner teacher, and *The Teacher* denies that human words teach. Yet Augustine also wrote that he would not believe the Gospel unless the authority of the Catholic church moved him (*Against the Letter of Mani Called Fundamental* 5.6, paraphrased). Luther drew on the inward Augustine of grace, the Council of Trent on the Augustine of the church. A reconciling reading makes authority the road and inner sight the goal.

**(4) Platonist eternity against narrative time.** God is a changeless present, and the soul's goal is to rise above time. Yet the *Confessions* and *City of God* are histories in which salvation comes through dated events: an Incarnation, a conversion in a Milan garden in 386, a story from creation to judgment. *Distentio* also names the fallen soul's scattering in time (*Conf.* XI.29.39), so time is both the medium of salvation and a symptom of the fall. Arendt (*Love and Saint Augustine*, 1929) and Paul Ricoeur (*Time and Narrative*, vol. 1, 1983) treat the tension as productive; see [Ricoeur](../../eras/05-20th-century/paul-ricoeur.md).

**(5) Coercion of the Donatists.** The Donatists held invalid the sacraments of clergy who had lapsed in persecution. Augustine first held that no one should be forced into unity. After the imperial edict of 405 and the Carthage conference of 411, he defended coercion. *Letter* 93 (408) says colleagues' experience changed his mind and cites the parable's "compel them to come in" (Luke 14:23); *Letter* 185 (417) develops the case (both paraphrased). He asked for fines and exile, not death. The defence sits badly with his claim that faith must be willed. Pierre Bayle's *Philosophical Commentary* (1686) attacked the "compel them to come in" argument as a warrant for persecuting Huguenots; see [Bayle](../../eras/03-early-modern/bayle-and-voltaire.md). Brown (*Augustine of Hippo*, ch. 21) reads the change as a bishop's response to violence on both sides; Flasch connects it to the grace doctrine, since a will that cannot heal itself may be helped from outside.
