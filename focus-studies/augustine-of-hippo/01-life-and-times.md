# Life and Times: Thagaste, Carthage, Milan, Cassiciacum, and Hippo

Augustine told the first half of his life once, as a prayer. The *Confessions*, written about 397–401, take the story from his infancy to his mother's death in 387 and then turn to memory, time and creation. They were written ten to fifteen years after the events by a bishop under suspicion, and a section below weighs them. The second half of the life comes from his letters and sermons (enlarged by the Divjak letters, 1981, and the Dolbeau sermons, 1990), from the *Retractations* (426–427), in which he reviewed his own books, and from the *Life of Augustine* by Possidius, bishop of Calama. The standard modern life is Peter Brown's *Augustine of Hippo* (1967; new edition with an epilogue, 2000); James J. O'Donnell's *Augustine: A New Biography* (2005) reads the sources more sceptically. The *Confessions* are quoted from E. B. Pusey's translation (1838), the *City of God* from Marcus Dods (1871). Possidius and the works with no public-domain English text in this dossier's corpus are paraphrased.

## Thagaste: Patricius and Monica, 354–366

Aurelius Augustinus was born on 13 November 354 at Thagaste, a small town in the Roman province of Numidia, now Souk Ahras in eastern Algeria. Possidius calls the family respectable and of the town council's rank. Augustine called his father, Patricius, "a poor freeman of Thagaste" (*Conf.* II.3.5), a small landowner who stretched his means to educate a clever son. Patricius was a pagan with a temper; he became a catechumen late and was baptized before he died, around 370–371 (*Conf.* II.3.6, IX.9.22). His mother, Monica, born about 331, was a Christian of the North African church. There was a brother, Navigius, and at least one sister.

Augustine was enrolled as a catechumen in infancy and not baptized, which was common. He hated school, the beatings and Greek, and loved Latin literature, Virgil above all (*Conf.* I.9–I.17). His reconstruction of how he learned to speak, by watching adults name things and point (*Conf.* I.8.13), is the passage Wittgenstein's *Philosophical Investigations* opens with (see [the Wittgenstein dossier](../ludwig-wittgenstein/README.md)). He never mastered Greek. This mattered later: his Plato and Plotinus came to him in Latin translation, and he read the Greek theologians little.

## Madauros, the idle year, and Carthage, 366–373

Around 366 he was sent to Madauros (M'Daourouch), a larger town some twenty miles south, the home of the second-century writer Apuleius, to study grammar and literature. In 369–370, his sixteenth year, the money ran out and he spent a year idle at home while his father saved for Carthage (*Conf.* II.3.5). The pear theft of *Confessions* II.4 belongs to this year. At the end of 370 or in 371, helped by Romanianus, a rich man of Thagaste (*Conf.* VI.14.24), he went to Carthage, the capital of Roman Africa, to study rhetoric, the subject that led to advocacy, teaching and imperial office.

## The partner and Adeodatus

In Carthage, about 371–372, he began to live with a woman whose name he never gives. He calls the arrangement a bond made "in a wayward passion", and adds that she was "but one, remaining faithful even to her" (*Conf.* IV.2.2). The relationship was a recognized form of concubinage. Roman law barred or discouraged marriage across a large gap in social rank, and an ambitious young man was expected to marry later and well. Their son, Adeodatus ("given by God"), was born about 372. He was "not quite fifteen" at his baptism in April 387 (*Conf.* IX.6.14). They lived together for about fifteen years.

## Cicero's *Hortensius*, 373

In 373, aged eighteen, he read Cicero's *Hortensius* in the ordinary course of study. The dialogue, now lost apart from fragments, urged its reader to philosophy (see [Cicero and Lucretius](../../eras/01-ancient/cicero-and-lucretius.md)). Augustine wrote that "this book altered my affections, and turned my prayers to Thyself O Lord; and made me have other purposes and desires" (*Conf.* III.4.7). He dates the reading to his nineteenth year, two years after his father's death (*Conf.* III.4.7), and dates his whole search for wisdom from it (*Conf.* VIII.7.17). He turned to the Bible next and found its Latin crude beside Cicero's (*Conf.* III.5.9).

## Nine years a Manichaean hearer, 373–382

He joined the Manichees. Their religion came from the third-century Persian prophet Mani and taught two eternal principles, Light and Darkness, mixed in the present world; the good soul is a fragment of Light trapped in matter, and evil comes from the alien power, not from God or the self. The sect promised reasons rather than faith and rejected the Old Testament. Its Elect kept a strict ascetic rule; its Hearers, Augustine among them, supported them. He kept to the sect, in his own count, "for this space of nine years (from my nineteenth year to my eight-and-twentieth)", during which "we lived seduced and seducing, deceived and deceiving, in divers lusts" (*Conf.* IV.1.1). Manichaeism was illegal under imperial law, and the sect's network of patrons and friends later helped his career in Rome. Monica, distressed, for a time refused to share a table with him (*Conf.* III.11.19).

## Teaching at Thagaste and Carthage, 375–383

He went home about 375 to teach, first grammar and then rhetoric (*Conf.* IV.4.7). There a close friend from childhood fell ill, was baptized while unconscious, mocked by Augustine on waking, and died a few days later. Augustine's grief is the first great piece of psychology in the book: "I became a great riddle to myself" (*Conf.* IV.4.9). He could not bear the town without the friend and moved back to Carthage (*Conf.* IV.7.12), about 376.

In Carthage he taught rhetoric until 383, won a poetry prize, and wrote his first book, *On the Beautiful and the Fitting*, lost already by the time of the *Confessions* (*Conf.* IV.13.20). His doubts about the Manichees grew from their astronomy, which could not match the eclipse predictions of the philosophers (*Conf.* V.3.3–6). The Manichees told him to wait for their bishop Faustus of Milevis. Faustus came in Augustine's twenty-ninth year, 382–383: "a certain Bishop of the Manichees, Faustus by name, a great snare of the Devil" (*Conf.* V.3.3). He proved charming, frank about his ignorance, and unable to answer the questions (*Conf.* V.6.10–V.7.13). Augustine did not leave the sect at once, but his hope in it was gone. About fifteen years later, as a bishop, he answered Faustus's book in thirty-three books, *Against Faustus*.

## Rome, 383

In 383 he sailed for Rome, where friends told him the students were better behaved than the riotous ones of Carthage (*Conf.* V.8.14). Monica wanted to stop him or come too. He told her he was seeing off a friend and sailed in the night: "And I lied to my mother, and such a mother, and escaped" (*Conf.* V.8.15). In Rome he lodged with a Manichaean Hearer, fell seriously ill, and found that students there changed teachers before the fee was due (*Conf.* V.12.22). He inclined toward the sceptics of the New Academy, who held that nothing can be known for certain (*Conf.* V.10.19; see [Pyrrho and the skeptics](../../eras/01-ancient/pyrrho-of-elis-and-the-skeptics.md)). His earliest surviving work, *Against the Academicians*, is aimed at that position, and his later answer to the sceptic, *si fallor, sum*, is the ancestor of Descartes' cogito (see [the Descartes dossier](../rene-descartes/README.md)).

## Milan, Symmachus and Ambrose, 384–386

In autumn 384 the city of Milan asked the prefect of Rome for a professor of rhetoric. Augustine applied through his Manichaean friends and asked "that Symmachus, then prefect of the city, would try me by setting me some subject, and so send me" (*Conf.* V.13.23). Quintus Aurelius Symmachus was the leading pagan senator of the day. That summer he had petitioned the emperor Valentinian II to restore the Altar of Victory to the Senate house, and Ambrose, bishop of Milan, had defeated the petition. Symmachus sent a non-Christian rhetorician to the court city where his opponent was bishop. Milan was the Western emperor's residence, and the professor delivered official speeches. Augustine gave a panegyric on Valentinian II (*Conf.* VI.6.9), in November 384 by the usual dating, and another on the consul Bauto on 1 January 385.

"To Milan I came, to Ambrose the Bishop" (*Conf.* V.13.23). Ambrose, a former governor, bishop since 374, received him kindly. Augustine went to his sermons for their style and found the content working on him. Ambrose read the Old Testament figuratively, as Philo and Origen had, and this removed the Manichaean objections to it (*Conf.* V.14.24, VI.4.6). Augustine also watched Ambrose read: "his eye glided over the pages, and his heart searched out the sense, but his voice and tongue were at rest" (*Conf.* VI.3.3). The passage is often cited to show that silent reading was rare in antiquity; Bernard Knox (1968) and others have shown it was practised, and what struck Augustine was that a busy bishop could not be approached. He decided to be a catechumen in the Catholic church again (*Conf.* V.14.25).

Monica arrived in 385. A marriage was arranged with a girl two years under the legal age of twelve. The partner was sent back to Africa: "my heart which clave unto her was torn and wounded and bleeding. And she returned to Afric, vowing unto Thee never to know any other man, leaving with me my son by her" (*Conf.* VI.15.25). He took another mistress while he waited. Augustine records the cruelty without defending it, but the woman appears in the book only as his loss.

## The books of the Platonists, 386

In the first half of 386 he read "certain books of the Platonists, translated from Greek into Latin" (*Conf.* VII.9.13), lent by a man he describes only as swollen with pride. The translator was Marius Victorinus, a famous African rhetorician at Rome who had become a Christian (*Conf.* VIII.2.3). Which books they were is disputed. Paul Henry and Pierre Courcelle argued for treatises of [Plotinus](../../eras/01-ancient/plotinus.md); Willy Theiler argued for Porphyry (see [the late Neoplatonists](../../eras/01-ancient/late-neoplatonists.md)); most scholars now think both. The books gave him two things the Manichees had not: a way to think of God as real without being a body, and a way to think of evil as a lack of good rather than a substance (*Conf.* VII.10.16–VII.16.22). He rose in thought toward God, fell back (*Conf.* VII.17.23), and turned to Paul (*Conf.* VII.21.27).

## The garden, August 386

The decision came in late summer. The old priest Simplicianus, who had instructed Ambrose and would succeed him, told him how Victorinus had made a public profession of faith at Rome (*Conf.* VIII.2.3–5). Then an African court official, Ponticianus, told him and his friend Alypius about the Egyptian monk Antony and about two imperial agents at Trier who had read the *Life of Antony* and left their careers on the spot (*Conf.* VIII.6.14–15). Augustine went into the garden of the house, in turmoil, and lay under a fig tree. From a neighbouring house a child's voice kept repeating "Take up and read; Take up and read" (*Conf.* VIII.12.29), in Latin *tolle lege*. He opened Paul at Romans 13:13–14, read the command to put on Christ, and was resolved. Alypius read the next verse and joined him (*Conf.* VIII.12.30). They went in and told Monica.

He did not resign at once. The vintage vacation was a few weeks off, and he chose to finish the term quietly; chest pains and a weak voice gave him a public reason to give up teaching (*Conf.* IX.2.2–4). At the end of the vacation he gave the city notice to find another rhetorician (*Conf.* IX.5.13).

## Cassiciacum, autumn 386–early 387

From the autumn of 386 he stayed with Monica, Adeodatus, Alypius, his brother, two cousins and two pupils at Cassiciacum, a country house near Milan lent by the grammarian Verecundus (*Conf.* IX.3.5). The place may be Cassago Brianza; the identification is not certain. There he wrote the first works that survive: *Against the Academicians*, *On the Happy Life*, *On Order* and the *Soliloquies*. The three dialogues are written up from discussions taken down by a stenographer, and Monica takes part in two of them. Their tone is calm, philosophical and Ciceronian. They mention his debt to the Platonists and to Paul, and nothing like the garden. That difference is the core of the modern dispute over the *Confessions* as a source (below).

## Baptism, Ostia and Monica's death, 387

He returned to Milan to enrol for baptism and was baptized by Ambrose at the Easter vigil of 24–25 April 387, with Alypius and Adeodatus (*Conf.* IX.6.14). Of his son he wrote: "He was not quite fifteen, and in wit surpassed many grave and learned men" (*Conf.* IX.6.14). The party, joined by Evodius, a fellow African and a former imperial agent, set out for Africa.

At Ostia, the port of Rome, they waited for a ship. One day Augustine and Monica stood at a window looking onto the garden of the house and talked of eternal life. In the conversation they rose in thought beyond all created things and "slightly touched on her with the whole effort of our heart" (*Conf.* IX.10.24), *her* being eternal Wisdom. Monica then said she had nothing left to live for (*Conf.* IX.10.26). Within days she had a fever. She had long wanted to be buried beside Patricius in Thagaste, and now asked only "that you would remember me at the Lord's altar, wherever you be" (*Conf.* IX.11.27). She died on the ninth day, in "the fifty-sixth year of her age, and the three-and-thirtieth of mine" (*Conf.* IX.11.28), in autumn 387. The *Confessions* end their narrative there, with a prayer for her and Patricius (*Conf.* IX.13.37).

## Rome and the return to Thagaste, 387–391

The voyage was blocked. The usurper Magnus Maximus had invaded Italy in 387, and Augustine stayed in Rome until Maximus fell in the summer of 388. There he wrote against the Manichees and began *On Free Choice of the Will*, finished about 395. In late 388 he reached Thagaste, gave away his property, and lived with friends in a small community of prayer and study. There he wrote *The Teacher* (*De magistro*), a dialogue with Adeodatus about whether words teach anything; he says the ideas given to his son in it were his son's (*Conf.* IX.6.14). Adeodatus died about 389–390, at seventeen or so. Augustine's friend Nebridius died about the same time.

## Hippo: priest, 391, and bishop, 395/396

In 391 he went to Hippo Regius, a port on the coast, now Annaba in Algeria, to recruit a friend for the community. He avoided towns that lacked a bishop, for fear of being chosen. Hippo had a bishop, Valerius. Valerius told his congregation that he needed a priest, and they seized Augustine in the church and presented him for ordination. Possidius says he wept (*Life* 4); Augustine told the story himself in a sermon decades later (Sermon 355.2). Forced ordination of this kind was common and binding. Valerius, a Greek speaker, let him preach, which African custom reserved to bishops, and gave him a garden for a monastery whose members became bishops elsewhere, Possidius at Calama among them.

In 395 or 396 Valerius had him consecrated as co-bishop, against a canon of the Council of Nicaea that neither man knew of; Megalius, primate of Numidia, at first objected because of rumours about Augustine's past. Valerius died soon after, and Augustine was bishop of Hippo for about thirty-four years. The job meant preaching several times a week, hearing civil cases in the bishop's court, managing church property and travelling to councils at Carthage.

## The *Confessions*, c. 397–401

In 396–397 he answered questions on Romans from Simplicianus, now bishop of Milan. In *To Simplician* I.2 he concluded that even the first movement of faith is God's gift. Looking back in the *Retractations* (II.1), he said that in that answer he had worked hard for the free choice of the will and that God's grace had prevailed. The *Confessions* followed, probably begun in 397 and finished by 401. Brown reads them as the new bishop taking stock of himself; O'Donnell also as a defence against rumours about his Manichaean past. Books I–IX tell the story, Book X describes the present state of his soul as bishop, and Books XI–XIII interpret the opening of Genesis. The narrative of the will in Book VIII is written in the light of *To Simplician*: the inability to will the good without grace is the theology of 396 told as the experience of 386.

## The Donatists and coercion, 393–412

The African church was split. In 311–312 a party had refused to accept Caecilian as bishop of Carthage because one of his consecrators was said to have handed over Scripture during the persecution of Diocletian. They elected a rival, soon succeeded by Donatus, and claimed to be the true church of the martyrs. The Donatists rebaptized converts and were often the majority church in Numidia, including Hippo. Augustine argued that the sacraments belong to Christ and do not depend on the holiness of the minister, and that the church is a mixed body of good and bad until the judgment.

At first he opposed imperial force, and he changed his mind around 405, the year of Honorius's Edict of Unity. A conference at Carthage in June 411, chaired by the imperial commissioner Marcellinus, ruled against the Donatists, and laws of 412 imposed fines and confiscation. In Letter 93 (c. 408) to the Rogatist bishop Vincentius he said the results at Thagaste and elsewhere had convinced him, since many who were forced back were glad of it, and he took *compelle intrare* (Luke 14:23) as warrant. Letter 185 (c. 417) to Count Boniface sets out the policy at length. He opposed the death penalty and torture for Donatists. The violence was not one-sided: bands called circumcellions attacked Catholic clergy, and Possidius records an ambush laid for Augustine himself (*Life* 12). Later centuries cited his defence of coercion to justify the persecution of heretics; whether that use was faithful to him is disputed, and Brown's chapter "Disciplina" is the standard account.

## The sack of Rome and the *City of God*, 410–427

On 24 August 410 the Visigoths under Alaric entered Rome and sacked it for three days. Rome had not fallen to a foreign enemy for eight hundred years, and refugees in Africa said it had fallen because it had left its gods. The *Retractations*, which Dods translates in his Editor's Preface, open the account of the work with "Rome having been stormed and sacked by the Goths under Alaric their king" and the pagan charge that followed, and continue: "It was this which kindled my zeal for the house of God" (*Retractations* II.43, Dods's numbering). The work was dedicated to Marcellinus: "The glorious city of God is my theme in this work" (*City of God* I, Preface). It was written in parts from 413 to 426 or 427, and was, he says, "in my hands for several years, owing to the interruptions occasioned by many other affairs" (*Retractations* II.43). The first ten books answer the pagan case; the last twelve trace the origin, history and ends of the two cities. Marcellinus was executed in 413 in the aftermath of a revolt by Heraclian, the governor of Africa, despite Augustine's pleas.

## The Pelagian controversy, 411–430

Pelagius, a British ascetic teacher at Rome, held that God commands nothing impossible, that human nature is not corrupted by Adam's sin, and that grace consists in the gift of free will, the law and Christ's example. He and his follower Caelestius fled Rome before the sack; Caelestius was condemned at Carthage in 411, and Pelagius went on to Palestine. Augustine wrote against the position from 411–412 (*On the Spirit and the Letter*, 412), at first without naming Pelagius, and *On Nature and Grace* (415) answered Pelagius's *On Nature*. A synod at Diospolis acquitted Pelagius in December 415. The Africans appealed to Rome; Innocent I condemned the teaching in 417, Zosimus wavered, and in 418 the emperor, a council at Carthage and Zosimus condemned it.

The sharpest opponent was Julian, bishop of Eclanum in southern Italy, who refused to sign Zosimus's condemnation in 418 and was deposed. From 419 until Augustine's death they wrote against each other. Julian charged him with Manichaeism under a new name and with making God unjust for condemning infants; the *Unfinished Work against Julian* was on Augustine's desk when he died. The late works on grace, *On Grace and Free Will* (426–427) for the monks of Hadrumetum and *On the Predestination of the Saints* (428–429) for critics in southern Gaul, push the doctrine to its hardest form: God's choice of the saved is not based on foreseen merit. Whether this "late Augustine" breaks with the early one or develops him is a standing dispute; Brown sees a turn around 396, Carol Harrison argues for continuity from 386, and James Wetzel reads the change as a deepening of the early account of the will.

## The *Retractations*, 426–427

In September 426 Augustine named the priest Eraclius as his successor and handed him much of the daily business (Letter 213). He used the time to review his books in order of writing, correcting what he now thought wrong. The two books of the *Retractations* cover ninety-three works in 232 books, by his own count. He meant to review his letters and sermons as well and did not live to do it.

## The Vandals and the death of Augustine, 429–430

In May 429 the Vandals under Gaiseric crossed from Spain into Africa, perhaps eighty thousand people by Victor of Vita's later count, and moved east. Count Boniface, the Roman commander, fell back on Hippo, and the siege began in May or June 430. Possidius, a refugee in the city, copies into the *Life* (30) Augustine's letter to Bishop Honoratus (Letter 228), which told clergy not to abandon their congregations while any of the people remained. In the third month of the siege he fell ill with a fever. He had the penitential psalms copied and fixed to the wall where he could read them from his bed, and asked to be left alone for his last ten days except when the doctors came or food was brought (*Life* 31). He made no will, Possidius says, because as one of God's poor he had nothing to leave, and he asked that the church library be kept. He died on 28 August 430, in his seventy-sixth year. Hippo was evacuated and partly burned in 431. The library survived, and Possidius compiled a list of its contents, the *Indiculum*.

## Weighing the sources

**The *Confessions*.** The book is a prayer addressed to God that the reader overhears, and its narrative serves an argument about grace. Augustine says plainly that he is not reporting words exactly: the Ostia conversation went "even if not in this very manner, and these same words" (*Conf.* IX.10.26). It was written by a bishop of about forty-three about events of his late twenties and early thirties, after the theology of *To Simplician*. In 1918 Prosper Alfaric argued that the Cassiciacum dialogues show a conversion to Neoplatonism, with Christianity added later. Pierre Courcelle, in *Recherches sur les Confessions de saint Augustin* (1950), rejected Alfaric's thesis but argued that the garden scene is built from literary and scriptural figures: the fig tree recalls Nathanael (John 1:48), the reading by lot copies Antony, and the child's voice serves as a sign from God. He concluded that the scene cannot be read as a plain report. O'Donnell's commentary (1992) and biography (2005) take the narrative as the bishop's retrospective construction of his life and ask what it was written to answer; they treat the historical core as hard to recover and the shaping as the thing to study. Brown and most biographers accept that a crisis and decision occurred in Milan in August 386, in a form that Augustine later shaped. Three cautions follow. Check the *Confessions* against the dialogues of 386–387 where they overlap. Treat speeches and dialogue as Augustine's reconstruction. Read the account of the will in Book VIII as the theology of 396 applied to 386.

**Possidius.** The *Life* was written about 432–437 by a member of the Hippo community from 391 who was present at the death. It is reliable for the bishop's habits and death. It is also a saint's life, it passes quickly over the years before 391 because the *Confessions* had told them, and Possidius's own quarrels with the Donatists of Calama colour his account of the schism.

**The *Retractations*.** They date the works and record second thoughts. They read the early works through the late doctrine of grace, sometimes against the plain sense of the early text, and Pelagian readers already cited the early works against the late ones.

**The Divjak letters and the Dolbeau sermons.** In 1975 Johannes Divjak found twenty-nine letters, twenty-seven of them new, in manuscripts at Marseille and Paris; they were published in 1981 (CSEL 88). They show the old bishop at work: Letter 10* asks for action against slave traders seizing free Africans, and Letter 20* deals with Antoninus of Fussala, a young bishop Augustine had installed who abused his flock. In 1990 François Dolbeau found twenty-six sermons, many unknown or known only in part, in a manuscript in the Mainz city library, published in full in 1996; several show his preaching against pagan practice and the Donatists in the 390s and 400s. Both finds fill out a bishop absorbed in pastoral and legal business, and Brown's epilogue of 2000 uses them to soften the first edition's picture of a bishop grown harsh in old age.
