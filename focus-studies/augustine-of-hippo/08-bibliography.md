# Bibliography — Augustine
**English** | [简体中文](../../zh/focus-studies/augustine-of-hippo/08-bibliography.md)

Annotated and tiered: read downward only as far as your questions require. Prices are given only where checked on the publisher's own page (Hackett, November 2026); New City Press, Oxford, Cambridge and Penguin prices were not visible to this check and are left out. The primary texts are *owned*, per the learning path's rule, because library lending windows are shorter than this plan's seven weeks. One fact governs the whole list: Augustine's works are cited by **book, chapter and section**: the chapter divisions of the early printed editions and the section numbers of the Benedictine edition of the Maurists (Paris, 1679–1700), which every serious modern translation prints. *Conf.* VIII.12.29 is Book VIII, chapter 12, section 29; *City of God* XIX.13 is Book XIX, chapter 13. [02-works-and-texts.md](02-works-and-texts.md) explains the system. Buy translations that print these numbers, and every reference in this dossier will find its place.

## 1. Primary texts — the editions the plan uses

**Maria Boulding (trans.), *The Confessions*, The Works of Saint Augustine I/1 (New City Press, 1997).** The learning path's first ruling for Weeks 1–4. Boulding was a Benedictine nun, and her English keeps the *Confessions* a prayer addressed to God, which is what the Latin is. Her notes are short and mark the scriptural echoes, which carry more of the argument than a first reader expects.

**F. J. Sheed (trans.), *Confessions*, 2nd ed., ed. Michael P. Foley, intro. Peter Brown (Hackett, 2007; 384 pp., $16 paper).** The path's other ruling. Sheed's translation dates from 1943 and is crisp and fast. Foley's notes are the reason to choose this edition: they trace the quotations from Scripture and the classics and flag the Latin puns, and Brown's introduction is a short version of his biography. Pick Boulding or Sheed and do not alternate; the section numbers are the same. Thomas Williams's newer Hackett translation (2019; $15 paper) renders key terms the same way every time, which helps a philosopher, and is a good second copy for checking a passage.

**Henry Chadwick (trans.), *Confessions* (Oxford World's Classics, 1991).** The translation the library entry names and the one most cited in English scholarship. Use it to compare a sentence, not as a third reading text.

**Henry Bettenson (trans.), *Concerning the City of God against the Pagans* (Penguin Classics, 1972; 2003 reissue, intro. G. R. Evans), or R. W. Dyson (ed. and trans.), *The City of God against the Pagans* (Cambridge Texts in the History of Political Thought, 1998).** Weeks 5–7. Both are complete, which matters because Weeks 6–7 jump between Books I, IV, V, XI, XII, XIV and XIX. Bettenson is the cheaper paperback; Dyson's notes are aimed at political theory and suit Week 7. Avoid abridgements that drop chapter numbers. Per the learning path, do not open the *City of God* before the *Confessions*.

**Thomas Williams (trans.), *On Free Choice of the Will* (Hackett, 1993; 192 pp., $13 paper).** Week 5's text and the path's ruling for it. Williams's introduction sets out the dialogue's three books as a single argument about the source of evil. The dossier paraphrases this work, so you need a copy to check every reference in Arguments 9–11.

**Edmund Hill (trans.), *The Trinity*, The Works of Saint Augustine I/5 (New City Press, 1991).** Week 5's Book X. Hill's introductions explain why a treatise on God spends its second half on the human mind.

**Peter King (trans.), *Against the Academicians* and *The Teacher* (Hackett, 1995; 208 pp., $18 paper).** Not on the plan's reading list, but the only cheap English text of the two early dialogues behind Argument 8 and the illumination theory.

**Free texts.** Project Gutenberg has Pusey's [*Confessions*](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/3296) and Dods's *City of God* in [Volume I](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/45304) and [Volume II](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/45305), the texts this dossier quotes. [New Advent's Church Fathers](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/) gives the Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers translations of nearly every work on the plan, with chapter and section numbers. For the Latin, James J. O'Donnell's [electronic edition of the *Confessions*](https://faculty.georgetown.edu/jod/conf/) reprints the text and the full commentary of his Oxford edition (1992), one note per section; [The Latin Library](https://www.thelatinlibrary.com/august.html) has the Latin of the other works. These are for checking a reference, not for study.

## 2. First secondary books, in order

1. **Henry Chadwick, *Augustine* (Oxford, Past Masters, 1986; reissued as *Augustine: A Very Short Introduction*, 2001).** One or two evenings, before Week 1. Chadwick was a historian of the early Church and the *Confessions*' translator, and the book is a map of the life and the main doctrines in about 120 pages.
2. **Peter Brown, *Augustine of Hippo: A Biography* (Faber and University of California Press, 1967; new edition with an epilogue, 2000).** The life, across the seven weeks; read Parts I and II before Week 3 and the rest by Week 6. Brown made Augustine a figure of late Roman Africa and read the later Augustine on grace as a darker man than the convert of 386. The 2000 epilogue revises that judgement in the light of the letters found by Johannes Divjak (1981) and the sermons found by François Dolbeau (1990). It is the source for most of the dates in [01-life-and-times.md](01-life-and-times.md).
3. **James J. O'Donnell, *Augustine: A New Biography* (Ecco, 2005; in Britain, *Augustine, Sinner and Saint*).** Read after Brown, Weeks 3–6. O'Donnell reads the *Confessions* as a bishop's self-defence and the Donatists as the majority church of Africa. It is the sceptical counterweight in the dispute about the garden scene that [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md) sets out.
4. **Garry Wills, *Saint Augustine* (Penguin Lives, 1999).** A short life for a reader who wants one more voice. Wills has his own renderings of key words (he calls the *Confessions* *The Testimony*), so check any phrase he quotes against your translation.

## 3. Advanced and specialist, by area

Open these when a week's checkpoint question has narrowed to a debate. Most are scholarly books at academic prices, best borrowed or bought used.

- **The whole philosophy (all weeks).** John M. Rist, *Augustine: Ancient Thought Baptized* (Cambridge, 1994): Augustine's debts to Plato, Plotinus, Cicero and the Stoics, topic by topic, and where he broke with them; the best single book on the philosophy. Christopher Kirwan, *Augustine* (Routledge, Arguments of the Philosophers, 1989): an analytic philosopher's reconstruction of the arguments on scepticism, time, lying, evil and free will, often hostile and always exact. Use it to test your premises for Arguments 7–11. Gareth B. Matthews, *Augustine* (Blackwell Great Minds, 2005): short chapters, each on one problem (the cogito, other minds, time, the dream argument, evil), by a philosopher who reads Augustine as a colleague. *The Cambridge Companion to Augustine*, ed. Eleonore Stump & Norman Kretzmann (2001); 2nd ed., ed. David Vincent Meconi & Eleonore Stump (2014): one chapter per area; the first edition has Kretzmann and Stump on the philosophy, the second adds more on theology and reception.
- **The *Confessions* (Weeks 1–4).** O'Donnell's three-volume *Augustine: Confessions* (Oxford, 1992) is the reference for any question about a passage; its online text is above. John Peter Kenney, *The Mysticism of Saint Augustine: Rereading the Confessions* (Routledge, 2005): the ascents of Book VII and the vision at Ostia (IX.10), and how far they are Plotinian. Pierre Courcelle, *Recherches sur les Confessions de saint Augustin* (Paris, 1950; 2nd ed. 1968): in French; the origin of the literary reading of the garden scene and of the reconstruction of Ambrose's Platonist circle in Milan.
- **Mind, knowledge and time (Weeks 4–5).** Gerard O'Daly, *Augustine's Philosophy of Mind* (Duckworth, 1987): perception, memory, imagination and time, closely argued. Michael Mendelson, the author of the SEP's former Augustine entry, wrote two papers that fit Week 4 and Argument 8's background: "*venter animi/distentio animi*: Memory and Temporality in Augustine's *Confessions*" (*Augustinian Studies* 31, 2000) and "By the Things Themselves: Eudaimonism, Direct Acquaintance, and Illumination in Augustine's *De Magistro*" (*Journal of the History of Philosophy* 39, 2001). Gareth B. Matthews, *Thought's Ego in Augustine and Descartes* (Cornell, 1992), and Stephen Menn, *Descartes and Augustine* (Cambridge, 1998): the two books on *si fallor, sum* and the cogito; read them with [the Descartes dossier](../rene-descartes/README.md).
- **Will, evil and grace (Weeks 3, 5).** James Wetzel, *Augustine and the Limits of Virtue* (Cambridge, 1992): the divided will of Book VIII and why Augustine came to think virtue needs grace. Simon Harrison, *Augustine's Way into the Will* (Oxford, 2006): a close reading of *On Free Choice*, the best companion to Arguments 9–11. Carol Harrison, *Rethinking Augustine's Early Theology: An Argument for Continuity* (Oxford, 2006): the case that the early and late Augustine on grace are one thinker, against the sharp break that Brown's first edition and Kurt Flasch drew at *To Simplician* (396).
- **The *City of God*, history and politics (Weeks 6–7).** R. A. Markus, *Saeculum: History and Society in the Theology of St Augustine* (Cambridge, 1970; revised 1988): the argument that Augustine left a neutral, secular space between the two cities, the book that set the modern debate on his politics. Gerard O'Daly, *Augustine's City of God: A Reader's Guide* (Oxford, 1999; 2nd ed. 2020): book-by-book, the right map before Week 6. Hackett's *Political Writings*, trans. Michael W. Tkacz & Douglas Kries, intro. Ernest L. Fortin (1994; $22 paper), collects the *City of God* chapters on politics and the letters on war and coercion for Week 7.
- **Reception.** Hannah Arendt, *Love and Saint Augustine*, ed. Joanna Vecchiarelli Scott & Judith Chelius Stark (Chicago, 1996): her 1929 dissertation, with an essay on how it shaped her later work (see [Arendt](../../eras/05-20th-century/hannah-arendt.md)).

## 4. Reference

- **[SEP, "Augustine of Hippo"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/augustine/)** — by Christian Tornau (first published September 2019; revised April 2024), replacing Michael Mendelson's older entry "Saint Augustine": the map of the life, works and system. Per the learning path's SEP rule, read the opening sections before Week 1 and the rest after. There is no separate SEP entry on Augustine's free will or politics. The satellite entries, by week: ["Neoplatonism"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/neoplatonism/) (Christian Wildberg) and ["Plotinus"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/plotinus/) (Paul Kalligas) for Week 2, with [Plotinus](../../eras/01-ancient/plotinus.md) and [the late Neoplatonists](../../eras/01-ancient/late-neoplatonists.md) in this library; ["Ancient Skepticism"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/skepticism-ancient/) (Katja Vogt) and ["Cicero"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/cicero/) (Raphael Woolf) for the sceptical phase and Argument 8, with [the skeptics](../../eras/01-ancient/pyrrho-of-elis-and-the-skeptics.md) and [Cicero](../../eras/01-ancient/cicero-and-lucretius.md); ["Divine Illumination"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/illumination/) (Robert Pasnau) for Argument 10 and its medieval afterlife; ["Foreknowledge and Free Will"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/free-will-foreknowledge/) (David Hunt & Linda Zagzebski) for Argument 11 and its sequel in [Boethius](../../eras/02-medieval/boethius.md); ["The Problem of Evil"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/evil/) (Michael Tooley) for Arguments 4 and 9; ["Medieval Philosophy"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/medieval-philosophy/) (John Marenbon) and ["Medieval Political Philosophy"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/medieval-political/) (Cary Nederman & Alessandro Mulieri) for Weeks 6–7 and what came after, through [Anselm](../../eras/02-medieval/anselm-of-canterbury.md) and [Aquinas](../../eras/02-medieval/thomas-aquinas.md). Free; budget an hour or two per entry (an estimate).
- **[IEP, "Augustine"](https://iep.utm.edu/augustine-overview/)** — by Mark J. Boone: the plainer first pass. Its companion entries suit the night before a week begins: ["Augustine: Political and Social Philosophy"](https://iep.utm.edu/augustine-political-and-social-philosophy/) (John Mark Mattox) for Weeks 6–7, ["Just War Theory"](https://iep.utm.edu/justwar/) for Argument 14, ["Foreknowledge and Free Will"](https://iep.utm.edu/foreknow/) (Norman Swartz) for Argument 11, and ["Logical Problem of Evil"](https://iep.utm.edu/evil-log/) for Arguments 4 and 9. Free.
- **Allan D. Fitzgerald (ed.), *Augustine through the Ages: An Encyclopedia* (Eerdmans, 1999).** Signed articles on every work, person and doctrine, with the dates of each work. Look up a name or a treatise here first. A library book.
- **Possidius, *Life of Augustine*.** The only life by someone who knew him, written by the bishop of Calama soon after 430, with a list of Augustine's works. An English version is in F. R. Hoare, *The Western Fathers* (Sheed & Ward, 1954). Treat it as a witness, not a history.
- **Kenny, *A New History of Western Philosophy*, Vol. 2, *Medieval Philosophy* (Oxford, 2005)** — the learning path's history spine for Stage 2, alongside throughout. Peter Adamson's *Philosophy in the Hellenistic and Roman Worlds* (Oxford, 2015), the second volume of the book version of his podcast, has the Augustine episodes in print.
- **Augnet**, the Augustinian Order's portal once at augnet.org, did not answer this check (November 2026) and is not linked.

## 5. Media (all verified November 2026; assignments in [07-study-plan.md](07-study-plan.md))

Peter Adamson's *History of Philosophy Without Any Gaps* reached Augustine in January 2013, in its run on ancient Christian philosophy (eps. 101–119). That run sits at the end of the learning path's Stage 1 range (eps. 1–119), not in the Latin-medieval range (196–300) where Stage 2's "Listen & watch" looks for it. The assigned media below total about 5 hours 40 minutes. Listen after your own reconstruction, never instead of it.
