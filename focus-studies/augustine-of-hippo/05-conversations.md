# Augustine in Conversation — Backward, Sideways, Forward
**English** | [简体中文](../../zh/focus-studies/augustine-of-hippo/05-conversations.md)

*Focus study: Augustine of Hippo · [Dossier home](README.md) · [Library entry](../../eras/02-medieval/augustine-of-hippo.md) · [Study guide](../../study/augustine-confessions.md)*

Augustine wrote almost nothing that was not a reply. The *Confessions* answer the Manichees and the critics of his past. The *City of God* answers the pagans who blamed Christianity for the sack of Rome. His last twenty years answer Pelagius and his followers, and his final book, unfinished at his death, replies line by line to Julian of Eclanum. Later readers kept the argument going, and several built their own positions out of a quarrel with him. This file traces those exchanges. The scholarly controversies and the reception by era are in [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md).

The *Confessions* are quoted from Pusey's translation (1838), the *City of God* from Dods (1871), and other works from the Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers, all public domain; read the *Confessions* in Boulding or Sheed, as the [learning path](../../learning-path.md) advises. Works without a public-domain text here, such as *Against the Academicians* and *On Free Choice of the Will*, are paraphrased, and so are the letters, cited by their standard numbers.

## Backward: what he inherited and what he refused

### Plato, Plotinus and Porphyry

In Milan in 386 Augustine was given "certain books of the Platonists, translated from Greek into Latin" (*Conf.* VII.9.13), in the version of Marius Victorinus (VIII.2.3). Which treatises they were is disputed: Paul Henry argued for Plotinus, Willy Theiler for Porphyry, and most scholars now assume some of each. They taught him to think of God and the soul as incorporeal and of evil as a lack in something good (VII.10–16). The inward ascents of VII.10 and VII.17 follow the pattern of Plotinus's *On Beauty* (*Enneads* I.6). His verdict was double. The Platonists saw the goal and missed the road, and he was glad to have read them before Scripture so as to "distinguish between presumption and confession" (VII.20.26).

The *City of God* states debt and quarrel together: "It is evident that none come nearer to us than the Platonists" (VIII.5). It quotes Plotinus's call, "We must fly to our beloved fatherland. There is the Father, there our all" (IX.17, from *Enneads* I.6.8). Porphyry is praised for his search and attacked for tolerating theurgy, the purification of the soul by rites addressed to spirits (X.9–10, X.27–29). Porphyry had admitted that no philosophy offered one way of salvation for every soul, and Augustine answers that Christianity "is the religion which possesses the universal way for delivering the soul" (X.32). See [Plato](../../eras/01-ancient/plato.md), [Plotinus](../../eras/01-ancient/plotinus.md), [Porphyry](../../eras/01-ancient/late-neoplatonists.md), and the [Plato dossier](../plato/05-conversations.md) for the story from the other end ([04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Arguments 3 and 4; week 2).

### Cicero and the Academic sceptics

[Cicero](../../eras/01-ancient/cicero-and-lucretius.md) started him on philosophy. In Carthage in 373, in his nineteenth year, he read the lost *Hortensius*, and "this book altered my affections, and turned my prayers to Thyself O Lord" (*Conf.* III.4.7). Cicero also gave him the [Academic sceptics](../../eras/01-ancient/pyrrho-of-elis-and-the-skeptics.md). In Rome in 383 he half-believed that those "whom they call Academics, were wiser than the rest, for that they held men ought to doubt everything" (V.10.19). *Against the Academicians*, written at Cassiciacum in November 386, argues that a sage who assents to nothing cannot act or be wise, and that some things are certain: disjunctions, mathematics, and how things appear to me (III.10.23–11.26). It closes with the conjecture that the later Academy hid a Platonist teaching behind its doubt (III.17.37–20.43). The mature answer is *si fallor, sum*, "if I am deceived, I am" (*City of God* XI.26; Argument 8; week 5).

He argued with Cicero to the end. Cicero, he reports, denied God's foreknowledge in order to save free will, "and thus, wishing to make men free, he makes them sacrilegious" (*City of God* V.9). Augustine holds both (V.10; Argument 11). In XIX.21 he takes the definition of a people that Cicero gives Scipio in *On the Republic*, a multitude bound by a common sense of right and common interest, and concludes that by it Rome was never a republic (Argument 13; week 7).

### The Stoics

Augustine knew the [Stoics](../../eras/01-ancient/zeno-of-citium-and-the-stoics.md) through Cicero, Seneca and Aulus Gellius. He accepted their account of first movements: frightening impressions "move the soul even of the wise man," who does not consent to them (*City of God* IX.4, retelling Gellius). He rejected their passionless sage. Christ felt grief and anger, and a life without feeling is a kind of stupor (XIV.8–9). Virtue does not make this life happy, and the Stoic permission of suicide concedes as much (XIX.4). See also the [Roman Stoics](../../eras/01-ancient/roman-stoics.md).

### Paul

Paul is the partner he argued with longest. After the Platonists he took up Paul's letters (*Conf.* VII.21.27), and the garden scene ends with Romans 13:13–14 (VIII.12.29). The divided will of Book VIII reads his life through Romans 7 (Argument 5; week 3). In 396, working on Romans 9 in *To Simplician* I.2, he concluded that even the first movement of faith is God's gift; he later said he had laboured for free choice, and grace won (*Retractations* II.1). In 428–429 he named the verse that convinced him, "For what have you that you have not received?" (1 Corinthians 4:7), and called his earlier view "a similar error" (*On the Predestination of the Saints* III.7). How sharp this break was is disputed: Peter Brown dates a new Augustine to the 390s, while Carol Harrison argues for continuity with the early works (Argument 12).

### Mani and the Manichees

Mani (216–c. 277) taught in Persia that the world is a mixture of two eternal substances, light and darkness. Augustine was a Manichaean hearer for nine years (*Conf.* IV.1.1). He lost faith when the sect's bishop Faustus of Milevis came to Carthage in 383 and could not answer his questions on astronomy (V.6–7). As a priest he debated the Manichee Fortunatus at Hippo in August 392, and around 400 he answered Faustus's *Chapters* in the thirty-three books of *Against Faustus*. There Faustus denies teaching two gods: "It is true, we believe in two principles; but one we call God, and the other Hyle" (XXI.1). Augustine's reply is the metaphysics of privation (Arguments 3 and 4; weeks 1–2). The Manichees also handed Julian of Eclanum his best charge, that inherited guilt was Manichaeism renamed.

### Ambrose, Simplicianus and Victorinus

Ambrose, bishop of Milan, made the Old Testament readable for him by allegory. Augustine heard him "recommend this text for a rule, The letter killeth, but the Spirit giveth life" (*Conf.* VI.4.6). He noticed that Ambrose read silently: "his eye glided over the pages, and his heart searched out the sense, but his voice and tongue were at rest" (VI.3.3). Pierre Courcelle argued in 1950 that Ambrose's sermons of 386 drew on Plotinus. Ambrose baptized him at Easter 387.

Simplicianus, Ambrose's teacher and later successor, told him about Marius Victorinus, the African rhetorician at Rome and translator of the Platonists, who professed his faith in public and gave up his chair when Julian's law of 362 barred Christians from teaching (VIII.2.3–5.10). "I was on fire to imitate him," Augustine writes (VIII.5.10). The story supplies the shame that drives Book VIII (week 3).

### Tyconius

Tyconius was a Donatist lay theologian whom his own church expelled around 380. Augustine summarized his *Book of Rules* for reading Scripture in *On Christian Doctrine* III.30–37: "One Tichonius, who, although a Donatist himself, has written most triumphantly against the Donatists" (III.30.42). The second rule describes the church as one body with two parts, good and bad, mixed until the end. Augustine made it his reply to the Donatists and the model of two cities intermingled in this world (*City of God* I.35, XIV.28; week 7).

## Sideways: friends, rivals and opponents

### Jerome on lying and on Galatians

His longest learned quarrel was by post with Jerome in Bethlehem. Jerome's commentary on Galatians (c. 386) followed Origen: when Paul rebuked Peter at Antioch (Galatians 2:11–14), the apostles staged the dispute for their converts' sake. In Letters 28 (c. 395) and 40 Augustine objected that one useful lie in Scripture would make every passage doubtful. Jerome took offence and replied in 404 (Letter 75 in Augustine's collection), and Augustine answered in Letter 82 (405). *On Lying* (c. 395) defines a lie as asserting what one believes false with intent to deceive, and finds no licence for it in the New Testament: "the simulation of Peter and Barnabas is not only recorded, but also reproved and corrected" (*On Lying* 8). By 415 they were allies against Pelagius.

### The Donatists

The Donatist church split from the Catholics in 311–312 over a bishop consecrated by a man said to have surrendered Scripture in persecution. It held that sacraments given by unworthy clergy are void and that the true church is pure. Augustine replied that sacraments belong to Christ, whoever the minister, and that the church is mixed. He at first refused force. After the imperial laws of 405 he defended coercion, reading the parable's command to compel guests to come in (Luke 14:23) as licence (Letter 93, c. 408). Marcellinus presided at the Conference of Carthage in June 411 and found for the Catholics. Peter Brown treats this defence of coercion as the darkest legacy of the episcopate.

### Pelagius, Caelestius and Julian of Eclanum

Pelagius, a British ascetic at Rome, objected when a bishop quoted Augustine's prayer, "Give what Thou enjoinest, and enjoin what Thou wilt" (*Conf.* X.29.40), as Augustine himself reports (*On the Gift of Perseverance* XX.53). If God commands, the command can be kept. Pelagius's *On Nature* argued that human nature can avoid sin, separating what can be from what is: "It is one thing, says he, to inquire whether a thing can be" (*On Nature and Grace* VII.8). Augustine replied in 415 that such teaching renders "the cross of Christ of none effect" (VII.7). A synod at Diospolis acquitted Pelagius in December 415. A letter of Pope Innocent I (417), an imperial rescript of April 418 and the Council of Carthage of 1 May 418 secured his condemnation, which Pope Zosimus confirmed after wavering.

Caelestius, his younger associate, had been condemned at Carthage in 411 for teaching that Adam's sin harmed only Adam and that infants are born as Adam was before the fall. Julian, bishop of Eclanum, refused to sign the condemnation of 418 and was deposed. He became Augustine's ablest critic. God cannot justly impute another's sin; sexual desire is a natural good; freedom is the power to choose good or evil; and Augustine, he said, had never left the Manichees. Augustine answered in *Against Julian* (c. 421) and the *Unfinished Work against Julian*, still in progress when he died (Argument 12; week 5).

### Orosius

Paulus Orosius, a Spanish priest, reached Hippo in 414. Augustine sent him to Jerome, and in July 415 he accused Pelagius before Bishop John of Jerusalem. At Augustine's request he wrote the *Seven Books of History against the Pagans* (c. 417–418), a catalogue of disasters before Christ meant to show the Christian era better. Augustine never cited it. R. A. Markus (*Saeculum*, 1970) argued that the mature *City of God* rejects Orosius's providential Christian empire; the reading is standard, though some historians think the gap smaller.

### Volusianus and Marcellinus

The *City of God* began in letters. In 412 Volusianus, a pagan aristocrat and proconsul of Africa, sent questions about the incarnation (Letter 135). The imperial commissioner Marcellinus reported the objections of Volusianus's circle: an ethic of turning the other cheek cannot run a state, and Christian emperors had brought Rome its disasters (Letter 136). Augustine replied in Letters 137 and 138 and addressed *On the Spirit and the Letter* (412) to Marcellinus. Marcellinus was executed at Carthage on 13 September 413, despite Augustine's efforts. The *City of God*, begun that year, opens by naming "my dearest son Marcellinus" (I, Preface).

### The pagan critics after 410

Alaric's Goths sacked Rome on 24 August 410. Refugees reached Carthage, and the charge came with them: Rome had stood while it kept its gods. Augustine took it up in sermons and then in Books I–X. Those who escaped owed their lives to the churches the Goths respected, and "they ought to attribute it to the spirit of these Christian times" (*City of God* I.1). Rome's older disasters came under the old gods (II–III). Its empire rested on *libido dominandi*, the lust for rule, and "what are robberies themselves, but little kingdoms?" (IV.4). The *Retractations* name this charge as the work's occasion (II.43; Argument 13; weeks 6–7).

## Forward: the heirs and the critics

### Boethius

[Boethius](../../eras/02-medieval/boethius.md) asked readers of his *On the Trinity* (c. 520) to judge whether seeds from Augustine's writings had borne fruit in him. Augustine had argued that foreknowledge does not compel what it foresees (*City of God* V.10) and that God's years are a single today (*Conf.* XI.13.16). The *Consolation of Philosophy* (524) made this a theory: eternity is the complete possession of unending life all at once, so God sees our future acts as present without necessitating them (V.6). The reply in Argument 11 is largely his (week 5).

### Anselm

[Anselm](../../eras/02-medieval/anselm-of-canterbury.md) said the *Monologion* (1076) contained nothing at odds with the Fathers, above all Augustine's *On the Trinity*. The *Proslogion* (1077–1078) was first titled *Faith Seeking Understanding*, and its first chapter says Anselm believes in order to understand. Both go back to Augustine's use of the Old Latin Isaiah 7:9, that without belief there is no understanding (*On Free Choice* II.2.6; *On the Trinity* XV.2.2). *On Free Choice* II.3–15 also gives the shape of Anselm's proof: start from a concept, here truth, and show that something greater than the mind exists (Argument 10).

### Aquinas, and the Augustinians against the Aristotelians

[Thomas Aquinas](../../eras/02-medieval/thomas-aquinas.md) quotes Augustine constantly and revises him quietly. On illumination he says that Augustine, steeped in Platonist teaching, kept what agreed with the faith, and he reads knowledge in the eternal reasons as the light of the intellect God gives us (*Summa theologiae* I.84.5). Bonaventure kept a stronger illumination. In 1277 the bishop of Paris condemned 219 propositions, and Archbishop Robert Kilwardby condemned others at Oxford, including Thomas's thesis that a human being has one substantial form. William de la Mare's *Correctorium* (c. 1278) listed Thomas's departures from Augustine, and Dominicans answered with corrections of the "corrupter." Historians now use the label "Augustinianism" for the anti-Thomist side with caution ([06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md)).

### Petrarch on Mont Ventoux

Petrarch's letter *Familiares* IV.1, dated 26 April 1336, describes climbing Mont Ventoux. At the top he opened the *Confessions* his confessor Dionigi da Borgo San Sepolcro had given him and read X.8.15: "And men go abroad to admire the heights of mountains, the mighty billows of the sea," and "pass themselves by." He came down in silence. The scene copies the garden reading of VIII.12.29. Whether the climb happened as told is disputed; Giuseppe Billanovich argued that the letter was composed in the early 1350s as a literary piece. See the [Renaissance humanists](../../eras/03-early-modern/renaissance-humanists.md).

### Luther and Calvin

Martin Luther joined the Augustinian friars at Erfurt in 1505. His *Disputation against Scholastic Theology* (1517) opens by defending Augustine's anti-Pelagian works against the charge of exaggeration. In the preface to his Latin works (1545) he recalls finding that Augustine read "the righteousness of God" in Romans 1:17 much as he did, if imperfectly. The text is *On the Spirit and the Letter*, which calls it the righteousness "not that whereby He is Himself righteous, but that with which He endows man when He justifies the ungodly" (IX.15). For Augustine this righteousness is made in us; for Luther it is credited to us. *The Bondage of the Will* (1525), against Erasmus, replays Augustine against Julian.

John Calvin cites Augustine far more than any other Father in the *Institutes* (final edition 1559) and claimed him against Catholic critics such as Albert Pighius. Whether Calvin's double predestination is Augustine's doctrine is disputed. The Council of Trent's decree on justification (1547) kept Augustine's grace while affirming that free will is not extinguished.

### Jansen, Port-Royal and Arnauld

Cornelius Jansen, bishop of Ypres, died in 1638. His *Augustinus* (1640) made the anti-Pelagian Augustine the measure of grace against the Jesuit Luis de Molina, and the abbey of Port-Royal became its centre. Innocent X condemned five propositions said to be in it (*Cum occasione*, 1653). Antoine Arnauld granted that they were heretical and denied that they were Jansen's, separating the question of right from the question of fact. Pascal's *Provincial Letters* (1656–1657) defended him.

Arnauld had earlier brought Augustine to Descartes. His Fourth Objections (1641) set the cogito beside *On Free Choice* II.3.7, and Descartes answered that Augustine used the point for other ends; the [Descartes dossier](../rene-descartes/05-conversations.md) gives the exchange. *On the Trinity* complicates the answer. The mind is certain of itself, "although it is not at all certain whether itself is air, or fire, or some body, or some function of body. Therefore it is not any of these" (X.10.16). Gareth Matthews and Stephen Menn read this as an early form of Descartes's argument that mind is distinct from body (Argument 8; week 5; [Descartes](../../eras/03-early-modern/rene-descartes.md)).

### Pascal

[Blaise Pascal](../../eras/03-early-modern/blaise-pascal.md) wrote from the Port-Royal circle. His *Writings on Grace* (c. 1656) place the disciples of Augustine between the Calvinists and the Molinists. The *Pensées* build on Augustine's double picture of human beings, made great and fallen, with a restlessness reason cannot satisfy.

### Malebranche

[Nicolas Malebranche](../../eras/03-early-modern/nicolas-malebranche.md) joined Descartes to Augustine. *The Search after Truth* (1674–1675) argues that we see all things in God and cites Augustine's divine light, while noting that Augustine applied it only to eternal truths. Arnauld replied in *On True and False Ideas* (1683) that Malebranche had misread both Augustine and Descartes.

### Kierkegaard

[Søren Kierkegaard](../../eras/04-19th-century/soren-kierkegaard.md)'s *The Concept of Anxiety* (1844) quarrels with the doctrine of hereditary sin. Its pseudonymous author rejects any account that sets Adam outside the race as the cause of everyone else's sin. Each person is both an individual and the race, and sin enters each life by a leap that anxiety precedes. He keeps Augustine's question of how a good creature first wills evil (Argument 9).

### Heidegger

[Martin Heidegger](../../eras/05-20th-century/martin-heidegger.md) lectured at Freiburg in summer 1921 on Augustine and Neoplatonism, chiefly on *Confessions* X. The course, published in 1995 in *The Phenomenology of Religious Life*, reads the temptations (X.30–41) as structures of a life that is a burden and a care to itself. *Being and Time* (1927) keeps the trace. Section 9 cites X.16.25, "I am become a heavy soil requiring over much sweat of the brow," and section 36 cites X.35 on the lust of the eyes in its account of curiosity (Argument 6; week 4).

### Arendt

[Hannah Arendt](../../eras/05-20th-century/hannah-arendt.md)'s dissertation under [Karl Jaspers](../../eras/05-20th-century/karl-jaspers.md), published in 1929 as *The Concept of Love in Augustine* (English, revised, 1996), sets out three concepts: love as craving, love that returns to the creator, and love of neighbour. Her question is whether Augustine can love the neighbour at all. *On Christian Doctrine* defines enjoyment as resting in a thing "for its own sake" (I.4.4) and reserves it for God, so the neighbour seems loved for God's sake, as a thing used. Arendt found a firmer ground in shared descent from Adam. She returned to Augustine for natality, the human capacity to begin, citing "That this beginning, therefore, might be, the first man was created" (*City of God* XII.20 in Dods; XII.21 in other editions). The sentence closes the 1958 edition of *The Origins of Totalitarianism* (Argument 13).

### Wittgenstein

[Wittgenstein](../../eras/05-20th-century/wittgenstein-late.md)'s *Philosophical Investigations* (1953) opens with the Latin of *Confessions* I.8.13: "When they named any thing, and as they spoke turned towards it, I saw and remembered that they called what they would point out by the name they uttered." He draws from it a picture in which every word names an object, and spends the following sections dismantling it. §89 quotes XI.14.17, "What then is time? If no one asks me, I know: if I wish to explain it to one that asketh, I know not," as the model of a puzzle that needs a reminder of how a word is used. Maurice Drury reports that Wittgenstein called the *Confessions* possibly the most serious book ever written. The [Wittgenstein dossier](../ludwig-wittgenstein/README.md) treats the exchange in its [Argument 3](../ludwig-wittgenstein/04-arguments.md); here it bears on Arguments 1 and 7 (weeks 1 and 4).

### Camus

[Albert Camus](../../eras/05-20th-century/albert-camus.md) was born at Mondovi, near Bône, the modern Annaba, where the ruins of Hippo Regius stand. His thesis for the University of Algiers (1936), *Christian Metaphysics and Neoplatonism*, traced how Christianity absorbed Greek thought from Plotinus to Augustine. He presented Augustine as the meeting point, where Greek reason and the Christian sense of sin and grace stay in tension. His later refusal of salvation answers that tension from the other side.

### Husserl, Ricoeur and Russell on time

[Edmund Husserl](../../eras/05-20th-century/edmund-husserl.md)'s lectures on inner time-consciousness (given 1905, edited by Heidegger in 1928) open by saying that anyone working on time must still study *Confessions* XI.14–28. [Paul Ricoeur](../../eras/05-20th-century/paul-ricoeur.md)'s *Time and Narrative* I (1983) pairs Augustine with Aristotle's *Poetics*: *distentio animi* is time lived as discordance, and plot is the concordance narrative makes of it. [Bertrand Russell](../../eras/05-20th-century/bertrand-russell.md)'s *History of Western Philosophy* (1945) praised the theory of time as an advance on the Greeks and called the remorse over the pear theft morbid (Arguments 2 and 7).

## The themes trace

- [§2 Mind & Body](../../themes.md#2-mind--body--what-is-mind-and-how-does-it-relate-to-the-body) — The mind certain of itself and uncertain whether it is body. Week 5; Plotinus behind, Descartes ahead.
- [§3 God & Religion](../../themes.md#3-god--religion--does-god-exist-and-what-follows-for-faith-and-reason) — Privation, the proof from truth, grace. Weeks 2 and 5; the Manichees and Pelagius beside, Anselm, Luther and Jansen ahead.
- [§4 Ethics & the Good Life](../../themes.md#4-ethics--the-good-life--how-should-i-live-and-what-is-right) — Ordered love, lying, the divided will. Weeks 1, 3 and 5; the Stoics behind, Jerome beside, Arendt ahead.
- [§5 Politics & Justice](../../themes.md#5-politics--justice--how-should-we-live-together-and-who-may-rule) — Two cities, kingdoms without justice, just war, coercion. Weeks 6–7; Cicero behind, the Donatists and Orosius beside.
- [§6 Self & Identity](../../themes.md#6-self--identity--what-am-i-and-what-makes-me-the-same-person-over-time) — Memory and the self as a question to itself. Week 4; Plotinus behind, Petrarch and Heidegger ahead.
- [§9 History & Progress](../../themes.md#9-history--progress--does-history-have-a-direction-and-can-we-know-it) — History as the story of two cities. Weeks 6–7; Orosius beside.
