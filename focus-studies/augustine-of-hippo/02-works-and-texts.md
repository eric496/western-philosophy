# Works and Texts: The Corpus in Reading Order

*Focus study: Augustine · [Dossier home](README.md) · Library entry: [Augustine of Hippo](../../eras/02-medieval/augustine-of-hippo.md) · Study guide: [*Confessions*](../../study/augustine-confessions.md) · Arguments in full: [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md)*

Augustine left more writing than any other ancient Latin author. Over a hundred works survive, from short treatises to the twenty-two books of *City of God*. With them come about 300 letters, roughly 250 of them his own, and more than 400 sermons, besides the expositions of all 150 Psalms and 124 tractates on the Gospel of John. The usual estimate is five million words. Possidius, his pupil and biographer, wrote soon after his death that the writings were so many a student could hardly read them all (*Life of Augustine* 18). A line in the verses on Isidore of Seville's library, two centuries later, says that whoever claims to have read all of Augustine is lying. The attribution of the verses to Isidore is traditional.

Two facts shape how the corpus is read. Most of it is occasional: Augustine wrote as a bishop answering someone, whether a pagan critic, a schismatic church or a monk with a theory of grace. The arguments this dossier reconstructs usually sit inside a controversy and take their shape from the opponent. And Augustine catalogued and judged his own work, which gives the corpus a spine.

## The *Retractations* (426–427)

In 426 or 427 Augustine wrote the *Retractations* (*Retractationes*), "reconsiderations" in the sense of a second handling, not recantations. He went through his books in the order he wrote them, one chapter per work, saying when and why each was written, quoting its opening words, and correcting what he now thought wrong or open to misuse. Book I covers the works before he became bishop, Book II those after. By his own count he reviewed 93 works in 232 books. He never reached the letters and sermons.

Modern chronologies are built on it. It is also partisan. Augustine wrote it during his controversy with Julian of Eclanum, and when he reaches his early books on free will he defends them against Pelagians who quoted them against him (*Retractations* I.9). Use it for sequence and for Augustine's late view of his own development, not as a neutral account of what the early works meant. Its chapter numbers differ between editions; this file gives the traditional Benedictine numbers, which the nineteenth-century translations print. Several of those translations also print the *Retractations* chapter on a work as its preface, which is how some passages below reach us.

## The early works (386–395)

### The Cassiciacum dialogues (386–387)

After giving up his chair of rhetoric in Milan in 386, Augustine spent the winter at Cassiciacum, a friend's country house, with Monica, his son Adeodatus, Alypius and a few others. The conversations there became his first surviving books. *Against the Academicians* (*Contra Academicos*) argues against the sceptical New Academy, as Cicero had presented it, that a wise man can know some truths. *On the Happy Life* concludes that happiness is possession of God. *On Order* asks how evil fits a providentially ordered world. The *Soliloquies*, a dialogue between Augustine and Reason, ask what the soul can know of God and of itself.

The dialogues are polished pieces in Cicero's manner, and more serene than the *Confessions*. That contrast drives a long dispute. Prosper Alfaric (1918) argued that the Augustine of 386 was converted to Neoplatonism, and that the *Confessions* reads a bishop's faith back into him. Pierre Courcelle's *Recherches sur les Confessions de saint Augustin* (1950) answered that the Platonism of Ambrose's Milan was already Christian, so the two accounts describe one conversion. [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md) sets out the positions. The dossier paraphrases *Against the Academicians* in summary for Argument 8.

### *On Free Choice of the Will* (begun 388, finished c. 395)

*On Free Choice of the Will* (*De libero arbitrio*) is a dialogue in three books between Augustine and his friend Evodius. Augustine began it in Rome in 388 and finished Books II and III in Africa, after his ordination as priest in 391, by about 395. It starts from Evodius's question whether God is the author of evil. Book I traces evil-doing to the will's free choice and to inordinate desire. Book II argues for God from the existence of a truth above the mind, starting from the certainty that one exists, lives and understands (II.3.7). Book III takes up foreknowledge and the suffering of infants. Week 5 reads it in selection for Arguments 9, 10 and 11.

Pelagius quoted the work in support of his view of freedom. Augustine replied that it was written against the Manichees and left grace aside because grace was not its question (*Retractations* I.9), and he quoted that chapter again late in life (*On the Predestination of the Saints*, section 27). Whether the early book and the late defence agree is part of the dispute over the "early" and "late" Augustine.

### *The Teacher* and other early works

*The Teacher* (*De magistro*, 389) is a dialogue with Adeodatus, then about sixteen, on whether words teach anything. It concludes that words prompt us to look, and that what teaches is the inner truth, Christ, consulted within. *On True Religion* (c. 390) belongs to the same years. Neither is set reading; *The Teacher* returns beside Wittgenstein in [05-conversations.md](05-conversations.md).

## The anti-Manichaean works (387–c. 405)

Augustine had followed the Manichees for about nine years, and he spent the next decade answering them. *On the Morals of the Catholic Church and of the Manichees* (387–389) sets the two ways of life side by side. *On Genesis against the Manichees* (388–389) defends the creation story they mocked. The *Debate with Fortunatus* (392) records a public disputation in Hippo with a Manichaean priest. *On the Nature of the Good* (c. 399) is the clearest short statement of evil as corruption of a good nature.

The largest is *Against Faustus* (*Contra Faustum*, thirty-three books, about 400; dates from 397 to 402 are proposed). Faustus of Milevis is the bishop whose charm and ignorance *Confessions* V describes. When Faustus published an attack on the Old Testament, Augustine answered it section by section, quoting each before replying, so the work is the main surviving source for Faustus's arguments. Book XXII defends the patriarchs and Moses, and in doing so gives an account of when war is just (XXII.74–75), read in Week 7.

## *To Simplician* (396)

Simplician, who had guided Augustine in Milan, succeeded Ambrose as bishop there and sent him questions on Scripture. The two books of replies, *To Simplician, On Various Questions*, were the first work Augustine wrote as bishop. In Question I.2, on Romans 9 and God's choice of Jacob over Esau, he concluded that even the beginning of faith is God's gift. Late in life he described the moment: "I laboured indeed on behalf of the free choice of the human will, but God's grace overcame" (*On the Predestination of the Saints* 4.8, quoting *Retractations* II.1).

How far this was a break is disputed. Peter Brown's *Augustine of Hippo* (1967; new edition 2000) makes *To Simplician* the turning point between a hopeful early Augustine and the bishop of grace. James Wetzel's *Augustine and the Limits of Virtue* (1992) traces a more continuous development of his thinking on the will, and Carol Harrison's *Rethinking Augustine's Early Theology* (2006) argues that the early works already depend on grace. The dossier paraphrases *To Simplician* I.2 for Argument 12.

## *Confessions* (c. 397–401)

Augustine wrote the thirteen books of the *Confessions* in the first years of his episcopate; the usual dates run from 397 to about 401. The whole work is addressed to God, and its first paragraph states the theme: "Thou madest us for Thyself, and our heart is restless, until it repose in Thee" (*Conf.* I.1.1). *Confessio* in Augustine's Latin means praise of God as well as admission of sin.

Learn the structure first:

- **Books I–IX** tell the story from infancy to 387: childhood and the pear theft (I–II), Carthage, Cicero's *Hortensius* and the Manichees (III–IV), Rome and Milan (V–VI), the books of the Platonists (VII), the garden (VIII), and baptism, Ostia and Monica's death (IX). The narrative stops ten years before the time of writing.
- **Book X** turns to the present. Its centre is an inquiry into memory, the "fields and spacious palaces of my memory" (*Conf.* X.8.12), where Augustine looks for God and for the happy life everyone wants. It ends with the temptations he still feels as bishop.
- **Books XI–XIII** read the opening of Genesis. Book XI asks what God did before creation and what time is: "If no one asks me, I know: if I wish to explain it to one that asketh, I know not" (*Conf.* XI.14.17). Book XII treats formless matter and how many true readings a text can bear. Book XIII reads the six days as an allegory of the Church.

The *Retractations* says the first ten books are about Augustine and the last three about Scripture (*Retractations* II.6). Why a life ends in commentary on Genesis is an old question about the work's unity. One answer is that the last books generalise the story: the soul's formation, fall and return in I–IX is creation's in XI–XIII. James J. O'Donnell's commentary gives the fullest case for reading the thirteen books as one design. The historicity of the narrative is a separate dispute. Courcelle argued that the fig tree and the child's chant in the garden, "Take up and read; Take up and read" (*Conf.* VIII.12.29), are literary symbols; O'Donnell treats the scene as carefully crafted without ruling on what happened. [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md) covers both.

## *On Christian Doctrine* (396–397, completed 426–427)

*On Christian Doctrine* (*De doctrina christiana*) is a handbook for reading and preaching Scripture. Augustine stopped writing it at III.25.35 and finished it thirty years later, adding the rest of Book III and all of Book IV (*Retractations* II.4). Its first division founds a theory of meaning: "All instruction is either about things or about signs" (*On Christian Doctrine* I.2.2). Book I separates what is to be enjoyed, God alone, from what is to be used for the sake of that enjoyment, a distinction behind the ordered loves of *City of God*. Book IV adapts Cicero's rhetoric to preaching. It is not set reading; [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md) uses its account of signs and of use and enjoyment.

## *On the Trinity* (399–c. 420)

*On the Trinity* (*De Trinitate*, fifteen books) is Augustine's most speculative work. Before he had finished Book XIII, copies were taken without his consent, and he nearly abandoned it. The letter to Aurelius of Carthage that he set at its head explains: "I began as a very young man, and have published in my old age, some books concerning the Trinity" (*Letter* 174, prefaced to *On the Trinity*). Books I–VII set out the doctrine from Scripture and work through the logic of relations. Books VIII–XV search the human mind for an image of the Trinity and settle on memory, understanding and will.

Book X is the centre for this dossier. It argues that the mind knows itself with certainty even while it doubts what it is made of, because whoever doubts thereby lives, remembers, understands, wants to be certain, thinks, knows that he does not know, and judges (X.10.14). Week 5 reads X.10.13–16 for Argument 8.

## *The Literal Meaning of Genesis* (401–c. 415)

*The Literal Meaning of Genesis* (twelve books) is the fullest of Augustine's several treatments of the creation story. It reads Genesis as an account of what happened, against the allegory of *Confessions* XII–XIII. It proposes that God created all things at once, some in "causal reasons" that unfold over time, and warns Christians against defending readings of Scripture that contradict what is known of nature. It is the background to *City of God* XI–XII.

## *City of God* (413–426/7)

In August 410 Alaric's Visigoths sacked Rome, and pagan refugees in Africa blamed the disaster on the abandonment of the old gods. Augustine's friend Marcellinus, the imperial official sent to settle the Donatist question, asked him to answer. The answer grew over thirteen years into *City of God* (*De civitate Dei*), issued in instalments. Books I–III appeared by 413–414; Book XXII was finished in 426 or 427.

Augustine described the plan in the *Retractations*, in a passage Dods prints as the preface to his translation. He says that "this great undertaking was at last completed in twenty-two books", in two parts:

- **Books I–X** answer pagan critics. The first five answer those who say the gods must be worshipped for prosperity in this life; Book IV holds the comparison of unjust kingdoms to robber bands (IV.4), Book V the discussion of fate and foreknowledge (V.9–11). The second five answer those who worship the gods for the life to come, and include Book VIII on the Platonists and Book X on Porphyry.
- **Books XI–XXII** give Augustine's own account. Of these twelve, "the first four contain an account of the origin of these two cities", in the fall of the angels and of Adam. "The second four treat of their history or progress; the third and last four, of their deserved destinies" (Dods's preface, quoting *Retractations* II.43). Book XIX, on the final good and on peace, opens the last four.

The two cities are not institutions. They are "entangled together in this world, and intermixed until the last judgment effect their separation" (*City of God* I.35), and Book XIV names their principle: "two cities have been formed by two loves" (XIV.28). The work is long and digressive. Weeks 6 and 7 read about a sixth of it.

## The anti-Donatist and anti-Pelagian works

The Donatists were the majority church in parts of Roman Africa. They had split from the Catholics after the persecution of 303–305, over bishops who had handed over the Scriptures, and held that sacraments given by unworthy clergy were invalid. Augustine wrote against them from 393, in *On Baptism* (c. 400–401), *Against the Letters of Petilian* (400–403) and other works, and an imperial conference at Carthage ruled against them in 411. *Letter* 93 (408) and *Letter* 185 (417) explain why he came to accept imperial force against them. They are the texts behind the charge that Augustine justified religious coercion, treated in [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md).

Pelagius, a British ascetic teaching in Rome, held that God's commands presuppose the power to obey them and that Adam harmed his descendants by example, not by inheritance. Augustine's replies fill his last twenty years: *On the Merits and Forgiveness of Sins* (411–412), *On the Spirit and the Letter* (412), and *On Nature and Grace* (415), against Pelagius's book *On Nature*. African councils and, in 418, Pope Zosimus condemned Pelagius. The quarrel passed to Julian of Eclanum, the ablest opponent Augustine met, who charged that inherited sin was Manichaeism in Catholic dress. Augustine answered him in *Against Julian* (421–422) and in an *Unfinished Work* he was dictating when he died. A last group answered monks in Africa and Provence who feared that grace left no room for effort: *On Grace and Free Will* (426–427), *On Rebuke and Grace* (426–427) and *On the Predestination of the Saints* (428–429). Week 5 reads *On Nature and Grace*, *On Grace and Free Will* and *On the Predestination of the Saints* in selection.

## *Enchiridion* (c. 421–422)

The *Enchiridion*, or *On Faith, Hope and Charity*, is a handbook written for a layman named Laurentius and arranged on the Creed and the Lord's Prayer. Chapters 10–15 give the shortest statement of evil as privation: "For what is that which we call evil but the absence of good?" (*Enchiridion* 11). Week 5 reads them for Argument 4.

## Letters, sermons and the new discoveries

The letters run from 386 to 430 and include short treatises, such as *Letter* 138 to Marcellinus on whether Christian teaching weakens the state. The sermons were taken down by stenographers as he preached. They show the doctrines in ordinary speech.

The corpus is still growing. In 1975 Johannes Divjak found twenty-nine unknown letters in manuscripts at Marseille and Paris, published in 1981 and numbered with an asterisk (1*–29*). Most date from Augustine's last years. *Letter* 10*, to Alypius, describes slave traders seizing free people in Africa for sale overseas, and is now read beside *City of God* XIX.15. In 1990 François Dolbeau found twenty-six sermons, several wholly unknown, in a fifteenth-century manuscript in the Mainz city library, and published them in 1996. In 2008 six more turned up at Erfurt. Possidius's *Life* comes with a list of the works, letters and sermons in the library at Hippo, which, he says, survived the Vandal siege (*Life* 18, 31).

## A note on citation

**Confessions:** book, chapter and section. *Conf.* VIII.12.29 is Book VIII, chapter 12, section 29. The chapters come from the printed tradition; the sections are continuous paragraph numbers added by later editors. Many translations print only one, so "VIII.12" and "VIII.29" can name the same passage. The study guide cites book and chapter.

**City of God:** book and chapter, *City of God* XIX.13. The chapters are short enough that sections are rarely needed.

**Other works:** book, chapter and section where a work has books (*On the Trinity* X.10.14, *On Free Choice* II.3.7); chapter and section where it has none (*On the Predestination of the Saints* 4.8); chapter alone for the *Enchiridion*. Letters and sermons carry their number in the Benedictine series, or the discoverer's name (*Sermon* Dolbeau 26).

**The Latin editions.** The Benedictine (Maurist) edition of 1679–1700, reprinted in J.-P. Migne's *Patrologia Latina* (PL) 32–47, was the standard for two centuries and is still the only text of some works. The modern series are the Vienna *Corpus Scriptorum Ecclesiasticorum Latinorum* (CSEL, from 1866) and Brepols's *Corpus Christianorum Series Latina* (CCSL, from 1953). For this dossier: *Confessions*, CCSL 27, ed. Lucas Verheijen (1981); *City of God*, CCSL 47–48, ed. Bernhard Dombart and Alfons Kalb (1955); *On the Trinity*, CCSL 50–50A, ed. W. J. Mountain (1968); *On Free Choice*, CCSL 29, ed. W. M. Green (1970); the *Retractations*, CCSL 57, ed. Almut Mutzenbecher (1984); the Divjak letters, CSEL 88 (1981).

**O'Donnell's *Confessions*.** James J. O'Donnell's *Augustine: Confessions* (Oxford, 1992, three volumes) gives the Latin text with a line-by-line commentary. It is the reference work in English on any question about the *Confessions*, and O'Donnell put text and commentary online at [stoa.org/hippo](https://faculty.georgetown.edu/jod/conf/). A reader without Latin can still use the commentary, which is keyed to book, chapter and section.

**Why the translation matters.** Augustine wrote the Latin of a trained rhetorician, full of rhythm, wordplay and the Psalms, and translators trade sound against sense. The choice also shapes arguments. *Amor*, *dilectio* and *caritas* all come out as love in English, and the case for two loves depends on which is meant. *Concupiscentia* is wider than sexual desire. *Liberum arbitrium*, free choice, and *libertas*, freedom, name different things in the grace debate, and a translation that calls both free will blurs Argument 12. Read a modern translation with section numbers, and check key terms in O'Donnell or the CCSL text.

**The dossier's quotations.** The dossier quotes the *Confessions* only in E. B. Pusey's translation (Oxford, Library of the Fathers, 1838), *City of God* only in Marcus Dods's (Edinburgh, T&T Clark, 1871), and other works only in the *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers*, first series, ed. Philip Schaff (1886–1889), as printed at New Advent. Every quotation must be checkable word for word against a free public text, and these are public domain and online. The modern translations the dossier recommends are under copyright, so it paraphrases them and never quotes them. The cost is Victorian English, Pusey's "Thou" and "asketh" and Dods's long periods, and Latin texts older than the critical editions. Where a nineteenth-century rendering misleads, the dossier says so. *On Free Choice*, *Against the Academicians*, *The Teacher* and the *Soliloquies* have no text in the checked corpus and are paraphrased.

## The works in the dossier's reading order

The [learning path](../../learning-path.md) sets *Confessions* I–VIII and XI in Stage 2, with *On Free Choice* in selection if the stage goes well, and advises against starting with *City of God*. The dossier keeps that order and extends it. Its seven weeks, about 43 hours all-in, sit on top of Stage 2's own budget, already the path's most compressed; [07-study-plan.md](07-study-plan.md) gives the hours.

### Week 1. *Confessions* I–IV

I.1 states the theme; I.6–8 reconstruct infancy and how a child learns words, the passage Wittgenstein quotes to open the *Investigations*. II.4–10 is the pear theft. III.4 is Cicero's *Hortensius*, III.6–10 the Manichees, IV.4–9 the friend's death. **Difficulty:** low. **Place:** the restless heart and the problem of evil, set by a life.

### Week 2. *Confessions* V–VII

V.3–7 is Faustus, V.10 the Manichaean picture of God and evil as bodies, V.14 Ambrose and the sceptical phase. VII.1–5 shows Augustine unable to think of a substance that is not a body; VII.9 brings the "books of the Platonists"; VII.10 and VII.17 the ascent; VII.12–16 evil as privation. **Difficulty:** moderate; VII is the hardest of the first nine books. **Place:** the metaphysics behind everything later.

### Week 3. *Confessions* VIII–IX

VIII.2 is Victorinus, VIII.6 Ponticianus. VIII.5 and VIII.9–10 analyse the divided will; VIII.12 is the garden. IX.4 recalls Cassiciacum, IX.10 the vision at Ostia, IX.11–13 Monica's death. **Difficulty:** low to moderate; VIII.9–10 repay slow reading. **Place:** the will, and the end of the story.

### Week 4. *Confessions* X–XI

X.8–27 is memory and the happy life; X.30–41 the present temptations. XI.10–13 answer what God did before creation; XI.14–31 ask what time is and arrive at *distentio animi*. **Difficulty:** high. **Place:** the philosophical summit.

### Week 5. *On Free Choice*; *City of God* XI.26; *On the Trinity* X.10.13–16; *Enchiridion* 10–15; Pelagian works

*On Free Choice* I, II.3–15 and III.1–4, in Williams's translation. *City of God* XI.26 answers the sceptic: "For if I am deceived, I am." *On the Trinity* X.10.13–16 gives the fuller version, *Enchiridion* 10–15 restates privation. Then selections from *On Nature and Grace*, *On Grace and Free Will* and *On the Predestination of the Saints*. **Difficulty:** moderate; the Pelagian works are repetitive more than hard. **Place:** what the *Confessions* tells as story, stated as argument.

### Week 6. *City of God* I, IV–V, XI–XII, XIV selections

Book I on the sack of Rome; IV.4: "Justice being taken away, then, what are kingdoms but great robberies?" V.9–11 on foreknowledge against Cicero. XI–XII on creation, with XII.6–7 on the evil will that has no efficient cause. XIV.1–4, 13 and 28 on pride and the two loves. **Difficulty:** moderate. **Place:** the doctrines of the *Confessions* applied to history and politics.

### Week 7. *City of God* XIX; *Against Faustus* XXII.74–75; capstone

XIX.1–4 on the highest good, XIX.7 on war, XIX.13 on peace: "The peace of all things is the tranquillity of order." XIX.15 on slavery, XIX.21 and 24 on Cicero's definition of a people and Augustine's revision. *Against Faustus* XXII.74–75 gives the earlier account of just war. **Difficulty:** moderate. **Place:** the political philosophy, and the capstone essay.

## Editions

The rule follows the learning path: Boulding or Sheed for the *Confessions*, and one modern translation for each other work.

**The course texts:**

- ***Confessions*:** Maria Boulding (trans.), *The Confessions*, The Works of Saint Augustine I/1 (New City Press, 1997), clear and well annotated; or F. J. Sheed (trans.), *Confessions*, 2nd ed., ed. Michael P. Foley, intro. Peter Brown (Hackett, 2007), whose notes trace the quotations from Scripture and the classics. Henry Chadwick's translation (Oxford World's Classics, 1991), named in the library entry, is a sound third choice and the one most cited in scholarship.
- ***City of God*:** Henry Bettenson (trans.), *Concerning the City of God against the Pagans* (Penguin Classics, 1972; reissued 2003, intro. G. R. Evans); or R. W. Dyson (ed. and trans.), *The City of God against the Pagans* (Cambridge Texts in the History of Political Thought, 1998), with notes aimed at political theory. Both are complete.
- ***On Free Choice of the Will*:** Thomas Williams (trans.), *On Free Choice of the Will* (Hackett, 1993).
- ***On the Trinity*:** Edmund Hill (trans.), *The Trinity*, The Works of Saint Augustine I/5 (New City Press, 1991).
- **The Pelagian works, the *Enchiridion* and *Against Faustus*:** the New City Press volumes, Roland J. Teske's *Answer to the Pelagians* (I/23–26) and *Answer to Faustus, a Manichean* (I/20), and Bruce Harbert's *The Augustine Catechism* for the *Enchiridion*. For selections this short, the NPNF texts at New Advent also serve.

**The reference set** is O'Donnell's commentary, the CCSL texts, and the Latin–English Loeb volumes for readers with some Latin.

**Avoid as reading texts** the nineteenth-century translations the dossier quotes: Pusey's and J. G. Pilkington's *Confessions*, Dods's *City of God*, and most of NPNF. Their English is archaic, they rest on pre-critical Latin, and they bury the short plain clauses that carry Augustine's arguments, as the study guide warns. The dossier uses Pusey and Dods because they can be quoted and checked, not because they should be read at the desk. Avoid also abridged *City of God* editions that drop chapter numbers, and free online texts without section numbers.

## The reading order

1. **Week 1, the restless heart:** *Confessions* I–IV.
2. **Week 2, Milan and the Platonists:** *Confessions* V–VII.
3. **Week 3, the will and the garden:** *Confessions* VIII–IX.
4. **Week 4, memory and time:** *Confessions* X–XI.
5. **Week 5, free will, certainty and grace:** *On Free Choice* I–III sel.; *City of God* XI.26; *On the Trinity* X.10.13–16; *Enchiridion* 10–15; the Pelagian works sel.
6. **Week 6, providence and the two loves:** *City of God* I sel., IV.4, V.9–11, XI–XII sel., XIV.1–4, 13, 28.
7. **Week 7, peace and war:** *City of God* XIX; *Against Faustus* XXII.74–75; the capstone.
