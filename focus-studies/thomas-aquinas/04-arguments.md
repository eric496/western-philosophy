# Aquinas: The Arguments
**English** | [简体中文](../../zh/focus-studies/thomas-aquinas/04-arguments.md)

*Focus study · [Dossier home](README.md) · Library entry: [Thomas Aquinas](../../eras/02-medieval/thomas-aquinas.md) · The system these arguments build: [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md) · Study guide: [*Summa Theologiae* selections](../../study/aquinas-summa.md)*

Each argument below is reconstructed in numbered premises in the house style: what the argument proves, the reconstruction, its textual basis, the strongest standard objection, and the best reply, Aquinas's own where he gives one and the literature's where he does not, labeled as such. Where scholars disagree about what the argument even is, the readings are set out with names on each side.

References to the *Summa Theologiae* give part, question and article: (ST Ia Q2 a3) means the First Part, question 2, article 3; "ad 2" means Aquinas's reply to the second objection of that article; Ia-IIae is the first half of the Second Part. Quotations from the *Summa* come from the translation by the Fathers of the English Dominican Province (Second and Revised Edition, 1920), because it is in the public domain and anyone can check it online. It is not the edition to read. The learning path's rule stands: read Davies and Leftow, *Questions on God* (Cambridge Texts), with McDermott's *Concise Translation* for orientation and Feser's *Aquinas: A Beginner's Guide* alongside, and not the full *Summa* in the old Dominican translation. The *Summa contra Gentiles* (SCG) is cited by book and chapter, so (SCG I.13) means Book I, chapter 13. SCG quotations come from the English Dominican translation (1924), public domain and checked against the Internet Archive scan. *On Being and Essence* (*De ente et essentia*) has no public-domain English translation; it is paraphrased, and the few quotations give the Latin of the Corpus Thomisticum text (Baur's edition of 1933) with a plain English gloss of my own, labeled as such. Its chapters are cited by the numbering in Maurer's translation and most modern editions, in which the argument for the real distinction is chapter 4; the online Corpus Thomisticum text labels the same chapter "cap. 3".

Arguments 1 to 5 are the Five Ways of ST Ia Q2 a3, in Aquinas's order, and belong to week 2 of the study plan. Arguments 6 to 8 are the metaphysics that tells you what the Ways have reached: the distinction of essence and existence, divine simplicity, and the analogy of names. They belong to week 3. Arguments 9 to 16 then turn to creation, evil, the soul, knowledge, happiness and law. The library entry already reconstructs the First and Third Ways, and the study guide reconstructs the First, Third and Fifth; the versions here go further into the text and the disputes, and do not replace them.

One point governs all five Ways. Article 2 has just argued that God's existence can be demonstrated only from effects, and that in such a proof "we may take for the middle term the meaning of the word 'God'" (ST Ia Q2 a2 ad 2). Each Way therefore ends at something that answers to part of what the word "God" means: a first mover, a first cause, a being necessary through itself, a maximum, a governing intelligence. Whether that something has the other attributes of God is argued afterwards, in Questions 3 to 11. As the SEP article on Aquinas puts it, for all the Ways show by themselves the first cause "might itself be a body". Reading the Ways as complete proofs of the God of Christian faith is the first mistake to avoid.

**Argument 1. The First Way: from motion (ST Ia Q2 a3; SCG I.13)**

*What it proves:* that there is a first mover, moved by no other, on which every change in the world depends here and now. Aquinas calls it "the first and more manifest way" (ST Ia Q2 a3).

"Motion" (*motus*) here means change of any kind: change of place, but also change of quality and size. Aquinas defines it in the article: "motion is nothing else than the reduction of something from potentiality to actuality" (ST Ia Q2 a3). The vocabulary is Aristotle's (see [Aristotle](../../eras/01-ancient/aristotle.md)), and so is the argument, which SCG I.13 presents as Aristotle's own, from *Physics* VIII and *Metaphysics* XII.

1. Some things are in motion. "It is certain, and evident to our senses, that in the world some things are in motion" (ST Ia Q2 a3).
2. To be in motion is to be passing from potency to act in some respect: the wood that is potentially hot is becoming actually hot.
3. Nothing is brought from potency to act except by something already in act in that respect: "nothing can be reduced from potentiality to actuality, except by something in a state of actuality" (ST Ia Q2 a3). Fire, actually hot, heats the wood.
4. Nothing is in act and in potency in the same respect at the same time. So nothing moves itself in the respect in which it is moved, and "whatever is in motion must be put in motion by another" (ST Ia Q2 a3).
5. If the mover is itself being moved, it too is moved by another, and so on.
6. This series cannot go on to infinity, "because then there would be no first mover, and, consequently, no other mover; seeing that subsequent movers move only inasmuch as they are put in motion by the first mover; as the staff moves only because it is put in motion by the hand" (ST Ia Q2 a3).
Therefore "it is necessary to arrive at a first mover, put in motion by no other; and this everyone understands to be God" (ST Ia Q2 a3).

The whole argument turns on what kind of series premise 6 has in mind, and Aquinas says it elsewhere. In efficient causes, he writes, "it is impossible to proceed to infinity per se", as with the stone moved by the stick and the stick by the hand, but an infinity *per accidens* is not impossible, as when "an artificer acts by means of many hammers accidentally, because one after the other may be broken" (ST Ia Q46 a2 ad 7). In a series *per se*, which modern writers call essentially ordered, each later member acts only while and because the earlier one acts on it. In a series *per accidens* each member has its power in its own right, and the series can run back without end. The SEP article reports that commentators generally agree Aquinas means to exclude only an infinite series of the first kind, a "vertical" series, not a "horizontal" series going back in time. Aquinas held that a beginning of the world in time cannot be demonstrated (Argument 9), so the First Way cannot depend on one.

SCG I.13 gives the longer Aristotelian version and is candid about its assumptions. Paraphrasing Pegis's modern translation: the Aristotelian proofs presuppose that motion is eternal, which Catholics deny, and Aquinas replies that the proof is strongest on that supposition, since if the world had a beginning, a cause of the beginning is all the more plainly needed. The proofs also assume that the heavens move themselves, that is, have souls; Aquinas answers that the argument then reaches an unmoved mover one step later. The 1924 translation states the premise plainly: "whatever is in motion is moved by another" (SCG I.13). Joseph Rickaby's abridged version of 1905 renders it "Everything that is in motion is put and kept in motion by some other thing"; the words "and kept" are Rickaby's, and they build the sustaining reading into the translation.

**Textual basis.** ST Ia Q2 a3 (the First Way); Ia Q2 a2 (demonstration from effects); Ia Q46 a2 ad 7 (series *per se* and *per accidens*); SCG I.13 (Aristotle's two proofs and their assumptions). The library entry reconstructs the same argument in its Signature arguments, and the study guide does so in section (a).

**Objection.** There are two standard objections. The first is from physics. Newton's first law says a body in uniform motion keeps moving with no mover at all, so premise 4 is false of the very case, local motion, that Aquinas's Aristotelian sources had in view. The second is aimed at premise 6. Why can a series of movers not be infinite? If every member is moved by the one before it, every member's motion is explained, and nothing is left over that needs a first mover. Thomas Oberle (2022, as reported in the SEP article on the cosmological argument) argues that the defence of premise 6 runs together two different claims: that taking the first cause out of a finite series removes its causal power, and that an infinite series of this kind lacks causal power. The first is true and the second does not follow from it. A third, older complaint is the one the library entry answers: if everything moved needs a mover, what moved God?

**Reply.** The literature's, in the main; Aquinas supplies the materials. On physics: "motion" in the Way is any passage from potency to act, not just change of place, so inertia, a law about uniform velocity, leaves the argument's real subject untouched. What needs explaining is that some potency is being actualized now, in the wood becoming hot or the cell dividing. This is the line Thomists take, Feser among them. On premise 6: the reply from the per se series is that a member of such a series has its causal power only on loan. The staff has no power of its own to move the stone; it moves only "because it is put in motion by the hand". A series made only of borrowers, however long, never explains where the power comes from. Ross Cameron (2022, reported in the same SEP article) puts the point as a vicious regress: each member's efficacy is explained by the next, the explanation is never completed, and so nothing is explained. Oberle (2024) replies that the regress is harmless if the series is taken member by member and not as a whole. The SEP article states the current position: the dispute "hinges in part on the distinction between per se and per accidens series" and on whether appealing to it begs the question. On "what moved God?": the conclusion is a mover that is not moved, that is, not in potency in the relevant respect. It is not one more member of the series waiting for a mover, so the question does not arise for it. Whether such a mover is one, immaterial and unchanging in every respect is not settled by the First Way. Questions 3 to 11 argue it, beginning with Argument 7.

**Argument 2. The Second Way: from efficient causes (ST Ia Q2 a3; SCG I.13)**

*What it proves:* that there is a first efficient cause, itself uncaused, on which every other efficient cause depends for its causing. Where the First Way starts from things changed, the Second starts from things acting: from the ordering of agents, not from the patients they act on.

1. "In the world of sense we find there is an order of efficient causes" (ST Ia Q2 a3). An efficient cause is what brings something about, as the builder brings about the house.
2. Nothing is the efficient cause of itself, "for so it would be prior to itself, which is impossible" (ST Ia Q2 a3).
3. In an ordered series of efficient causes, "the first is the cause of the intermediate cause, and the intermediate is the cause of the ultimate cause, whether the intermediate cause be several, or only one" (ST Ia Q2 a3).
4. "Now to take away the cause is to take away the effect" (ST Ia Q2 a3). So if there were no first cause, there would be no intermediate and no last cause either.
5. If the series went on to infinity, there would be no first cause, and so no intermediate causes and no last effect, "all of which is plainly false" (ST Ia Q2 a3).
Therefore "it is necessary to admit a first efficient cause, to which everyone gives the name of God" (ST Ia Q2 a3).

SCG I.13 gives the same argument and credits it to Aristotle, *Metaphysics* II. The 1924 translation renders it: "In all efficient causes following in order, the first is the cause of the intermediate cause, and the intermediate is the cause of the ultimate" (SCG I.13).

Read quickly, premises 4 and 5 look circular. The 1920 text's "if there be no first cause among efficient causes, there will be no ultimate, nor any intermediate cause" is true when the series is finite, because then removing the first member removes a cause the others need. In an infinite series nothing is removed: every member has a cause. The argument works only if the series is the per se kind from Argument 1, in which an intermediate cause acts only while the earlier cause acts through it. Aquinas's own example of the other kind is human generation: a man generates a son "as a man, and not as the son of another man" (ST Ia Q46 a2 ad 7). The father's power to beget does not run through his own father, who may be long dead, so an infinite line of ancestors is not excluded. The Second Way is about causes whose causing is borrowed now, like the hand, the staff and the stone.

There is a dispute over what the effect in question is. On one reading the Second Way, like the First, concerns causes of change and of things coming to be. On another, favoured by Thomists who read the Ways in the light of *De ente* (Argument 6), the deepest series in view is the series of causes of a thing's being here and now, ending in a cause whose essence is its existence. The text of Ia Q2 a3 does not decide between them. The SEP article on the cosmological argument quotes Caleb Cohoe's description of Aquinas's causality, which suits the second reading: it "is primarily a vertical relation, not a horizontal one".

**Textual basis.** ST Ia Q2 a3 (the Second Way); Ia Q46 a2 ad 7 (the two kinds of series and the example of generation); SCG I.13 (the argument from *Metaphysics* II). The study guide lists the Way in its Q2 a3 section.

**Objection.** The strongest objection comes from David Hume (*Dialogues concerning Natural Religion*, 1779, part 9) and was restated by Paul Edwards; William Rowe named it the Hume-Edwards principle (all as reported in the SEP article). If each member of a collection is explained by another member, the collection is explained, and asking for a further cause of the whole is a confusion. In Hume's words, the uniting of the parts into a whole "is performed merely by an arbitrary act of the mind, and has no influence on the nature of things" (*Dialogues*, part 9). Bertrand Russell, in his 1948 radio debate with Copleston, added that the universe needs no cause; it is "just there, and that's all" (SEP's quotation). Applied to the Second Way: an infinite series of efficient causes, each causing the next, leaves nothing over that a first cause would explain. See [David Hume](../../eras/03-early-modern/david-hume.md) and [Bertrand Russell](../../eras/05-20th-century/bertrand-russell.md).

**Reply.** The literature's. First, the Hume-Edwards principle fits the series *per accidens*, where each member has its causal power in its own right, and that is the series Aquinas grants may be infinite. It does not fit the per se series, where no member has the power that is being passed along. Second, Rowe himself rejected the principle: "it is one thing for there to be an explanation of the existence of each dependent being and quite another thing for there to be an explanation of why there are dependent beings at all" (Rowe 1975, quoted in SEP). Duns Scotus had made the same point long before Hume: even if essentially ordered causes were infinite, "the whole series of effects would be dependent upon some prior cause" (Scotus on the first book of the *Sentences*, d.2, in the translation quoted by SEP); see [Duns Scotus](../../eras/02-medieval/john-duns-scotus.md). Whether the reply succeeds depends on whether there really are per se series in nature, and on whether a cause of the whole series must lie outside it. Critics, among them J. H. Sobel and Graham Oppy, have challenged the notion of a sustaining per se cause on which the reply depends. Like the First Way, the Second reaches a first cause, not yet a God: the step from "first efficient cause" to a simple, perfect, knowing being is taken in Questions 3 to 14.

**Argument 3. The Third Way: from possibility and necessity (ST Ia Q2 a3)**

*What it proves:* that not everything is able not to be; that something exists necessarily; and that among necessary things there is one whose necessity comes from itself and causes the necessity of the rest.

The argument has two stages. The first, steps 1 to 5, owes much to [Avicenna](../../eras/02-medieval/avicenna.md)'s argument from the possible and the necessary. The second, step 6, is needed because Aquinas thinks some created things are necessary in the sense used here: the angels, human souls and, in medieval cosmology, the heavenly bodies cannot cease to exist by any natural process. Anthony Kenny pointed out that "necessary" in the Third Way means this, being unable to cease to exist, and not the logical necessity of later philosophy (Kenny, *The Five Ways*, 1969, as reported in SEP).

1. "We find in nature things that are possible to be and not to be, since they are found to be generated, and to corrupt" (ST Ia Q2 a3).
2. Such things cannot always exist, "for that which is possible not to be at some time is not" (ST Ia Q2 a3).
3. "Therefore, if everything is possible not to be, then at one time there could have been nothing in existence" (ST Ia Q2 a3).
4. If at one time nothing existed, nothing would exist now, "because that which does not exist only begins to exist by something already existing" (ST Ia Q2 a3).
5. Things exist now. So "not all beings are merely possible, but there must exist something the existence of which is necessary" (ST Ia Q2 a3).
6. A necessary thing either has its necessity caused by another or not. An infinite series of necessary things caused by others is impossible, "as has been already proved in regard to efficient causes" (ST Ia Q2 a3).
Therefore there is "some being having of itself its own necessity, and not receiving it from another, but rather causing in others their necessity. This all men speak of as God" (ST Ia Q2 a3).

Step 3 is stronger in Latin than in the 1920 English. The translators wrote that there "could have been" nothing; Aquinas wrote *aliquando nihil fuit in rebus*, "at some time there was nothing in things" (this dossier's gloss). Either way, interpreters disagree about how the step works. On the temporal reading, step 2 relies on the principle that a possibility never realized in infinite time is no real possibility, so a world of perishable things with an infinite past would already have perished entirely. On the atemporal reading, defended by John Haldane (in Smart and Haldane, *Atheism and Theism*, 1996, as reported in SEP), the argument concerns the order of dependence among things now: if every member of that order could fail to be, nothing in it would account for the existence of the rest.

**Textual basis.** ST Ia Q2 a3 (the Third Way); compare SCG I.15, where Aquinas runs a version of the same argument to prove that God is eternal. The library entry reconstructs the Third Way and the quantifier-shift objection; the study guide does so in its section (b). The source usually cited is Avicenna's *Metaphysics* of the *Healing*; see the Avicenna entry.

**Objection.** The standard objection is the quantifier shift, pressed by Alvin Plantinga (1967), Kenny (1969) and William Rowe (1975) among others (SEP). Grant that for each perishable thing there is a time at which it does not exist. It does not follow that there is a time at which no perishable thing exists. Perishable things could overlap forever, each starting before the last one ends, as in a relay. Step 3 moves from *each at some time is not* to *at some time all are not*, and that is invalid. A second objection asks why the necessary being should be God. Bede Rundle argued that matter-energy, which is conserved, is necessary in exactly Kenny's sense, since it cannot cease to exist, while its arrangements come and go (SEP).

**Reply.** The literature's; Aquinas's text gives no direct reply. Defenders take one of three lines. (a) Defend the temporal reading with its supporting principle: if the whole stock of perishable things is perishable, then over infinite time its perishing is a real possibility that would by now have been realized. Critics answer that the principle is false, or that it begs the question. (b) Take Haldane's atemporal reading, on which there is no shift from "each" to "all" over time, since the argument is about what grounds the whole order of perishable things at any time. Critics answer that this reading turns the Third Way into the Second. (c) Accept the objection against steps 1 to 5, and note that step 6 does not need them, since Aquinas already grants that there are things necessary through another. Against Rundle, step 6 is the reply: SEP notes that Aquinas's second stage anticipates the move by asking whether a necessary being has its necessity from itself or from another. Matter-energy, a Thomist will say, is still a potency that is actualized in this or that form, and so not necessary through itself. The library entry's verdict holds: the Third Way is "best studied as an open argument, not a closed one".

**Argument 4. The Fourth Way: from gradation (ST Ia Q2 a3; SCG I.13)**

*What it proves:* that there is something which is best, truest and most fully a being, and which causes being, goodness and every other perfection in everything else. It is the most Platonic of the Ways and the one modern readers find hardest to take seriously.

1. "Among beings there are some more and some less good, true, noble and the like" (ST Ia Q2 a3).
2. "More" and "less" are said of things "according as they resemble in their different ways something which is the maximum, as a thing is said to be hotter according as it more nearly resembles that which is hottest" (ST Ia Q2 a3).
3. So there is something truest, best and noblest, and so "something which is uttermost being; for those things that are greatest in truth are greatest in being" (ST Ia Q2 a3, citing Aristotle, *Metaphysics* II).
4. "Now the maximum in any genus is the cause of all in that genus; as fire, which is the maximum heat, is the cause of all hot things" (ST Ia Q2 a3).
Therefore "there must also be something which is to all beings the cause of their being, goodness, and every other perfection; and this we call God" (ST Ia Q2 a3).

SCG I.13 gives a shorter version built on Aristotle alone: what is most true is most a being, and of two false things one is more false than the other, so there is something supremely true. The paraphrase here follows the 1924 translation. The pattern is older than Aquinas. [Anselm](../../eras/02-medieval/anselm-of-canterbury.md)'s *Monologion* argues from degrees of goodness to a supreme good, and the idea of things good by sharing in a good that is good of itself goes back through [Augustine](../../eras/02-medieval/augustine-of-hippo.md) to [Plato](../../eras/01-ancient/plato.md).

The perfections in the argument are not ordinary qualities like heat or size. Good, true and being are what the medievals called transcendentals: they belong to everything that exists, in some measure, and cut across Aristotle's categories. Heat and fire are an illustration from medieval physics, not the argument's subject. The metaphysics behind premise 4 is participation, which Aquinas states in ST Ia Q44 a1: "whatever is found in anything by participation, must be caused in it by that to which it belongs essentially, as iron becomes ignited by fire." Things that have being in a limited degree have it, not of themselves, but from what is being itself; so "all beings apart from God are not their own being, but are beings by participation" (ST Ia Q44 a1). That claim depends on Argument 6.

**Textual basis.** ST Ia Q2 a3 (the Fourth Way); Ia Q4 a2–3 (God's perfection, and how creatures resemble God); Ia Q44 a1 (being by participation); SCG I.13. The study guide lists the Way in its Q2 a3 section.

**Objection.** Three objections. (a) Comparison does not need a real maximum. One person can be taller than another without anyone being the tallest possible, and one number larger than another without a largest number. Richard Dawkins (*The God Delusion*, 2006) parodied the argument: people vary in smelliness, so by the same reasoning there must be a supreme stinker, and we call him God. (b) Premise 4 is false as a general rule. The hottest thing need not be the cause of other things' heat, and Aquinas's example rests on the physics of fire as the element of heat. (c) Premise 4 sits badly with Aquinas's own theology. He argues that God is not in any genus (ST Ia Q3 a5; see Argument 7), so God cannot be "the maximum in any genus".

**Reply.** The literature's, built on Aquinas's texts. (a) The argument does not cover every comparative. It concerns perfections that admit of a pure, unlimited case: being, goodness and truth, not height, number or smell, which are limited by their nature and come in degrees only because they are bodily. Keith Ward replied to Dawkins (*Why There Almost Certainly Is a God*, 2008) that every possible smell may be in God's mind, but a God with no body does not himself smell. The limited degree of a pure perfection, the Thomist says, shows that the thing has the perfection by sharing and not of itself, and what has a perfection by sharing has it from what has it essentially (Ia Q44 a1). (b) So understood, premise 4 is a claim about participation, and the fire is only an example. Defenders concede that it holds only where the lesser cases need a cause and nothing outside the "genus" could supply one, conditions that the transcendentals meet. (c) "Genus" in the Way is used loosely. Aquinas's own gloss on how creatures resemble God is that they do so "solely according to analogy, inasmuch as God is essential being, whereas other things are beings by participation" (ST Ia Q4 a3 ad 3). This reply makes the Fourth Way only as strong as the metaphysics of participation and analogy (Arguments 6 and 8). Critics who reject that metaphysics regard the Fourth Way as the least persuasive of the five to a modern reader.

**Argument 5. The Fifth Way: from the governance of things (ST Ia Q2 a3; SCG I.13)**

*What it proves:* that there is an intelligent being by whom natural things that lack knowledge are directed to their ends.

1. "We see that things which lack intelligence, such as natural bodies, act for an end" (ST Ia Q2 a3).
2. The evidence is regularity: they act "always, or nearly always, in the same way, so as to obtain the best result. Hence it is plain that not fortuitously, but designedly, do they achieve their end" (ST Ia Q2 a3).
3. "Now whatever lacks intelligence cannot move towards an end, unless it be directed by some being endowed with knowledge and intelligence; as the arrow is shot to its mark by the archer" (ST Ia Q2 a3).
Therefore "some intelligent being exists by whom all natural things are directed to their end; and this being we call God" (ST Ia Q2 a3).

SCG I.13 gives a version from John of Damascus that starts from the order of the whole, not from each thing's tendency: "It is impossible for contrary and discordant things to accord in one order always or frequently except by someone's governance" (SCG I.13, tr. 1924).

The Fifth Way is not the design argument of William Paley's *Natural Theology* (1802). Paley starts from complex contrivances, like a watch or an eye, whose parts work together, and treats them as artifacts. Aquinas starts from something simpler: any natural thing that acts in a steady way toward a definite result, as an acorn grows into an oak and not into anything else, or a heavy body falls. This is Aristotle's final cause, defended in *Physics* II.8 against those who said nature acts only by necessity and chance (see [Aristotle](../../eras/01-ancient/aristotle.md)). The end need not be intended by the thing that acts. The arrow does not aim at the target; it is directed. The argument asks what explains the fact that things without minds behave as if they aimed.

**Textual basis.** ST Ia Q2 a3 (the Fifth Way) and ad 2; SCG I.13 (the argument from the order of the whole). The study guide reconstructs the Fifth Way in its section (c).

**Objection.** Aquinas himself raised the modern objection. Article 3's second objection says that "everything we see in the world can be accounted for by other principles, supposing God did not exist. For all natural things can be reduced to one principle which is nature" (ST Ia Q2 a3, obj. 2). Modern science seems to make good on that claim. Since Darwin's *On the Origin of Species* (1859), the apparent purpose in living things has been explained by variation and natural selection, and the regular behaviour of bodies by laws of nature, with no director in view. [Hume](../../eras/03-early-modern/david-hume.md)'s *Dialogues* (1779) add that even if order needs a cause, the analogy with human makers gives no reason to think the cause is one, wise or good. On this view the Fifth Way reads human intention into processes that need none.

**Reply.** Aquinas's own reply to the objection is brief: "Since nature works for a determinate end under the direction of a higher agent, whatever is done by nature must needs be traced back to God, as to its first cause" (ST Ia Q2 a3 ad 2). He does not deny that nature explains natural effects. He claims that nature's own directedness is the thing to be explained. The literature develops this. Natural selection explains why some organisms and not others survive, but it works only because chemistry and physics already behave regularly, so that a given cause tends to produce a given effect. That regularity is the "acting for an end" the Fifth Way starts from, and so Darwin does not touch it. Thomists, Feser among them, press this line. Critics answer that this turns the Fifth Way into a request for an ultimate explanation of laws of nature, which a naturalist may decline to give, and that "acts for an end" is a metaphor for "behaves regularly". The dispute turns on whether directedness is a real feature of natural things, as Aristotle held, or a projection. On Hume's point, Aquinas would agree that the Fifth Way alone does not prove that the director is one or good. Those attributes are argued in Questions 6 and 11.

**Argument 6. The real distinction of essence and existence (*De ente et essentia* ch. 4; ST Ia Q3 a4, Q44 a1)**

*What it proves:* that in every creature, what the thing is (its essence) and its existence (*esse*) are distinct, the essence standing to existence as potency to act; that there can be at most one thing whose essence is its existence; and that such a thing exists and is the cause of existence in everything else. This is the metaphysics on which Aquinas's whole account of God and creatures rests.

The occasion is a dispute about angels. Some of Aquinas's contemporaries, following Avicebron (Ibn Gabirol) and, on the IEP's account, Bonaventure, held that every creature, even an angel, is composed of matter and form, since matter is the principle of potency and only God is free of potency. Aquinas denies that angels have matter of any kind. He then needs another source of potency in them, or they would be pure act and so equal to God. His answer is the composition of essence and existence. The IEP article on Aquinas's metaphysics divides the argument into three stages, and commentators disagree about which stage establishes the distinction as real rather than merely conceptual.

*Stage one: the understanding of essence.*

1. Whatever is not part of the understanding of an essence comes to it from outside, as something composed with it.
2. Every essence can be understood without anything being understood about its existence. *Omnis autem essentia vel quiditas potest intelligi sine hoc quod aliquid intelligatur de esse suo; possum enim intelligere quid est homo vel Phoenix et tamen ignorare an esse habeat in rerum natura* (*De ente* ch. 4). Gloss (this dossier's): "every essence or quiddity can be understood without anything being understood about its existence; for I can understand what a human being or a phoenix is and still not know whether it has existence in the nature of things."
3. So existence is other than essence, "unless perhaps there is something whose quiddity is its very existence" (*nisi forte sit aliqua res, cuius quiditas sit ipsum suum esse*; this dossier's gloss).

*Stage two: there can be only one such thing.*

4. A nature is multiplied in only three ways: by adding a difference (as a genus is divided into species), by being received in different matter (as a species is multiplied in individuals), or by being in one case absolute and in others received.
5. A thing that was existence alone, subsistent existence, could receive no added difference, since then it would be existence plus something else, and still less could it be received in matter.
6. So such a thing, if there is one, *non potest esse nisi una et prima*, "can only be one and first" (*De ente* ch. 4; this dossier's gloss). In everything else, existence is other than essence.

*Stage three: such a thing exists and causes existence in all else.*

7. Whatever belongs to a thing is caused either by the principles of its own nature or by something outside it.
8. A thing's existence cannot be caused by its own essence as efficient cause, for then a thing would bring itself into being, which is impossible.
9. So every thing whose existence is other than its nature *habeat esse ab alio*, "has existence from another" (*De ente* ch. 4; this dossier's gloss).
10. What is through another leads back to what is through itself, or there would be an infinite regress of causes.
Therefore there is a first cause that is existence alone (*esse tantum*), "and this is the first cause, which is God" (*Et hoc est causa prima, quae Deus est*; this dossier's gloss). And since whatever receives something from another is in potency to what it receives, a creature's essence is in potency to the existence it receives from God (*De ente* ch. 4).

The *Summa* restates the conclusions. God "is not only His own essence ... but also His own existence" (ST Ia Q3 a4). In God existence cannot differ from essence, because whatever has existence from another is caused, "But this cannot be true of God; because we call God the first efficient cause" (ST Ia Q3 a4). And existence is the deepest act of a thing: "existence is that which actuates all things, even their forms" (ST Ia Q4 a1 ad 3). The doctrine transforms Avicenna's distinction. Avicenna was read in the Latin West as making existence something that happens to an essence; for Aquinas, *esse* is the act by which an essence is real at all. See [Avicenna](../../eras/02-medieval/avicenna.md).

**Textual basis.** *De ente et essentia* ch. 4 (Corpus Thomisticum "cap. 3"); ST Ia Q3 a4; Ia Q4 a1 ad 3; Ia Q44 a1. The library entry states the doctrine under "Act and potency; essence and existence". The week 3 readings in the study plan begin here.

**Objection.** There are two. (a) Stage one proves too little. That I can understand what a phoenix is without knowing whether one exists shows that the concept of a thing's essence differs from the concept of its existing. It does not show a difference in the thing. The IEP gives the comparison: "Morning star" and "Evening star" are different concepts and the same planet. (b) A broadly Fregean objection, common among analytic critics of Aquinas's theory of being: existence is not a component of anything. To say that phoenixes do not exist is to say that nothing is a phoenix, not that a phoenix lacks an ingredient. On this view there is no *esse* to be really distinct from essence, and "God is subsistent existence" has no clear sense. Later scholastics also denied a real distinction: [Suárez](../../eras/03-early-modern/suarez-and-grotius.md) held that essence and existence are distinct only by reason, not in the thing (*Disputationes metaphysicae* 31).

**Reply.** Partly Aquinas's, partly the literature's. On (a), Thomists do not agree that stage one is meant to carry the weight. The IEP reports three readings. Walter Patt, Anthony Kenny and Steven Long take stage one to establish, or aim at, a real distinction, since a difference between the act of understanding what a thing is and the act of knowing that it is points to a difference in the thing. John Wippel and Joseph Owens deny that stage one is meant to establish more than a conceptual distinction. Wippel holds that stage two settles it, since there can be only one being whose essence is its existence and there are many beings. Owens holds that only stage three settles it, once a being whose essence is its existence has been shown to exist, and he charges Wippel's reading with an ontological-argument move from concept to reality. On (b), Aquinas has his own distinction ready. "'To be' can mean either of two things. It may mean the act of essence, or it may mean the composition of a proposition effected by the mind in joining a predicate to a subject" (ST Ia Q3 a4 ad 2). The Fregean analysis fits the second sense, the "is" of "there is a phoenix". It does not show that there is no first sense, the act by which this horse is actual rather than merely possible. Whether that first sense can be made clear is the question between Thomists and their analytic critics, and it is still open. Within Thomism, the library entry notes, "the real distinction between essence and *esse*" is itself a live debate.

**Argument 7. Divine simplicity (ST Ia Q3)**

*What it proves:* that God is wholly without composition: not a body, not matter and form, not a nature in a subject, not essence plus existence, not genus plus difference, not substance plus accidents. God does not *have* goodness, wisdom or existence; God *is* them.

Question 3 is the first of the questions that turn the conclusion of the Five Ways into an account of God, and its method is removal. Aquinas says at SCG I.14 that we know God by knowing what he is not, by "negative differences", in the 1924 translation's phrase. Articles 1 to 6 remove one kind of composition each, and article 7 draws the conclusion.

1. God is not a body, since the first mover is pure act and every body is in potency (ST Ia Q3 a1).
2. So God is not composed of matter and form (a2), nor of a nature and an individual that has it (a3).
3. God's essence is his existence, by the argument of *De ente* ch. 4 (Argument 6): otherwise God would have existence from another, "But this cannot be true of God; because we call God the first efficient cause" (ST Ia Q3 a4).
4. So God is in no genus (a5), since every member of a genus differs from the others in existence while sharing the essence of the genus, and has no accidents (a6), since an accident is a potency in its subject actualized.
5. In general, "every composite is posterior to its component parts, and is dependent on them; but God is the first being" (ST Ia Q3 a7).
6. And "every composite has a cause, for things in themselves different cannot unite unless something causes them to unite. But God is uncaused" (ST Ia Q3 a7).
Therefore God "is nowise composite, but is altogether simple" (ST Ia Q3 a7).

The SEP article on divine simplicity states the motive. A God who has properties as creatures do would depend on them, as a whole depends on its parts or as a thing that is F depends on F-ness. So divine aseity, God's being from himself, "would seem to require that God be rather than have his attributes".

**Textual basis.** ST Ia Q3 a1–8; Ia Q13 a4 (why the names of a simple God are not synonymous); Ia Q19 a3 (God's will and creatures); SCG I.14–27 (the way of removal, and God's essence as his existence at SCG I.22). For the doctrine's sources in Augustine, Boethius and the Islamic philosophers see [Augustine](../../eras/02-medieval/augustine-of-hippo.md), [Boethius](../../eras/02-medieval/boethius.md), [Avicenna](../../eras/02-medieval/avicenna.md) and [Maimonides](../../eras/02-medieval/maimonides.md).

**Objection.** Two objections are standard today. (a) Incoherence. Alvin Plantinga (*Does God Have a Nature?*, 1980) argued that if God is identical with each of his properties, and the properties are identical with one another, then God is a single property. But a property is abstract and causally inert, and God is a personal creator. So the doctrine makes God an abstract object (SEP). A related worry: if God's wisdom is God's power, "wise" and "powerful" say the same thing of God. (b) Modal collapse. On the doctrine, God is necessary and is pure act with no unrealized potency. His act of creating is identical with his essence, which is necessary. Then it seems he could not have failed to create, nor have created any other world, and everything that exists exists necessarily. R. T. Mullins (2013, 2016), Brian Leftow (2015) and others have pressed this; SEP reports that the claim that the argument rests on a simple modal fallacy "has not held up well under scrutiny".

**Reply.** On (a), the literature's, with a text of Aquinas. SEP notes that Plantinga's objection assumes a framework, properties as abstract objects, that medieval defenders of simplicity did not hold. Michael Bergmann and Jeffrey Brower (2006) and Brower ("Making Sense of Divine Simplicity", 2008) offer a truthmaker reading: "God is wise" is true because God exists, and God himself, not a property, makes it true. Eleonore Stump and Norman Kretzmann ("Absolute Simplicity", 1985) had earlier defended the doctrine against charges of incoherence. On the synonymy worry, Aquinas's reply is ST Ia Q13 a4: the names signify one thing, but "because they signify that under many and different aspects, they are not synonymous". On (b), Aquinas's reply is ST Ia Q19 a3. God "wills His own goodness necessarily", but other things only as ordered to it, and his goodness "can exist without other things" (a3 ad 2). The contingency lies in the creature, not in a defect of the divine will: "that God does not necessarily will some of the things that He wills, does not result from defect in the divine will, but from a defect belonging to the nature of the thing willed, namely, that the perfect goodness of God can be without it" (ST Ia Q19 a3 ad 4). Critics answer that this restates the contingency without explaining how one simple, necessary act could have been otherwise. SEP's survey of the replies ends in what it calls an aporetic postscript. Simplicity is the doctrine on which classical theists and their critics divide most sharply today.

**Argument 8. The analogy of divine names (ST Ia Q13 a5)**

*What it proves:* that words such as "good", "wise" and "being" are said of God and of creatures neither univocally (in exactly the same sense) nor purely equivocally (in unrelated senses), but analogically, so that we can speak truly of God while not knowing what God is.

The problem follows from Argument 7. If God is simple and in no genus, a word drawn from creatures cannot apply to him in the same sense. But if it applies in a wholly different sense, the Five Ways prove nothing about God, since their conclusions would use their terms in a different sense from their premises. Article 5 is Aquinas's way between the two.

1. Univocal predication is impossible. Perfections divided and many in creatures "pre-exist in God unitedly". When "wise" is said of a man it signifies "some perfection distinct from a man's essence, and distinct from his power and existence", whereas said of God it signifies nothing distinct from his essence, power or existence. Said of a man it "in some degree circumscribes and comprehends the thing signified", but said of God it "leaves the thing signified as incomprehended" (ST Ia Q13 a5).
2. Pure equivocation is also impossible, "Because if that were so, it follows that from creatures nothing could be known or demonstrated about God at all; for the reasoning would always be exposed to the fallacy of equivocation" (ST Ia Q13 a5). That would be against the philosophers who proved things about God and against Romans 1:20.
3. There is a middle way, the analogous, in which a term "signifies various proportions to some one thing" (ST Ia Q13 a5). "Healthy" is said of an animal, of medicine as the cause of its health, and of urine as the sign of it.
4. We name God only from creatures, and creatures are related to God "as its principle and cause, wherein all perfections of things pre-exist excellently" (ST Ia Q13 a5).
Therefore "these names are said of God and creatures in an analogous sense, i.e. according to proportion" (ST Ia Q13 a5).

Articles 2, 3 and 6 complete the doctrine. Aquinas rejects two rival views: that the names only deny something of God, "taught by Rabbi Moses", that is [Maimonides](../../eras/02-medieval/maimonides.md), and that they only name God as cause (ST Ia Q13 a2). "So when we say, 'God is good,' the meaning is not, 'God is the cause of goodness,' or 'God is not evil'; but the meaning is, 'Whatever good we attribute to creatures, pre-exists in God'" (ST Ia Q13 a2). The names "signify the divine substance, but in an imperfect manner, even as creatures represent it imperfectly" (ST Ia Q13 a2). Article 3 distinguishes what a name signifies from its way of signifying: as to the perfection signified, "they belong properly to God, and more properly than they belong to creatures", but as to their mode of signification they "do not properly and strictly apply to God" (ST Ia Q13 a3). Article 6 adds the order: as to the thing signified, the names apply "primarily to God rather than to creatures", but as to the imposition of the names, "they are primarily applied by us to creatures which we know first" (ST Ia Q13 a6).

**Textual basis.** ST Ia Q13 a1–6, esp. a5; Ia Q4 a3 (likeness of creatures to God); SCG I.30–34. The library entry's section "Analogy and divine names" states the doctrine and Scotus's attack on it.

**Objection.** The decisive objection came from [Duns Scotus](../../eras/02-medieval/john-duns-scotus.md), in his theological works around 1300. Unless one concept of being is common to God and creatures, Scotus argued, neither metaphysics nor theology is possible, because any argument from creatures to God will change the sense of its terms between premises and conclusion. An analogical term is either one concept, and then it is univocal, or several, and then it is equivocal. Scotus therefore held that "being" is univocal, while God and creatures still differ as infinite and finite (SEP, "Medieval Theories of Analogy"). On this view Aquinas's middle way collapses into one of the two options it was meant to avoid, and his own premise 2 then tells against him.

**Reply.** The literature's, and here the literature is divided about what Aquinas's doctrine is. The SEP article on medieval analogy reports that Aquinas's writings vary. In the *De veritate* he argued for the analogy of proportionality, in which God is to his wisdom as a man is to his. In the SCG and the *Summa* he returns to analogy of attribution grounded in causal likeness, the "healthy" model of Q13 a5. Some scholars (Bernard Montagnes, John Wippel) read this as a development. Others (Joshua Hochschild) hold that Aquinas uses each where the context calls for it. Cardinal Cajetan's treatise on analogy (1498) made proportionality the Thomist standard; Ralph McInerny argued that Cajetan misread Aquinas, and Lawrence Dewan and Hochschild have defended Cajetan against McInerny. Against Scotus, Thomist replies hold that an argument needs only enough unity of meaning to avoid the fallacy of equivocation, and that the order of analogy, in which the secondary sense is understood by reference to the primary, supplies it. Aquinas's own reply to the objection that "every equivocal term is reduced to the univocal" is that in causes it runs the other way: "all univocal predications are reduced to one first non-univocal analogical predication, which is being" (ST Ia Q13 a5 ad 1). Whether analogy is a stable middle or a promise Scotus was right to call in is still disputed.

**Argument 9. The eternity of the world cannot be disproved (ST Ia Q46 a2; *De aeternitate mundi*)**

*What it proves:* that reason can show neither that the world always existed nor that it began, so that its beginning is known by faith alone. This is a claim about the limits of proof, not about the world's age. Aquinas believed the world began. He denied that anyone could demonstrate it.

The question divided Paris. Aristotle argued in *Physics* VIII that motion and time had no beginning. Many theologians, Bonaventure among them, held that reason could prove the opposite. Aquinas stood between them. Q46 a1 answers Aristotle: his arguments are only "relatively, demonstrative" (ST Ia Q46 a1), good against early theories of origin but not proofs. Q46 a2 answers the theologians.

1. A demonstration starts from the essence of a thing. But an essence says nothing about when its bearer exists: "everything according to its species is abstracted from 'here' and 'now'" (ST Ia Q46 a2). So no argument from what the world is can show that the world did not always exist.
2. The only other source of proof would be the world's cause. But God creates by will, and reason can know God's will only "as regards those things which God must will of necessity" (ST Ia Q46 a2).
3. God need not will creatures at all (ST Ia Q19 a3), so whether and when he willed them to begin is not open to reason.
4. The arguments offered for a beginning fail one by one (ST Ia Q46 a2, replies 1–8). The main ones follow.
Therefore "By faith alone do we hold, and by no demonstration can it be proved, that the world did not always exist" (ST Ia Q46 a2).

The replies carry most of the article. Against the claim that an infinite past cannot have been crossed to reach today (objection 6), Aquinas answers: "Whatever bygone day we choose, from it to the present day there is a finite number of days which can be passed through" (ST Ia Q46 a2 ad 6). Against the claim that an eternal world would need an infinite series of causes (objection 7), he uses the distinction behind the Second Way. An essentially ordered series cannot go to infinity, but an accidentally ordered one can: a smith may work with one hammer after another, and each father begets "as a man, and not as the son of another man" (ST Ia Q46 a2 ad 7). Against the claim that an eternal world would equal God (objection 5), he follows Boethius: an endless world would still pass through time, while God's being is "all being simultaneously without succession" (ST Ia Q46 a2 ad 5).

The short treatise *De aeternitate mundi* (c. 1271) goes further. Q46 shows only that no proof of a beginning works. The treatise argues that a created and beginningless world involves no contradiction. "In hoc ergo tota consistit quaestio, utrum esse creatum a Deo secundum totam substantiam, et non habere durationis principium, repugnent ad invicem, vel non" (*De aeternitate mundi*, Leonine ed.). Gloss (this dossier's): "So the whole question turns on this: whether being created by God in one's whole substance and having no beginning of duration are incompatible." He argues that they are not. A cause that acts instantly need not precede its effect in time, as the sun lights the air the moment the sun is there. And "made from nothing" can mean *not from anything*, with a priority of nature, not of time: left to itself a creature would be nothing, but it need never have been nothing. The treatise ends its survey of authorities with a jibe at opponents who see the contradiction the great philosophers missed: "Ergo illi qui tam subtiliter eam percipiunt, soli sunt homines, et cum illis oritur sapientia" (*De aeternitate mundi*). Gloss (this dossier's): "So those who perceive it so subtly are the only men, and wisdom is born with them", an echo of Job 12:2.

He also says why the point matters. Bad proofs harm the faith: they give "occasion to unbelievers to laugh, thinking that on such grounds we believe things that are of faith" (ST Ia Q46 a2).

**Textual basis.** ST Ia Q46 a1 (Aristotle's arguments), a2 (body and replies 1–8); ST Ia Q19 a3; *De aeternitate mundi* (Latin, Corpus Thomisticum, Leonine text; read in English in a modern translation such as Vollert's, not quoted here); SCG II.31–38 treats the same ground. The [library entry](../../eras/02-medieval/thomas-aquinas.md) notes that Aquinas follows Maimonides here; see [Maimonides](../../eras/02-medieval/maimonides.md).

**Objection.** Bonaventure's, from the theologians' side (*Commentary on the Sentences* II d.1 p.1 a.1 q.2, as reported in the SEP's "Saint Bonaventure"). Whatever depends wholly on another for its being is made from nothing. If the world is made from nothing, and not from nothing as from a material, then it is made from nothing as from a starting point: it has being *after* non-being. Nothing that has being after non-being can be eternal. So *eternal and created from nothing* is a contradiction, and Bonaventure thought no philosopher, however slight, had held it. He also thought the difficulties of an infinite past put a beginning within reach of natural reason. Arguments of that kind appear in Q46 a2 as objections 6 and 8: an infinite past could not have been crossed, and an eternal world would contain an actually infinite number of human souls. The crossing argument has a modern descendant in William Lane Craig's *kalām* cosmological argument (*The Kalām Cosmological Argument*, 1979).

**Reply.** Aquinas's own, in *De aeternitate mundi*. *After nothing* names an order, and order can be of nature as well as of time. A creature's non-being comes first by nature, because existence is something it has only from another; it need not come first in time. On the crossing argument, ad 6 holds that every crossed interval is finite. Craig and others reply that the problem is the whole past series, not any one interval, so the debate continues among philosophers of time. On the souls argument, Aquinas's answers differ between texts. In ST Ia Q46 a2 ad 8 he grants that an actual infinite is impossible (citing Ia Q7 a4) and says the argument covers only one case, since God could have made an eternal world without men. In the later treatise he adds: "non est adhuc demonstratum, quod Deus non possit facere ut sint infinita actu" (*De aeternitate mundi*). Gloss (this dossier's): "it has not yet been demonstrated that God cannot make things that are actually infinite." The shift is a known puzzle for interpreters. The result in either text is the measured one the SEP describes: no demonstration is possible either way.

**Argument 10. Evil as privation (ST Ia Q48–49)**

*What it proves:* that evil is the absence of a good that a thing ought to have, that only good can cause it, and that God causes the corruption of natural things incidentally, for the sake of the order of the whole, but is in no way the cause of moral fault.

Aquinas inherits the privation theory from Augustine (see Argument 4 of the [Augustine dossier](../augustine-of-hippo/04-arguments.md)) and gives it a sharper form. The two questions sit inside the treatise on creation, after Q47 on the distinction of things, because the question is whether a universe made by a good God can contain evil.

1. Good is what is desirable, and every nature desires its own being and perfection. So being and perfection are good (ST Ia Q48 a1).
2. Evil is the opposite of good. So evil cannot be a being, a form or a nature: "it must be that by the name of evil is signified the absence of good" (ST Ia Q48 a1).
3. Not every absence is evil. "Absence of good, taken negatively, is not evil"; otherwise "a man would be evil who had not the swiftness of the roe, or the strength of a lion" (ST Ia Q48 a3). Evil is an absence taken privatively, the lack of a good a thing should have, as "the privation of sight is called blindness" (ST Ia Q48 a3).
4. A privation exists only in a subject. So evil exists only in something good, and it can never wholly consume the good it inhabits (ST Ia Q48 a3–4).
5. Every evil has a cause, but "only good can be a cause; because nothing can be a cause except inasmuch as it is a being, and every being, as such, is good" (ST Ia Q49 a1). Good causes evil only incidentally, by a defect in the agent or by producing a form that excludes another (ST Ia Q49 a1).
6. There is no defect in God. So the evil that lies in a defective action, which includes all sin, does not come from God (ST Ia Q49 a2).
Therefore evil is real but not a thing; it is caused only by goods acting incidentally; and "there is no one first principle of evil, as there is one first principle of good" (ST Ia Q49 a3). The last clause is aimed at the Manichees.

Two distinctions do new work. The first divides evil into pain (*poena*) and fault (*culpa*). An evil of pain removes a thing's form or integrity, as blindness does. An evil of fault is the lack of due order in a voluntary action (ST Ia Q48 a5). Fault is the worse of the two (ST Ia Q48 a6). The second distinction explains how God relates to each. God intends the good of the order of the universe, and that order requires corruptible things, some of which in fact fail. So God, "consequently and as it were by accident, causes the corruptions of things" (ST Ia Q49 a2). The order of justice also requires punishment. Hence "God is the author of the evil which is penalty, but not of the evil which is fault" (ST Ia Q49 a2). The image for sin is a limp: the motion in limping comes from the power to move, while "what there is of obliqueness in it does not come from the motive power, but from the curvature of the leg" (ST Ia Q49 a2 ad 2). Whatever is real in a sinful act comes from God; its defect comes from the creature.

Q48 a2 adds the reason God allows any of this. A universe with every grade of goodness must contain goods that can fail, "and thence it follows that sometimes they do fail" (ST Ia Q48 a2). "Hence many good things would be taken away if God permitted no evil to exist; for fire would not be generated if air was not corrupted, nor would the life of a lion be preserved unless the ass were killed" (ST Ia Q48 a2 ad 3).

**Textual basis.** ST Ia Q48 a1–6; Q49 a1–3; with Q47 a2 (inequality in things) and Q22 a2 ad 2 (providence and defect). The fullest treatment is the *Disputed Questions on Evil* (*De malo*, 1270–71), whose q.1 covers the same ground.

**Objection.** The objections to Augustine's version apply here too (see Argument 4 of the Augustine dossier): pain and cruelty seem positive, not mere lacks, and J. L. Mackie and William Rowe argue that a theory of what evil *is* does nothing to explain why an omnipotent good God permits it. Aquinas's version adds a target of its own. The ad 3 of Q48 a2 justifies the ass's death by the lion's life and calls the whole universe better for containing failure. That is an aesthetic theodicy. It seems to treat a creature's suffering as a cost the whole may properly impose on a part, and Rowe's fawn dying in a forest fire is just the case it struggles with. The penalty clause adds a moral worry: if God is "the author of the evil which is penalty", then much suffering is God's direct work.

**Reply.** The literature's, which divides. Brian Davies (*The Reality of God and the Problem of Evil*, 2006) argues that Aquinas's God is not a moral agent among others who could be blamed for failing a duty, so Rowe's argument, which treats God as such an agent, misses its target; Aquinas needs only to show that God causes no evil *as such*, which Q49 a2 claims. Eleonore Stump (*Wandering in Darkness*, 2010) rejects that line and gives a defence centred on persons, not on the whole: on her reading the goods that justify suffering must be goods for the sufferer, chiefly union with God, so the lion-and-ass passage is not the core of the theodicy. On the penalty clause, Aquinas's own reply is that pain is a lesser evil than fault and is inflicted to prevent it, as "the surgeon cuts off a limb to save the whole body" (ST Ia Q48 a6). He quotes Dionysius: "To be punished is not an evil; but it is an evil to be made worthy of punishment" (ST Ia Q48 a6). Whether either reply meets the evidential argument is still disputed.

**Argument 11. The soul as the form of the body; the unity of substantial form (ST Ia Q76 a1, a4)**

*What it proves:* that the intellectual soul is the substantial form of the human body, and that a human being has exactly one substantial form, the soul, which does everything lower forms do in other things. A human being is one thing, not a soul using a body.

Q75 has already argued that the soul is "a principle both incorporeal and subsistent" (ST Ia Q75 a2), because thinking is not done through a bodily organ. Q76 then has to show that so independent a soul is still the form of a body. Aquinas fights on two fronts: against Plato, who in his reading made the person a soul that uses a body, and against Averroes, who in his reading linked one separate intellect to each human only through that person's images.

The argument of a1 runs from the experience of thinking.

1. Whatever a thing primarily acts by is its form, since "nothing acts except so far as it is in act" (ST Ia Q76 a1).
2. The soul is what we primarily live, sense, move and understand by (ST Ia Q76 a1).
3. Each person is aware that it is the same person who understands and who senses: "each one is conscious that it is himself who understands" (ST Ia Q76 a1). Sensing needs a body, so the body is part of the one who understands; Plato's view that the person is the soul alone fails (ST Ia Q76 a1, citing Q75 a4).
4. Averroes' link through images does not make the thinking *this person's*. Images in a person are related to the intellect as colours are to sight, and the wall whose colours are seen does not see: "we do not say that the wall sees, but rather that it is seen" (ST Ia Q76 a1). On Averroes' view the person is understood, not understanding.
5. Nor can the intellect be joined to the body only as a mover to what it moves, for then thinking would belong to the person only as a carpenter's act belongs to the saw (ST Ia Q76 a1).
Therefore "There remains, therefore, no other explanation than that given by Aristotle—namely, that this particular man understands, because the intellectual principle is his form" (ST Ia Q76 a1).

The soul is a form of an unusual kind. It "excels corporeal matter in its power by the fact that it has an operation and a power in which corporeal matter has no share whatever" (ST Ia Q76 a1). It also gives its own existence to the body, so that "the existence of the whole composite is also the existence of the soul", which is why it "retains its own existence after the dissolution of the body" (ST Ia Q76 a1 ad 5).

Article 4 adds the unity thesis.

1. "Of one thing there is but one substantial being. But the substantial form gives substantial being. Therefore of one thing there is but one substantial form" (ST Ia Q76 a4, *sed contra*).
2. An accidental form makes a thing be *such*; a substantial form makes it *be*, without qualification. Its arrival is generation and its departure corruption without qualification (ST Ia Q76 a4).
3. Suppose some earlier substantial form, such as a form of bodiliness, already made the body an actual being. Then the soul would come to an existing thing and would make it only *such*. The soul would be an accidental form, and death would be only a change in a body that remains (ST Ia Q76 a4).
Therefore "there is no other substantial form in man besides the intellectual soul", and the soul "virtually contains" the nutritive and sensitive souls and all lower forms (ST Ia Q76 a4).

**Textual basis.** ST Ia Q75 a2, a4–6; Q76 a1 (body and replies), a3 (one soul, not three), a4; *Disputed Questions on the Soul* (*De anima*) q.1–2, q.11; SCG II.56–72. The [library entry](../../eras/02-medieval/thomas-aquinas.md)'s section "Soul as form of the body" gives the summary and the consequence that full personal survival requires resurrection.

**Objection.** The pluralists'. Most of Aquinas's contemporaries held that a living body has more than one substantial form: at least a form of bodiliness and a soul. Their main argument, as the SEP's "Thomas Aquinas" (§5) reports, was that pluralism explains how a living thing's body can persist after the soul leaves at death. On Aquinas's view the corpse is not the same body at all, which seems plainly false: the body on the bed is the body that was alive a minute ago. The dispute was not academic. In March 1277 Archbishop Robert Kilwardby condemned, at Oxford, theses that included Aquinas's unity of substantial form, and the Franciscan William de la Mare's *Correctorium fratris Thomae* attacked it (SEP, §9). A second objection presses from the other side: if the soul subsists on its own and survives death, it is hard to see how it is a form at all, as opposed to a Cartesian thing that happens to be lodged in a body.

**Reply.** To the pluralists, Aquinas's own reply is to bite the bullet: the persistence of the body is only apparent, and strictly what endures through a substance's corruption is prime matter (SEP, §5). Apparent sameness is explained by the qualities of the new mixture, which resemble those of the living body, as the forms of the elements remain in a compound "not actually but virtually" (ST Ia Q76 a4 ad 4). His Dominican followers defended the view in the replies titled *Correctorium corruptorii Thomae* (SEP, §9). To the second objection, the reply turns on Q76 a1 ad 5–6: the soul is a form whose being is shared with the body, and when separated it keeps "an aptitude and a natural inclination to be united to the body" (ST Ia Q76 a1 ad 6). It is an incomplete part, not a complete substance. How incomplete is a live dispute. "Corruptionists" (Patrick Toner, 2009; Christina Van Dyke, 2012) read Aquinas as holding that the human person does not exist between death and resurrection, since only the soul does. "Survivalists" (Eleonore Stump, 2022; Christopher Brown, 2007; Jeffrey Brower, 2014) hold that the person survives, constituted for a time by the soul alone. Both sides are reported in the SEP's §5.

**Argument 12. Against the unity of the intellect (ST Ia Q76 a2; *De unitate intellectus*)**

*What it proves:* that each human being has his or her own intellect, so that the possible intellect, the power that receives intelligible forms, is not one separate substance shared by the whole species. The case is made from Aristotle's principles and from what it is for a person to think, not from faith.

Averroes, in the *Long Commentary on the De Anima*, held that the possible intellect is a single separate substance for all humans, joined to each person only through that person's images. The library's [Averroes](../../eras/02-medieval/averroes.md) entry gives his reasons. Some masters in the Paris arts faculty, the Latin Averroists of the library's Averroes entry, defended the view; Siger of Brabant is usually named as Aquinas's main target, though the treatise names no living opponent. Aquinas wrote *De unitate intellectus contra Averroistas* in 1270. The SEP's "Thomas Aquinas" and the library entry both date it to that year.

The *Summa* argument of Q76 a2 is a trilemma: the single intellect fails however it is joined to us.

1. If, as Plato held in Aquinas's reading, the person is the intellect, one intellect would make Socrates and Plato one man, differing only as "one man with a tunic and another with a cloak; which is quite absurd" (ST Ia Q76 a2).
2. If, as Aristotle held, the intellect is a power of the soul that is the form of the body, then one intellect for many is impossible, since "it is impossible for many distinct individuals to have one form, as it is impossible for them to have one existence" (ST Ia Q76 a2).
3. However it is joined, the intellect is the principal agent in a person, and the senses serve it. One principal agent with its own act means one agent and one act. So "all men are but one 'understander,' and have but one act of understanding, in regard, that is, of one intelligible object" (ST Ia Q76 a2).
4. Different images in different people cannot divide the act, since from many images of a stone one intellect abstracts only one intelligible species (ST Ia Q76 a2).
Therefore "it is altogether impossible and unreasonable to maintain that there exists one intellect for all men" (ST Ia Q76 a2).

The treatise makes the same point with a stone: if all humans understand by one intellect, then "si ego intelligo lapidem et tu similiter, oportebit quod una et eadem sit intellectualis operatio et mei et tui" (*De unitate intellectus* ch. 4). Gloss (this dossier's): "If I understand a stone and you do too, the intellectual operation of mine and of yours will have to be one and the same." Most of the treatise is not the trilemma but a close reading of Aristotle's *De anima* and of the Greek and Arabic commentators, Themistius above all, to show that Averroes misread Aristotle. Aquinas calls him "qui non tam fuit Peripateticus, quam philosophiae Peripateticae depravator" (ch. 2). Gloss (this dossier's): "who was not so much a Peripatetic as a corrupter of Peripatetic philosophy". He states his method at the start: the view is to be shown to be "non minus contra philosophiae principia esse, quam contra fidei documenta" (prologue). Gloss (this dossier's): "no less against the principles of philosophy than against the teachings of the faith." And at the end: "non per documenta fidei, sed per ipsorum philosophorum rationes et dicta" (ch. 5). Gloss (this dossier's): "not through the teachings of the faith, but through the arguments and words of the philosophers themselves."

Chapter 5 also attacks the opponent's way of holding the view. Aquinas reports him as saying: "per rationem concludo de necessitate, quod intellectus est unus numero; firmiter tamen teneo oppositum per fidem" (ch. 5). Gloss (this dossier's): "By reason I conclude of necessity that the intellect is one in number; but I firmly hold the opposite by faith." Aquinas answers that only a necessary truth can be concluded of necessity, so this makes faith hold a false impossibility, which even God cannot make true. This is the source of the charge that the Latin Averroists taught a "double truth"; whether any of them held such a doctrine is disputed. The treatise closes with a public challenge: let the opponent not speak "in angulis nec coram pueris" (ch. 5), "in corners or before boys", in this dossier's gloss, but write a reply.

**Textual basis.** ST Ia Q76 a2 (body and replies 1–6); *De unitate intellectus* prologue and chs. 1–5 (Latin, Corpus Thomisticum, from L. W. Keeler's edition, Turin printing of 1954; read in English in Ralph McInerny's *Aquinas against the Averroists*, 1993, not quoted here); SCG II.73–78. The unity of the intellect was among the propositions condemned at Paris in 1270, as the [Averroes](../../eras/02-medieval/averroes.md) entry notes.

**Objection.** Averroes' own, given in Q76 a2 as objections 1, 3 and 4 and set out in the Averroes entry. An immaterial substance cannot be multiplied within a species, since matter is what makes many of a kind; so if the intellect is immaterial there can be only one (objection 1). Worse, a received form takes on the condition of what receives it. An intellect individuated by a body would receive forms as individual, and universal knowledge would be impossible (objection 3). And if my intellect differs from yours, what I understand differs from what you understand, so there is no common object of science and teaching (objection 4). On this reading Averroes is the more faithful Aristotelian: Aristotle called the intellect separate and unmixed (*De anima* III.4–5), and Aquinas's individual intellect looks like a theologian's compromise.

**Reply.** Aquinas's own, in the replies. To objection 1: the human soul has no matter it is made from, but it is the form of some matter, so souls are many in one species "according to the division of matter" (ST Ia Q76 a2 ad 1). An angel has no such relation to matter, which is why each angel is its own species. To objection 3: what blocks universal knowledge is materiality, not individuality. A separate intellect would itself be an individual, and Averroes allows that it knows universals; so "even if there were but one, it would necessarily be an individual intellect" (ST Ia Q76 a2 ad 3). To objection 4: what is understood is the thing, not the species in the mind, so many intellects can understand one stone, as many eyes see one colour (ST Ia Q76 a2 ad 4). On faithfulness to Aristotle, the scholarship is still divided. Aquinas's reading of *De anima* III.4–5 has defenders, but many modern readers think Aristotle left the status of the intellect unclear, so that both medieval readings had a footing in the text. The SEP's "Thomas Aquinas" (§6) adds a distinction: Aquinas judged a single shared *agent* intellect mistaken but not absurd, given its likeness to Augustine's doctrine of illumination, while he found a single *possible* intellect preposterous, because it could not explain individual acts of thought.

**Argument 13. Knowledge through phantasms: abstraction (ST Ia Q84 a6–7, Q85 a1)**

*What it proves:* that all human intellectual knowledge begins in the senses; that in this life the intellect understands nothing without turning to sense images (phantasms); and that it grasps the natures of material things by abstracting them from those images, not by innate ideas or by contact with separate Forms.

The questions sit in the treatise on the soul, after Q75–76 have set out what the soul is. Q84 a1–5 sets aside the alternatives one by one: that the soul knows bodies through its own essence (a2), through innate species (a3), or through species flowing from separate forms, as Plato and, in another way, Avicenna held (a4); and a5 allows that we know things "in the eternal types" only in the sense that our intellectual light is a participation in the divine light, not by seeing the types themselves, which is reserved to the blessed. Article 6 then sets three positions side by side. Democritus did not distinguish intellect from sense and made all knowledge an impression of images. Plato distinguished them and made the intellect independent of sense, so that the senses only "rouse the intellect to the act of understanding" (ST Ia Q84 a6). "Aristotle chose a middle course" (ST Ia Q84 a6), and Aquinas follows him.

1. Intellect and sense are distinct powers. Sense works through a bodily organ; intellect does not (ST Ia Q84 a6; Q75 a2).
2. A body cannot act on what is incorporeal. So sense images cannot by themselves impress anything on the possible intellect (ST Ia Q84 a6; Q85 a1 ad 3).
3. So an active power is needed: the agent intellect, which "causes the phantasms received from the senses to be actually intelligible, by a process of abstraction" (ST Ia Q84 a6).
4. The knowing power is proportioned to its object. The human intellect is not the act of an organ but belongs to a soul that is the form of a body. So its proper object is "a quiddity or nature existing in corporeal matter" (ST Ia Q84 a7), known not as existing in this matter (ST Ia Q85 a1).
5. To know a form that exists in individual matter, but not as existing in this individual matter, "is to abstract the form from individual matter which is represented by the phantasms" (ST Ia Q85 a1).
Therefore "our intellect understands material things by abstracting from the phantasms" (ST Ia Q85 a1), and sense knowledge is not the whole cause of understanding "but rather that it is in a way the material cause" (ST Ia Q84 a6).

Article 7 adds that even knowledge already gained needs images to be used: "it is impossible for our intellect to understand anything actually, except by turning to the phantasms" (ST Ia Q84 a7). There are two reasons. Damage to a bodily organ, as in "frenzy" or "lethargy", stops a man from understanding what he already knew, though the intellect itself has no organ. And experience: "when he tries to understand something, he forms certain phantasms to serve him by way of examples" (ST Ia Q84 a7), which is why teachers give examples.

Q85 a1 ad 1 guards the theory against the charge that abstraction falsifies. To say that colour exists apart from the coloured apple would be false. To consider colour without considering the apple is not. In the same way the intellect can consider what it is to be a stone or a horse "apart from the individualizing principles" (ST Ia Q85 a1 ad 1). The intellect would be false only "if it abstracted the species of a stone from its matter in such a way as to regard the species as not existing in matter, as Plato held" (ST Ia Q85 a1 ad 1). And Q85 a2 adds that the species is not what we understand: "the intelligible species is not what is actually understood, but that by which the intellect understands" (ST Ia Q85 a2, *sed contra*; the body of the article argues for it).

**Textual basis.** ST Ia Q84 a1–8; Q85 a1–2; Q79 a3–4 (the agent intellect); Q88 a1–3 (how we know immaterial things). The argument assumes Argument 11 (the soul as form) and Argument 12 (each person has an intellect). Ia Q84 a7 ad 3 states the cost: God and angels are known only by comparison with bodies, "by way of excess and by way of remotion" (ST Ia Q84 a7 ad 3), which ties this argument to Argument 8 on analogy.

**Objection.** Two, from opposite sides. The empiricist objection, pressed by George Berkeley against Locke in the Introduction to the *Principles of Human Knowledge* (1710), is that abstraction as a mental act is unintelligible: one cannot form an idea of a triangle that is neither scalene nor isosceles, or of a man of no height and no colour. On this view nothing in experience corresponds to the agent intellect, which explains universal thought only by naming a power that does it. The SEP's "Thomas Aquinas" (§6) concedes that for the whole Aristotelian tradition the agent intellect has worked as a black box, its operation taken as a brute fact and not analysed. The second objection is that the theory, with species standing between mind and world, leads to the "veil of ideas": if we think by means of species, we seem to know only our species, and scepticism about the outer world follows, as it did for the early moderns.

**Reply.** To the second objection, Aquinas's own, in Q85 a2: the species is "that by which" we understand, not "what" we understand. If species were the objects of thought, "the objects of sciences would not be things, but only intelligible species" (ST Ia Q76 a2 ad 4). How far this commits him to direct realism is disputed. Dominik Perler (2000) reads him as a direct realist, since the species just is the thing's form existing in a new way. Robert Pasnau (*Theories of Cognition in the Later Middle Ages*, 1997, ch. 3) and Claude Panaccio (2001) argue that the species sometimes work as representations that mediate between knower and thing (both readings in SEP §6.1). To Berkeley, Thomists reply that Aquinas's abstraction is not the forming of a general image but, as Q85 a1 ad 1 puts it, a "simple and absolute consideration" of a nature apart from what individuates it. That is a claim about thought, not imagery, and Berkeley's argument targets Locke's imagery. Whether that reply answers the deeper worry, that the agent intellect explains nothing, is open; Therese Scarpelli Cory ("Rethinking Abstractionism", 2015) is one recent attempt to say what the operation is.

**Argument 14. Happiness is the vision of God (ST Ia-IIae Q2–3)**

*What it proves:* that no created good can be the last end of human life, that happiness consists essentially in an act of the intellect and not of the will, and that perfect happiness is the vision of God's essence, which cannot be had in this life.

Ia-IIae Q1 has argued that every human act aims at some end, and that there is a single last end for a whole life. Q2 asks what thing that end is; Q3 asks what attaining it consists in. The structure follows Aristotle's *Nicomachean Ethics* I and X, and the conclusion goes beyond Aristotle.

1. Happiness is "the perfect good, which lulls the appetite altogether; else it would not be the last end, if something yet remained to be desired" (ST Ia-IIae Q2 a8).
2. Wealth, honour, fame, power, bodily goods, pleasure and even goods of the soul each fail this test (ST Ia-IIae Q2 a1–7). Wealth, for example, is only a means; power can be used well or badly.
3. The object of the will is the universal good, as the object of the intellect is the universal true. Every creature has goodness only by participation. So "naught can lull man's will, save the universal good. This is to be found, not in any creature, but in God alone" (ST Ia-IIae Q2 a8).
4. The end is twofold: the thing desired and the attaining of it, as the miser's end is money and also the having of money (ST Ia-IIae Q3 a1). God is the end in the first sense. Happiness in the second sense, the attaining, is a created act in us.
5. That act is not an act of the will. The will desires an absent end and delights in a present one, but desire is not attaining and delight follows attaining. If willing were attaining, "the covetous man would have it from the very moment that he wished for it" (ST Ia-IIae Q3 a4). An intelligible end is made present by an act of intellect, "and then the delighted will rests in the end when attained" (ST Ia-IIae Q3 a4).
6. The intellect is perfected by knowing what a thing is. As long as it knows only that a cause exists and not what it is, the natural desire to know remains, as a man who sees an eclipse wonders and inquires until he knows the cause (ST Ia-IIae Q3 a8).
7. In this life we know God only through effects, that he is and not what he is (ST Ia Q12 a12; Ia-IIae Q3 a6–7).
Therefore "Final and perfect happiness can consist in nothing else than the vision of the Divine Essence" (ST Ia-IIae Q3 a8).

The conclusion has a corollary that matters for the ethics that follows. In this life happiness is at best imperfect, because the act that joins us to God "neither can be continual, nor, consequently, is it one only" (ST Ia-IIae Q3 a2 ad 4). Aquinas reads Aristotle as having seen this. When Aristotle placed happiness in this life, he called it imperfect and concluded: "We call men happy, but only as men" (ST Ia-IIae Q3 a2 ad 4, quoting *Nicomachean Ethics* I.10). The contemplation of the sciences is "a certain participation of true and perfect happiness" (ST Ia-IIae Q3 a6), not its substance. Q5 a5 adds that perfect happiness exceeds the powers of any created nature and is given by God. The virtues of Ia-IIae Q55–67 are therefore ordered to an end they cannot reach by themselves, which is the job of the theological virtues and grace.

**Textual basis.** ST Ia-IIae Q1 a4–8 (one last end); Q2 a1–8; Q3 a1–8; Q4 (what happiness requires: delight, rectitude of will, and in its fullness the body); Q5 a3 and a5 (not attainable in this life or by nature); ST Ia Q12 (how God is known); SCG III.25–63 gives the longer form of the argument.

**Objection.** Two. The first is Duns Scotus's, against premise 5. Scotus held that the will is the higher power and that the essence of beatitude lies in the will's love of God, not in the intellect's vision (*Ordinatio* IV d.49). Love is the response of the whole person to a good, and an act of seeing that left the will unmoved would be no happiness at all. See [Duns Scotus](../../eras/02-medieval/john-duns-scotus.md). The second objection comes from inside Thomism and is harder. Premise 6 rests on a *natural* desire to see God's essence, but Q5 a5 says that vision exceeds every created nature. If the desire is natural, God would seem to owe its fulfilment, or nature would be in vain; if it is not natural, the argument from desire in premise 6 collapses. Either way the line between nature and grace blurs.

**Reply.** To Scotus, Aquinas's own, in Q3 a4: he does not exclude the will from happiness. Delight belongs to happiness as its "proper accident" (ST Ia-IIae Q3 a4), and Q4 requires rectitude of will as a condition of it. His claim is only about what the attaining itself consists in, and the miser's case gives his reason: wanting a thing is not having it. On the second objection the literature is sharply divided. The commentator tradition from Cajetan onward read the desire as elicited, a desire that arises once one knows God exists, and posited a possible order of "pure nature" with a merely natural end. Henri de Lubac (*Surnaturel*, 1946) argued that this reading betrayed Aquinas: the desire is innate in every intellect, God gives what it longs for freely, and nature is open to a gift it cannot claim. Lawrence Feingold (*The Natural Desire to See God according to St. Thomas Aquinas and His Interpreters*, 2001) defended a version of the older reading against de Lubac. The question is still argued among Thomists.

**Argument 15. The first precept of natural law and its order (ST Ia-IIae Q94 a2)**

*What it proves:* that natural law has many precepts but one first precept, "good is to be done and pursued, and evil is to be avoided"; that this precept is self-evident to practical reason as the principle of non-contradiction is to theoretical reason; and that the further precepts follow the order of human natural inclinations as reason grasps them.

The [study guide](../../study/aquinas-summa.md) reconstructs this article as its argument (d), with the objection that the derivation would bless every human urge, aggression included, and Aquinas's reply that inclinations count only as reason grasps and orders them. This entry adds what the guide leaves out: the analogy with theoretical reason that gives the first precept its status, and the dispute over how the precepts are known.

1. The precepts of natural law are to practical reason what the first principles of demonstration are to theoretical reason: "both are self-evident principles" (ST Ia-IIae Q94 a2).
2. A proposition is self-evident in itself when its predicate is contained in the notion of its subject. Some are self-evident to all, some only to the wise (ST Ia-IIae Q94 a2). The distinction is the one Ia Q2 a1 used for "God exists".
3. In theoretical reason, "being" is the first thing grasped, and the first principle, non-contradiction, rests on the notion of being and not-being (ST Ia-IIae Q94 a2).
4. In practical reason, "good" is the first thing grasped, "since every agent acts for an end under the aspect of good" (ST Ia-IIae Q94 a2). Good is "that which all things seek after".
5. So the first precept is "good is to be done and pursued, and evil is to be avoided", and "whatever the practical reason naturally apprehends as man's good (or evil) belongs to the precepts of the natural law as something to be done or avoided" (ST Ia-IIae Q94 a2).
6. Good has the nature of an end. So "all those things to which man has a natural inclination, are naturally apprehended by reason as being good" (ST Ia-IIae Q94 a2).
Therefore "according to the order of natural inclinations, is the order of the precepts of the natural law" (ST Ia-IIae Q94 a2). Three levels follow. What humans share with all substances gives precepts of preserving life. What they share with other animals gives precepts about "sexual intercourse, education of offspring and so forth" (quoting the *Digest* of Justinian). What is proper to reason gives a "natural inclination to know the truth about God, and to live in society", and hence precepts "to shun ignorance, to avoid offending those among whom one has to live" (ST Ia-IIae Q94 a2).

The replies explain how one law has many precepts. They "have the character of one natural law, inasmuch as they flow from one first precept" (ST Ia-IIae Q94 a2 ad 1). Even the inclinations of the lower appetites belong to natural law "in so far as they are ruled by reason" (ST Ia-IIae Q94 a2 ad 2). Articles 4–6 then limit the result. The first principles are the same for all, but the more detailed conclusions hold only in most cases and can be obscured by bad custom. The study guide's checkpoint answer 12 summarises these articles.

**Textual basis.** ST Ia-IIae Q94 a1–6, with Q90 a1 (law as a work of reason), Q91 a2 (natural law as the rational creature's participation in eternal law) and Q91 a3; ST Ia Q2 a1 (the two kinds of self-evidence); Ia-IIae Q18 a1 on good and bad acts. The library entry's section "Natural law" gives the summary.

**Objection.** Hume's Law. In the *Treatise* (III.1.1, 1739–40), Hume noted that moral writers pass without notice from "is" to "ought", and later writers turned this into the thesis that no normative conclusion follows from non-normative premises. Premise 6 seems to do exactly that: from the fact that humans are inclined to life, offspring, knowledge and society, it concludes that these are goods and their pursuit a precept. And if the order of precepts follows the order of inclinations, the argument seems to rank goods by how widely the inclination is shared, so that bare survival, shared with stones, comes first.

**Reply.** The literature's, which is split on what the article says. Germain Grisez ("The First Principle of Practical Reason", *Natural Law Forum*, 1965) and John Finnis (*Natural Law and Natural Rights*, 1980) answer Hume by denying that Aquinas derives the goods from facts about nature. The first precept is not a moral rule but a principle that makes action intelligible at all, and the basic goods are grasped as self-evident by practical reason, not inferred from a theory of human nature. On this "new natural law" reading the three levels are not a ranking, and the basic goods are not commensurable. Critics in the older Thomist line, such as Ralph McInerny (*Aquinas on Human Action*, 1992) and Russell Hittinger (*A Critique of the New Natural Law Theory*, 1987), reply that this cuts natural law from the metaphysics of nature and eternal law that Q91 builds, and that Aquinas makes no secret of grounding the good in nature. The SEP's "The Natural Law Tradition in Ethics" separates "derivationist" and "inclinationist" readings and grants each side a point: Hume's Law shows at most that a derivationist needs bridge principles linking nature to good, while inclinationism explains better why the first precepts are known to all. On the ranking worry, a common reading is that "order" in the article is the order in which inclinations appear, from the most widely shared to the most properly human, not a scale of importance; and on any reading the third level, which is proper to reason, rules the other two (ad 2).

**Argument 16. Double effect in self-defence (ST IIa-IIae Q64 a7)**

*What it proves:* that a private person may use force that kills an attacker, provided the killing is not intended, the intention is to save one's own life, and the force is proportionate to that end. The article is usually credited as the first statement of what later moralists called the principle of double effect.

The article answers Augustine. Q64 a7's first two objections quote him: a letter to Publicola (Ep. 47) and *On Free Choice* I.5, where he doubts that a private person who kills an attacker is free of sin, because the killer acts for the sake of things that can be lost against one's will. The [Augustine dossier](../augustine-of-hippo/04-arguments.md) treats these passages under its Argument 14. Aquinas has already ruled in Q64 a3 that only public authority, acting for the common good, may kill a malefactor. The question is whether a private person who kills in self-defence must be guilty.

1. "Nothing hinders one act from having two effects, only one of which is intended, while the other is beside the intention" (ST IIa-IIae Q64 a7).
2. Moral acts are judged by what is intended: "moral acts take their species according to what is intended, and not according to what is beside the intention, since this is accidental" (ST IIa-IIae Q64 a7).
3. An act of self-defence can have two effects: "one is the saving of one's life, the other is the slaying of the aggressor" (ST IIa-IIae Q64 a7).
4. If the intention is to save one's own life, the act is not unlawful, "seeing that it is natural to everything to keep itself in 'being,' as far as possible" (ST IIa-IIae Q64 a7). This is the first inclination of Argument 15.
5. "And yet, though proceeding from a good intention, an act may be rendered unlawful, if it be out of proportion to the end" (ST IIa-IIae Q64 a7). Force beyond what is needed is unlawful; force used with moderation is lawful, as the jurists say: "it is lawful to repel force by force, provided one does not exceed the limits of a blameless defense" (ST IIa-IIae Q64 a7).
6. A private person may not intend to kill, since only public authority may take life for the common good (ST IIa-IIae Q64 a3, a7).
Therefore a private person who kills an attacker while intending only to save his own life, and using no more force than needed, does not sin; and he need not hold back from moderate defence to spare the attacker, "since one is bound to take more care of one's own life than of another's" (ST IIa-IIae Q64 a7).

Two details matter. Public officials are treated differently: a soldier or a judge's officer may intend to kill, provided they "refer this to the public good", though "even these sin if they be moved by private animosity" (ST IIa-IIae Q64 a7). And Aquinas reads Augustine's objections narrowly. Augustine, he says, meant the case where "one man intends to kill another to save himself from death" (ST IIa-IIae Q64 a7 ad 1). So the condemnation stands for intended killing and does not reach a defence in which death is a side effect.

Later moralists turned the article into a general rule. Joseph Mangan's standard formulation (1949), reported in the SEP's "Doctrine of Double Effect", has four conditions: the act must be good or indifferent in itself; the good effect and not the bad must be intended; the good effect must not be produced by means of the bad; and there must be a proportionately grave reason for permitting the bad effect. Only the second and fourth are explicit in Q64 a7. The third, that the bad effect may not be the means, is the later addition that does most work in modern cases, from bombing in war to the hysterectomy of a pregnant woman with cancer.

**Textual basis.** ST IIa-IIae Q64 a7 (body and replies 1–5), with a2–3 (killing malefactors belongs to public authority), a6 (never lawful to kill the innocent) and a8 (killing by chance); on intention, Ia-IIae Q12 a1 and Q20 a5. The just-war conditions in IIa-IIae Q40 a1 are the public counterpart.

**Objection.** Two, one about the text and one about the principle. About the text: it is not clear that Q64 a7 states double effect at all. The defender surely knows the blow may kill, and in a lethal case he chooses a blow that kills as his means of stopping the attack. If "beside the intention" (*praeter intentionem*) covers that, intention seems to shrink to whatever the agent is aiming at in the end, and almost any killing could be redescribed as a side effect. About the principle: the SEP's §4.2 names the problem of closeness. It is hard to tell a harm foreseen as a side effect from a harm so close to the means that it must be intended. In the hysterectomy case, where a pregnant woman's cancerous womb is removed, the fetus dies just as it would in an abortion, and the principle must say why one death is a side effect and the other a means. Consequentialists add that the intended–foreseen distinction has no moral weight at all: what matters is what one knowingly brings about.

**Reply.** The literature's. On the text, interpreters divide over what "intend" covers. One reading, found in Mangan's historical study and in much of the manual tradition, takes the article as double effect in the full sense: the defender intends to stop the attack, and the attacker's death lies outside the intention even when it is foreseen as certain. Another reading takes Aquinas's "intention" to bear chiefly on the end, so that the article is silent on whether a lethal means may be chosen, and the later rule against bad means is not in it. Germain Grisez ("Toward a Consistent Natural-Law Ethics of Killing", 1970) used the first reading to build a general ethics of killing; T. A. Cavanaugh (*Double-Effect Reasoning*, 2006) surveys the dispute. On the principle, defenders such as G. E. M. Anscombe ("War and Murder", 1961) hold that the line between intending and foreseeing is real and marks the line between murder and lawful force, while warning that the doctrine is easily abused by people who simply redescribe what they are doing. Aquinas's article carries a check against that abuse which the four-condition formula can lose: the force must not exceed "the limits of a blameless defense", and private animosity corrupts even a lawful act. The problem of closeness is still open; the SEP reports that no clear resolution has emerged.
