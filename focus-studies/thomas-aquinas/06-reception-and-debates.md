# Aquinas Received: Suspect, Saint, Common Doctor, and the Quarrels Still Open

*Focus study: Thomas Aquinas · [Dossier home](README.md) · [Library entry](../../eras/02-medieval/thomas-aquinas.md) · [Study guide](../../study/aquinas-summa.md)*

Three years to the day after Aquinas died, the bishop of Paris condemned a list of propositions that seemed to touch his teaching, and within a decade his Franciscan critics had published a "correction" of his works. Fifty years after his death he was a canonized saint. In the sixteenth century his *Summa* replaced Peter Lombard's *Sentences* as the theology textbook of the great Spanish universities, and the Jesuits were told to follow him. Two centuries later the moderns had driven the scholastics from most universities, and in 1879 a pope made him the guide of Catholic philosophy. The twentieth century produced several Thomisms that disagreed with each other, and then a Thomism conducted in the idiom of analytic philosophy. This file tells that story by era, states nine live debates with names on each side, and closes with the standard misconceptions in full form and the myths about the life and the work.

The *Summa Theologiae* is quoted from the translation of the Fathers of the English Dominican Province (1920), because it is in the public domain and anyone can check it. The learning path's rule still stands: read Aquinas in Davies and Leftow's *Questions on God* (Cambridge Texts) and McDermott's *Concise Translation*, with Feser's *Aquinas: A Beginner's Guide* alongside, and not the full *Summa* in the old Dominican translation. The 1912 *Catholic Encyclopedia* article "Thomism" is quoted as a public-domain witness to how the neo-Thomist generation told its own history; where modern scholarship corrects it, the correction is given. The arguments are cited by their numbers in [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md).

## 1277 and the correctorium dispute

On 7 March 1277 Stephen Tempier, bishop of Paris, prohibited the teaching of 219 propositions disputed in the arts faculty. Pope John XXI had written to him on 18 January asking him to find out where and by whom certain errors were being spread, but the SEP's account of the condemnation notes that the evidence also fits a bishop already preparing his list when the letter arrived. Only 79 of the 219 can be traced, with varying probability, to Siger of Brabant, Boethius of Dacia and three anonymous writings. Others may come from theologians. The SEP's "Thomas Aquinas" judges that a significant number seem to implicate Aquinas's teaching; John Wippel has argued, against Roland Hissette, that some were taken from his writings. The 1912 *Catholic Encyclopedia* names the two points usually cited: the principle of individuation by matter and the related thesis that God cannot make several angels of one species. Both follow from Aquinas's view that matter individuates (see [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md) and Argument 12).

A second process may have been aimed at him directly. Robert Wielockx argued that after censuring fifty-one propositions of Giles of Rome, Tempier opened an inquiry against Thomas before 28 March 1277, and that during the papal vacancy of that year, between 20 May and 25 November, the curia ordered him to stop. His evidence is a letter of John Pecham. Most scholars accepted the thesis; Wippel and Hans Thijssen have since re-examined the evidence and questioned it (SEP, "Condemnation of 1277", §6). Later in March 1277, at Oxford, the Dominican archbishop of Canterbury, Robert Kilwardby, condemned a list that explicitly included two of Thomas's distinctive doctrines: the unity of substantial form in a human being (Argument 11) and prime matter as pure potentiality.

The written dispute followed. The Franciscan William de la Mare published a *Correctorium fratris Thomae*, which set verbatim quotations from Thomas beside "corrections". Histories of the dispute report that the Franciscan general chapter of Strasbourg (1282) allowed friars to read the *Summa* only with William's corrections at hand; that decree is not in the sources saved for this file and is given here as the usual account. Dominican disciples answered with a series of works the SEP calls *Correctorium corruptorii Thomae*, the "correction of the corrupter". The *Catholic Encyclopedia* names the English Dominican Richard Clapwell (also spelled Knapwell or Clapole) as the author of one reply, and reports that another, printed at Venice in 1516, has been attributed to Giles of Rome, to Clapwell and to John of Paris. P. Glorieux's edition (1927) began the modern study of these texts. The Dominican general chapters of Milan (1278) and Paris (1279) moved to discipline friars who spoke against "the venerable Brother Thomas", and in the SEP's summary scholasticism for the next quarter century took shape around his Dominican supporters and his Franciscan critics. The story that the aged Albert the Great travelled to Paris in 1277 to defend his pupil, which the *Catholic Encyclopedia* repeats, comes from the early Dominican lives; many historians doubt it.

Two modern quarrels about 1277 touch Aquinas. Pierre Duhem argued that the condemnation, by insisting on God's absolute power against Aristotle's necessities, freed Christian thought to imagine a void and a moving universe, and so marked the birth of modern science. The SEP reports that no historian of science now holds that modern science started in 1277, though Edward Grant keeps a refined version of the thesis. The second quarrel is about "double truth". Tempier's prologue accused the arts masters of saying that things are true according to philosophy and not according to the faith, as if there were two contrary truths. Scholars now agree that no medieval author held that two contradictory propositions could both be true. Aquinas's own position, that truth is one and faith and reason cannot contradict each other, is stated in [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md).

## Canonization, 1323, and the lifting of the censure, 1325

The cult began at Fossanova at once, with reported miracles and a long dispute over who would keep the body. The canonization case opened in 1317; its inquiries sat at Naples in 1319 and at Fossanova in 1321, and John XXII canonized Thomas at Avignon on 18 July 1323 ([01-life-and-times.md](01-life-and-times.md) gives the details and weighs the witnesses). Two years later the bishop of Paris, Stephen Bourret, declared that the 1277 condemnation should not be held to apply to Thomas's teaching. The SEP stresses that he made clear this was not an endorsement. The *Catholic Encyclopedia*, which dates the act to 1324, gives its wording more warmly: "that blessed confessor and excellent doctor, Thomas Aquinas, had never believed, taught, or written anything contrary to the Faith or good morals".

The *Catholic Encyclopedia* adds a council. The Council of Vienne (1311–12) defined, against Peter John Olivi, that the rational soul is the form of the human body, and the encyclopedia calls this a solemn vindication of Thomas. The decree does assert the soul's role as form, which Aquinas taught (Argument 11). It does not decide whether a human being has one substantial form or several, which was the point Kilwardby condemned, and Ockham, for one, still held a plurality of forms after 1312. The neo-Thomist reading of Vienne is therefore stronger than the text.

The order consolidated its own position. The Dominicans' statutes required their friars to teach his doctrine; the general chapter of Carcassonne (1342) declared it received throughout the world as sound and solid; and according to the *Catholic Encyclopedia* the *Summa* had by the middle of the fourteenth century replaced the *Sentences* as the textbook of the Dominican schools. That last claim should be read with care: commentary on the *Sentences* remained the standard exercise for a bachelor of theology everywhere until the sixteenth century, which is when historians usually date the *Summa*'s arrival as a university lecture text (next sections). In 1378 the University of Paris, condemning the errors of the Dominican John of Montesono, felt it had to add: "We have said a thousand times, and yet, it would seem, not often enough, that we by no means include the doctrine of St. Thomas in our condemnation." Dante had already placed Thomas among the wise in the heaven of the Sun (*Paradiso* X), beside Albert and Siger.

Meanwhile the field moved on. In the SEP's account the early fourteenth century belonged to two original Franciscans, [Duns Scotus](../../eras/02-medieval/john-duns-scotus.md) with his realism and univocity of being and [William of Ockham](../../eras/02-medieval/william-of-ockham.md) with his nominalism, and against them Aquinas's views came to seem both less urgent and less dangerous. The *via antiqua* of the fifteenth-century universities, which included Thomists and Albertists, defined itself against the *via moderna* of the Ockhamists.

## The Thomist school, Capreolus and Cajetan

A school needs commentators, and the Thomist school's first great one was John Capreolus (d. 1444), whose *Defensiones theologiae divi Thomae* answered Scotus, Durand, Peter Auriol and the nominalists point by point, through the frame of a *Sentences* commentary. The *Catholic Encyclopedia* reports his title "Prince of Thomists". In the SEP's account Capreolus made Thomism a systematic philosophy, and a century later Thomas de Vio, Cardinal Cajetan (1468–1534), developed it further.

Cajetan matters to a reader of this dossier for three reasons. His commentary on the whole *Summa Theologiae*, written in the first decades of the sixteenth century, is printed with the text in the Leonine edition, as is Francesco Silvestri of Ferrara's (d. 1528) commentary on the *Summa contra Gentiles*, so the "standard" Thomas of the modern edition comes with its standard reading attached. His treatise *On the Analogy of Names* (1498) fixed the analogy of proper proportionality as the Thomist doctrine (Argument 8). And he and his successors worked out the idea of a "pure nature", a human nature with a natural end of its own that grace exceeds, which became the target of Henri de Lubac in the twentieth century (Argument 14; debate 5 below). Cajetan was also the papal legate who examined Luther at Augsburg in 1518. The question whether Cajetan's Thomism is Thomas's, raised by Gilson and many since, runs through every debate below.

## The Second Scholastic: Salamanca, Trent and the *de auxiliis* quarrel

The revival of scholastic theology in sixteenth-century Spain and Portugal is now called the Second Scholastic; the label is usually traced to the Italian historian Carlo Giacon (1940s). Its centre was the Dominican chair at Salamanca. Francisco de Vitoria (d. 1546) lectured on the *Summa* in place of the *Sentences*, and his lectures on the American conquests and on war turned Aquinas's treatment of law, dominion and just war (IIa-IIae Q40; Argument 15) toward the law of nations. Domingo de Soto (d. 1560), Melchior Cano (d. 1560) and Domingo Báñez (d. 1604) continued the school. The Jesuits, founded in 1540, took Aquinas as their theologian too: Ignatius of Loyola enjoined his study, and the order's members, Francisco Suárez among them, were told to hold the safest and best-established views, especially his (SEP, citing Pasnau). [Suárez](../../eras/03-early-modern/suarez-and-grotius.md) (1548–1617) followed him with independence; his *Metaphysical Disputations* (1597) taught metaphysics to Catholic and Lutheran universities for a century and denied the real distinction of essence and existence that Thomists defended (Argument 6).

Pius V named Aquinas a Doctor of the Church in 1567, a title previously reserved for the ancient Fathers, and the Piana edition of his works followed in 1570. The story that the Council of Trent (1545–63) placed the *Summa* on the altar beside the Scriptures is treated below under myths.

The quarrel that split the school was about grace and freedom (tension 5 in [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md)). The Jesuit Luis de Molina's *Concordia* (1588) held that God knows by "middle knowledge" what any free creature would do in any circumstance, and so can govern the world through grace whose effect depends on the creature's free consent. Báñez and the Dominicans replied that this made God's knowledge depend on creatures, and held that God moves the will to its act by an efficacious "physical premotion" that does not destroy its freedom. Each side claimed Aquinas. A papal commission, the *Congregatio de auxiliis* (1598–1607), heard the case, and in 1607 Paul V let both schools teach while forbidding each to call the other heretical. The *Catholic Encyclopedia* concedes that the controversy did not end and that "the question is not yet settled". Later commentators of the school included John of St. Thomas (João Poinsot, d. 1644), the Carmelites of Salamanca (the *Salmanticenses*, whose *Cursus theologicus* appeared 1631–72), and Charles-René Billuart (d. 1757).

## The long eclipse, c. 1650–1850

Thomism remained strong inside the religious orders while the learned world left it. The SEP gives three attackers: Luther, who hoped that soon there would be no Thomists, Albertists, Scotists or Ockhamists left, only simple Christians; the Italian humanists, who preferred history and philology to intricate metaphysics; and the seventeenth-century "moderns", among them [Hobbes](../../eras/03-early-modern/thomas-hobbes.md), who scorned the schoolmen. It was not until the eighteenth century, the SEP adds, that the moderns drove the Thomists and other scholastics out of the universities. The *Catholic Encyclopedia* gives its own list of causes from inside the tradition: Protestantism, which made positive theology seem more urgent than scholastic distinctions; humanism; the new sciences, which the scholastics received badly; and the French Revolution, which closed Louvain and nearly destroyed the Dominican provinces of northern Europe. Several schools stayed open far from Europe's upheavals, among them the Dominican university founded at Manila (1645). Catholic seminaries of the early nineteenth century taught a mixture of Cartesian, Wolffian and eclectic philosophy, and that mixture was what the revival set out to replace.

## *Aeterni Patris*, 1879, and neo-Thomism

The revival began before the pope endorsed it. In Italy the secular priest Gaetano Sanseverino at Naples and the Jesuits Matteo Liberatore and Giovanni Maria Cornoldi, and the German Jesuit Joseph Kleutgen in Rome, argued from the mid-nineteenth century that the scholastics, and Aquinas above all, offered a unified philosophy that could answer Descartes, Kant and the idealists. The Dominican Tommaso Maria Zigliara taught the same programme in Rome. On 4 August 1879 Leo XIII issued the encyclical *Aeterni Patris*, which called for the restoration of Christian philosophy in the spirit of Thomas. Its list of the universities "where Thomas reigned supreme" (Paris, Salamanca, Alcalá, Douai, Toulouse, Louvain, Padua, Bologna, Naples, Coimbra), as the *Catholic Encyclopedia* quotes it, describes the past the revival wanted to recover.

The SEP names two fruits. The first was the Leonine Commission (1880), charged with a critical edition of all his works; its first volume appeared in 1882 and the edition is still unfinished. The second was a renewed picture of Aquinas at the summit of philosophy, with everything after him, from Scotus to Descartes and Kant, dismissed as decline. Institutions followed: a chair of Thomistic philosophy at Louvain (1880), which became the Institut Supérieur de Philosophie under Désiré Mercier in 1889–90; the Dominican theology faculty at Fribourg (1889) and the *Revue thomiste*; the Roman Angelicum, where Réginald Garrigou-Lagrange (1877–1964) taught for half a century.

The revival was then made law. Pius X's motu proprio *Doctoris Angelici* (29 June 1914) required the principles of Aquinas in Catholic schools, and on 27 July 1914 the Congregation of Studies approved twenty-four theses as containing his principal doctrines, beginning with act and potency and the real distinction. The Code of Canon Law of 1917 (canon 1366 §2) required professors of philosophy and theology to treat these subjects according to the Angelic Doctor's method, doctrine and principles and to hold them religiously, and Pius XI's encyclical *Studiorum ducem* (1923) repeated it. The textbooks of this period, written in Latin by many hands, are what later readers mean by "manual Thomism". Its critics then and since, from the Modernists of the 1900s to Gilson, Chenu and de Lubac, charged that it read Aquinas through Cajetan and Suárez, in an essentialist and rationalist key, as a set of theses to be defended against modernity rather than a thinker in a history.

## The twentieth century: historians, existential Thomists, transcendental Thomists

**The historians.** Pierre Mandonnet (1858–1936) and Martin Grabmann (1875–1949) put Thomas back into the thirteenth century, in the university, among Siger, Bonaventure and the condemnations. Étienne Gilson (1884–1978) was the decisive figure: a historian trained on Descartes, he wrote *Le thomisme* (first edition 1919) and founded the Institute of Mediaeval Studies at Toronto in 1929. In 1931, at the Société française de philosophie, the historian Émile Bréhier argued that one can no more speak of Christian philosophy than of Christian mathematics, and Gilson replied that the history of philosophy shows Christian revelation generating new philosophical ideas, being as existence among them. Jacques Maritain took his side; Maurice Blondel, Fernand Van Steenberghen and the Dominican Pierre Mandonnet, for different reasons, did not (IEP, "Christian Philosophy: The 1930s French Debates"; debate 2 below).

**Existential Thomism.** Gilson's reading made *esse*, the act of being, the centre of Thomas's metaphysics, and charged the commentators from Cajetan onward with "essentialism", forgetting existence for essence (*Being and Some Philosophers*, 1949; Argument 6). Jacques Maritain (1882–1973) made a related case in *The Degrees of Knowledge* (1932) and in his political writings argued that Thomist natural law grounds human rights and democracy; he took part in the drafting debates around the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948). Cornelio Fabro (1939) and Louis-Bertrand Geiger (1942) recovered the Platonic doctrine of participation in Aquinas, against the picture of a purely Aristotelian Thomas.

**Ressourcement and its conflict with Rome.** At the Dominican house of Le Saulchoir, Marie-Dominique Chenu read Thomas as a historian and a theologian of history; his manifesto *Une école de théologie: le Saulchoir* (1937) was placed on the Index in 1942. The Jesuit Henri de Lubac's *Surnaturel* (1946) argued that the commentators' "pure nature" was foreign to Thomas, who taught a natural desire for the vision of God (Argument 14). Garrigou-Lagrange attacked the "new theology" in print, and Pius XII's *Humani generis* (1950) warned against those who denied that God could have created intellectual beings without ordering them to the beatific vision.

**Transcendental Thomism.** The Belgian Jesuit Joseph Maréchal (1878–1944) argued in *Le point de départ de la métaphysique* (fifth volume 1926) that Aquinas's account of the intellect's dynamism toward being could meet Kant on his own ground. Karl Rahner (*Spirit in the World*, 1939) and Bernard Lonergan (*Insight*, 1957) built on him. Its critics among Thomists, as Feser summarizes them, think it concedes too much to modern philosophy to count as Thomism, and it has been more influential among theologians than philosophers.

**Other schools.** The River Forest and Laval Thomists (Charles De Koninck at Laval; the Dominicans of River Forest, Illinois) put the philosophy of nature before metaphysics. The Lublin school in Poland (Mieczysław Krąpiec, Karol Wojtyła) joined existential Thomism to phenomenology.

**After the Council.** The Second Vatican Council's decree on priestly formation (*Optatam totius*, 1965, §16) still named St Thomas as teacher, but the single compulsory system did not survive the 1960s. Gerald McCool's *From Unity to Pluralism* (1989) tells the story as the internal evolution of Thomism itself: once the historians had shown that Thomas differed from his commentators, there was no longer one Thomism to impose. Outside Catholic institutions, [Bertrand Russell](../../eras/05-20th-century/bertrand-russell.md)'s *History of Western Philosophy* (1945) had given the common view, that Aquinas knew his conclusions in advance and so was arguing a case, not following an argument.

## Analytical Thomism

The meeting of Aquinas with analytic philosophy began in Oxford and Cambridge in the 1950s, with Wittgenstein in the room. [G. E. M. Anscombe](../../eras/05-20th-century/anscombe-and-foot.md) and Peter Geach, Catholics and Wittgenstein's pupils, wrote *Three Philosophers* (1961), with Anscombe on Aristotle and Geach on Aquinas and Frege. Geach's *Mental Acts* (1957) attacked "abstractionism", the empiricist theory that concepts are got by noticing what particulars share, and Fergus Kerr has pointed to its Thomist roots (Argument 13). Anscombe's "Modern Moral Philosophy" (1958) and her work on intention fed the revival of virtue ethics and the modern discussion of double effect (Argument 16). Anthony Kenny's *The Five Ways* (1969) gave the analytic examination of Arguments 1–5 that every later defender answers, and his *Aquinas on Being* (2002) rejected the doctrine of God as *ipsum esse*, subsistent being itself, as incoherent. Norman Kretzmann and Eleonore Stump at Cornell made Aquinas a working figure in philosophy of religion; Stump's *Aquinas* (2003) is a standard of the genre. The *Cambridge History of Later Medieval Philosophy* (1982), edited by Kretzmann, Kenny and Jan Pinborg, put medieval logic and semantics into the analytic curriculum.

The name came later. John Haldane coined "analytical Thomism" in 1992, as the title of two lectures at Notre Dame, and edited an issue of *The Monist* on it (October 1997) with contributors including Hilary Putnam, Brian Davies and Stump. His Aquinas Lecture, "Thomism and the Future of Catholic Philosophy", appeared with replies in *New Blackfriars* (April 1999), and Craig Paterson and Matthew Pugh's collection *Analytical Thomism: Traditions in Dialogue* (2006) took stock. Haldane's definition is broad: a philosophical approach that brings the methods and concerns of recent Anglo-American philosophy to bear on the ideas of Aquinas and the Thomists. It does not mean the philosophy Thomas held, only a living tradition of ideas that derive from him.

Its critics are Thomists. Brian Shanley and John Knasas argued that analytic philosophy's treatment of existence as a second-level predicate, a property of concepts, cannot accommodate Aquinas's *esse* as the act of a thing, so analytical Thomism risks keeping the vocabulary and losing the metaphysics; Kenny's rejection of *ipsum esse* is their example. Christopher Martin argued that analytic form cannot be wholly separated from analytic theses, some of which Thomism cannot accept, and that its habit of isolating single questions leads toward reductionism. Shanley's verdict on Kenny is the sharpest form of the objection: a reader who rejects the act of being can be an interpreter of Aquinas but not a Thomist. Haldane's position is that Thomist theses can be stated in analytic language and argued there, which makes the question about *esse* one to be argued, not a reason to stop (summary in the *Philosophica* encyclopedia article "Tomismo analítico"). The bibliography's tier 3 lists the main books ([08-bibliography.md](08-bibliography.md)).

## Aquinas today

Five lines of work now carry most of the attention.

**The texts.** The Leonine edition continues, volume by volume. Roberto Busa's *Index Thomisticus*, a concordance of every word in the corpus begun with IBM in 1949 and printed in 1974–80, is often called the first project in humanities computing; its successor is the online Corpus Thomisticum, from which this dossier quotes the Latin. The historical and theological reading of Jean-Pierre Torrell (*Saint Thomas Aquinas*, vol. 1, 1993) and the "Brief Catalogue" of works have become the scholarly standard for dates (see [01-life-and-times.md](01-life-and-times.md) and [02-works-and-texts.md](02-works-and-texts.md)).

**The Church.** John Paul II's encyclical *Fides et ratio* (1998) both said that the Church has no philosophy of her own and does not canonize any one philosophy, and praised Aquinas as a model of the harmony of faith and reason. Readers on each side of the Thomist question quote one half.

**Ethics and law.** Natural law theory in jurisprudence (John Finnis's *Natural Law and Natural Rights*, 1980), virtue ethics after Anscombe and Philippa Foot, and [Alasdair MacIntyre](../../eras/05-20th-century/alasdair-macintyre.md)'s Thomism (*Whose Justice? Which Rationality?*, 1988; *Three Rival Versions of Moral Enquiry*, 1990) are the most widely read Thomist work outside Catholic institutions. Debates 7 and 8 below.

**Metaphysics and philosophy of religion.** Classical theism, divine simplicity and the cosmological arguments have revived, with Edward Feser, Brian Davies and Eleonore Stump defending versions and Graham Oppy and Kenny among the critics (Arguments 1–5, 7). Hylomorphism has returned to analytic metaphysics and philosophy of mind, and the debate over what survives death has become one of the busiest in Aquinas studies (debate 9).

**Theology.** Protestant theologians have read him more sympathetically since the mid-twentieth century. The sharpest exception is the oldest: Karl Barth, in the preface to *Church Dogmatics* I/1 (1932), called the analogy of being an invention of Antichrist and the decisive reason he could not become a Catholic, with the Jesuit Erich Przywara as his target (Argument 8). John Milbank and Catherine Pickstock's *Truth in Aquinas* (2001) claimed him for "Radical Orthodoxy", and Thomist critics replied that they had made him a Neoplatonist. A political debate has also reopened, between readers who, with Maritain, find in Aquinas a basis for liberal democracy and human rights, and "integralist" readers who stress his subordination of temporal to spiritual power.

## Live debates

Debates that [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md) already sets out with their texts are given here only as a state of the question, with a pointer.

**(1) Was 1277 aimed at Thomas?** The question has two parts. On the content of Tempier's list, Roland Hissette's study (1977) found few propositions that clearly touch Thomas; John Wippel (1995) argued that a number of them were taken from his writings, and the SEP's article on Aquinas sides broadly with Wippel when it says that a significant number seem to implicate him. On procedure, Robert Wielockx's thesis of a separate process against Thomas, opened in March 1277 and stopped by the curia, was generally accepted for two decades; Wippel and Hans Thijssen have since questioned the reading of Pecham's letter on which it rests. Jean-Pierre Torrell's biography accepts that a censure of the unity of form and of prime matter as pure potency was planned and cut short. The Oxford list of the same month is the undisputed case.

**(2) Is Aquinas a philosopher?** In 1931 Émile Bréhier denied that a Christian philosophy is possible: either reason answers to a dogmatic authority and is not philosophy, or the history shows no new concept that Christianity gave philosophy. Gilson answered on the history: revelation generated rational ideas, being as existence and creation among them, which were then argued philosophically. Maritain distinguished the nature of philosophy, which is purely rational, from its state in a Christian thinker. Mandonnet and Van Steenberghen, Thomists both, agreed with Bréhier that the term confuses philosophy with theology (IEP). The quarrel has a current form. Norman Kretzmann's *The Metaphysics of Theism* (1997) read *Summa contra Gentiles* I–III as natural theology that stands without revelation, and analytic readers generally extract the philosophy from the theology. Mark Jordan's *Rewritten Theology* (2006) argues that Aquinas wrote no philosophy as such, only theology that uses philosophy, and that extraction misreads the genre. Aquinas's own formula sits between them: "Since therefore grace does not destroy nature but perfects it, natural reason should minister to faith as the natural bent of the will ministers to charity" (ST Ia Q1 a8 ad 2, tr. Fathers of the English Dominican Province, 1920).

**(3) How central is *esse*, and is it coherent?** Gilson, Fabro, Joseph Owens and John Wippel (*The Metaphysical Thought of Thomas Aquinas*, 2000) make the act of being the centre of the metaphysics; Thomists of the commentator tradition reply that Gilson's "existential" Thomas is partly Gilson's. Within the existential camp the disputes are over how the real distinction is proved, which the IEP sets out as three readings of *De ente* ch. 4 (Argument 6). Outside it, Kenny's *Aquinas on Being* (2002) argues that "being" in Aquinas shifts between senses and that subsistent being itself is not a coherent notion; Thomist reviewers, Gyula Klima and Stephen Brock among them, have replied with semantic reconstructions that keep it.

**(4) Analogy.** Cajetan's *On the Analogy of Names* (1498) made proper proportionality the Thomist doctrine. Ralph McInerny (*The Logic of Analogy*, 1961; *Aquinas and Analogy*, 1996) argued that Cajetan misread Thomas, who treats analogy as a matter of logic, of how one name is said of several things in related senses, not as a metaphysics of proportions. The SEP's article on medieval analogy notes that Aquinas's own texts vary between the *De veritate* and the *Summa*. Scotus's objection, that without a univocal concept of being natural theology equivocates, has been revived by modern Scotists, and Barth's rejection of the analogy of being, answered by Przywara and later by Thomist and Catholic theologians, keeps the question alive in theology (Argument 8).

**(5) Pure nature and the natural desire to see God.** The commentators from Cajetan to Garrigou-Lagrange held that human nature has a proportionate natural end, so that the vision of God is a wholly gratuitous addition. De Lubac's *Surnaturel* (1946) and *The Mystery of the Supernatural* (1965) argued that Aquinas taught a natural desire for the vision itself and that "pure nature" was the commentators' invention. *Humani generis* (1950) defended the possibility of an intellectual creature not ordered to the vision. The debate revived after 2000: Lawrence Feingold (*The Natural Desire to See God according to St. Thomas Aquinas and His Interpreters*, 2010) and Steven Long (*Natura Pura*, 2010) defend the commentators' reading, and John Milbank (*The Suspended Middle*, 2005) defends and radicalizes de Lubac. The texts are ST Ia-IIae Q3 a8 and Q62 a1 (Argument 14; tension 3 in [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md)).

**(6) Freedom under God's causality.** The *de auxiliis* quarrel is still argued as a question about Aquinas. Báñez and Garrigou-Lagrange read him as teaching physical premotion. Bernard Lonergan's studies of operative grace (articles 1941–42; *Grace and Freedom*, 1971) argued that Thomas did not teach Báñez's premotion and that the commentators had misread his account of divine causality. Maritain, in *Existence and the Existent* (1947), proposed that God's motions toward the good can be broken by a creature's free failure to consider the rule, which makes sin the creature's first initiative. Among philosophers the SEP reports two camps: compatibilist readings (Kenny, Robert Pasnau, Brian Shanley and others) on which freedom fits causal and theological determinism, and libertarian readings, divided between Stump's, on which the will need only be an ultimate creaturely source, and those requiring alternative possibilities (Tobias Hoffmann and Cyrille Michon). Tensions 1 and 5 in [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md) give the texts.

**(7) What grounds natural law?** Germain Grisez (1965), John Finnis (1980) and Joseph Boyle read ST Ia-IIae Q94 a2 as a list of basic goods grasped as self-evident by practical reason, not inferred from facts about human nature, so that Hume's is–ought objection misses. Ralph McInerny objected that this cuts practical reason off from nature, and Finnis and Grisez replied in "The Basic Principles of Natural Law: A Reply to Ralph McInerny" (*American Journal of Jurisprudence* 26, 1981). Russell Hittinger's *A Critique of the New Natural Law Theory* (1987) argued that the theory fails because it cannot show how and why nature is normative without a philosophical anthropology and metaphysics. Jean Porter and Steven Long read the inclinations as natural facts that ground the goods. The first precept itself, "good is to be done and pursued, and evil is to be avoided" (ST Ia-IIae Q94 a2, tr. 1920), is agreed by all; its relation to nature is what is disputed (Argument 15).

**(8) Is double effect in Aquinas?** Joseph Mangan's "An Historical Analysis of the Principle of Double Effect" (*Theological Studies*, 1949) traced the principle to IIa-IIae Q64 a7. Peter Knauer (1965) reread it in terms of proportionate reason, the root of the "proportionalism" of the 1970s. Christopher Kaczor (*Theological Studies*, 1998) agreed with Knauer that double-effect reasoning changed in character between Aquinas and the manualists, and argued that proportionalism extends the Jesuit manuals rather than recovering Aquinas. Others read Q64 a7 as permitting only proportionate defence, with no general principle in view. Anscombe (1982) defended the intending–foreseeing line on its own merits (Argument 16).

**(9) Does the person survive death?** Aquinas holds that a human being is the whole composite (ST Ia Q75 a4), that the separated soul is not a person, "since it is a part of the human species" (ST Ia Q75 a4 ad 2, tr. 1920), and that the resurrection is needed for our survival. The "corruptionists" (Patrick Toner, 2009; Christina Van Dyke, 2012; Turner Nevitt, 2014) conclude that the human being ceases to exist at death, while the soul survives as a part and not a person, and Nevitt argues for an intermittent existence restored at the resurrection. The "survivalists" (Christopher Brown, 2007; Jeffrey Brower, 2014; Stump, 2022) hold that the person survives, constituted by the soul alone, since otherwise the doctrine of the intermediate state is lost (SEP, "Thomas Aquinas", §5; Argument 11).

## Misconceptions, in full form

The [library entry](../../eras/02-medieval/thomas-aquinas.md)'s "Misreadings to avoid" and the [study guide](../../study/aquinas-summa.md)'s "Common misreadings" are the short forms; the library's [misconceptions list](../../misconceptions.md) (item 8) gives the general warning against "Aquinas proved God, end of story".

- **"The Five Ways are five freestanding proofs, or an early intelligent-design argument."** They are five compressed arguments in one article of a theology textbook, each ending in a first principle, as in the First Way's "and this everyone understands to be God" (ST Ia Q2 a3, tr. 1920), and each depending on the metaphysics of act and potency and of causal order that the *Summa* develops elsewhere (Arguments 1–5). Presented without that metaphysics they look like word-magic, as the misconceptions list warns. The Fifth Way argues from the regular end-directed activity of things without minds, not from the improbable complexity of organisms, so it is closer to Aristotle's teleology than to Paley's watch (Argument 5). What the Ways establish is only a first mover, first cause, necessary being, maximum and governor; that this is the God of Christian belief is argued over the following questions (Arguments 6–8).

- **"The First Way says God started the universe long ago."** The series in the First and Second Ways are essentially ordered: each member acts only while it is acted on now. Aquinas thought a temporal beginning could not be demonstrated at all: "By faith alone do we hold, and by no demonstration can it be proved, that the world did not always exist" (ST Ia Q46 a2, tr. Fathers of the English Dominican Province, 1920). His arguments are meant to work even if the world had no beginning (Argument 9).

- **"Once reason proves God, faith is redundant."** The truths reason can reach are, he says, "not articles of faith, but are preambles to the articles" (ST Ia Q2 a2 ad 1, tr. 1920), and the same reply allows that someone who cannot follow a proof may hold those truths by faith. The Trinity, the Incarnation and the beginning of the world are beyond demonstration, though Aquinas holds that arguments against them can always be answered.

- **"Natural law means: do what is natural."** Natural law is the rational creature's participation in the eternal law. Its first precept is a principle of practical reason, and the inclinations count only as reason grasps and orders them; the study guide's argument (d) and Argument 15 take this up. How much the inclinations ground the goods is debate 7, but no reading makes biology alone the rule.

- **"Aquinas baptized Aristotle."** He used Aristotle's physics, psychology and ethics, but his metaphysics of *esse* has no counterpart in Aristotle; it draws on Avicenna's distinction of essence and existence and on the Neoplatonic doctrine of participation that he read in Pseudo-Dionysius, Augustine and the *Book of Causes*, as Fabro and Geiger showed. He also rejected Aristotle's arguments for the eternity of the world as demonstrations. See the backward sections of [05-conversations.md](05-conversations.md).

- **"In the Middle Ages Aquinas was the Church's official philosopher."** In his lifetime and for a generation after, he was a contested master: some of his theses were condemned at Oxford, his works were "corrected" by Franciscans, and the fourteenth century followed Scotus and Ockham as much as him. His official status dates from 1567 (Doctor of the Church) and, for philosophy, from 1879 and the legislation of 1914–17. The picture of a thirteenth-century summit followed by decline is a neo-Thomist construction, which the SEP now reverses: seen as a philosopher, Aquinas looks less like the culmination of an era than the beginning of a renaissance.

- **"Thomism is simply what Thomas taught."** Several doctrines long taught as Thomist are disputed as readings of Thomas: analogy of proportionality (debate 4), pure nature (debate 5), physical premotion (debate 6), and the textbook ordering of the twenty-four theses. Thomas also held views later Thomists dropped, such as his view that the human soul is infused only after the embryo has had vegetative and sensitive souls (ST Ia Q118 a2 ad 2). The dossier keeps "Aquinas" for the texts and "Thomism" for the traditions.

- **"Aquinas held a 'double truth' against the Averroists, or they held one against him."** He held that truth is one and that a conclusion contrary to faith must rest on a faulty argument; *On the Unity of the Intellect* ch. 5 attacks an opponent who reportedly concluded one thing by reason and held the opposite by faith (Argument 12). But no medieval author defended two contradictory truths, as the SEP reports. The charge comes from Tempier's 1277 prologue and describes, at most, a way of teaching Aristotle's conclusions while declining to assert them.

## Myths about the life

[01-life-and-times.md](01-life-and-times.md) ("Weighing the sources") sorts the stories of the life by witness: the hermit's prophecy, the family's detention and the temptress, the "dumb ox", the crucifix that spoke, the levitations and the "straw" of December 1273 rest on William of Tocco, Bernard Gui and the canonization inquiries of 1319 and 1321, written to support or drawn from the case for sainthood. They are reported in this dossier as testimony, not as fact. The myths below concern the work and its afterlife.

- **Angels on the head of a pin.** No scholastic, Aquinas included, asked how many angels can dance on a pin. The nearest text in the *Summa* asks whether several angels can be in the same place and answers no: "There are not two angels in the same place. The reason of this is because it is impossible for two complete causes to be the causes immediately of one and the same thing" (ST Ia Q52 a3, tr. 1920). An angel is "in" a place only by acting on it, so the question is about causation, not crowding. The pinhead joke is a later polemic against scholasticism; its earliest attestations are usually traced to seventeenth-century Protestant writers.

- **The *Summa* on the altar at Trent.** The *Catholic Encyclopedia*, and *Aeterni Patris* before it, say that the fathers of the Council of Trent laid the *Summa* on the altar beside the Scriptures. The council's acts are not cited for it, and historians treat it as a tradition. What is documented is that Trent's decrees on justification and the sacraments draw heavily on Thomist theology, and that Thomist theologians were prominent among its advisers.

- **Poisoned by Charles of Anjou.** Dante has Hugh Capet say that Charles sent Thomas back to heaven (*Purgatorio* XX.69), and later writers took this as a report of murder. His companions' accounts give a blow to the head on the road and an illness; there is no evidence of poison ([01-life-and-times.md](01-life-and-times.md)).

- **"Beware the man of one book."** The saying *timeo hominem unius libri* is often attributed to Aquinas. It is not found in his works, and the attribution rests on later tradition.

- **"All I have written is straw" as a repudiation of reason.** The "straw" remark is testimony about a religious experience, and its wording varies with the witness and the edition. Even taken as reported, it compares what he had written with what he had seen, and does not say the writing was false. He made no retraction, and his profession of faith at Fossanova submitted his writings to the Church's judgment without disowning them.
