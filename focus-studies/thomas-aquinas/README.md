# Focus Study: Thomas Aquinas (c. 1225 – 1274)

## The figure in brief

Thomas Aquinas was born about 1225 at Roccasecca, between Rome and Naples, into a family of minor nobility, and was given as a boy to the Benedictine abbey of Monte Cassino. At the University of Naples he met two things that shaped the rest of his life: the newly translated works of Aristotle and the Dominicans, a young order of preaching friars. He joined them against his family's will, studied under Albert the Great in Paris and Cologne, and became a master of theology at Paris in 1256. Over the next seventeen years he taught in Paris and in Italy and wrote more than eight million words: commentaries on Scripture, on Peter Lombard and on Aristotle, disputed questions, short treatises, and two great syntheses, the *Summa contra Gentiles* and the unfinished *Summa Theologiae*. He stopped writing in December 1273 and died in March 1274 on his way to the Council of Lyon. Some positions linked with his teaching were caught up in the Paris condemnations of 1277; he was canonized in 1323.

He matters to this library because he built the most complete medieval system on Aristotle's terms: act and potency, matter and form, the four causes. He then used it to argue for God's existence and nature, for the soul as the form of the body, for happiness as the human end and for a natural law that reason can know. Much of the early modern period is written against him, or against the school philosophy that carried his name: Descartes, Hobbes, Locke and Spinoza. His terms still organize arguments about cosmological proofs, double effect, natural law and the relation of faith and reason.

## Why he rewards weeks of study

First, *the method*. Every article of the *Summa* states its question, gives the strongest objections, cites an authority on the other side, answers, and then replies to each objection. Once you can read that form, a short article teaches you the debate as well as the verdict, and you learn to read the replies, where much of the philosophy is.

Second, *the arguments*. The Five Ways are among the most discussed arguments in philosophy and among the most often misread. Read in their article, with the essentially ordered series, the real distinction of essence and existence and divine simplicity behind them, they are a different and stronger argument than the one usually attacked. The same holds for analogy, evil as privation, the soul as form, abstraction and the first precept of natural law.

Third, *the bridge*. Aquinas is where Aristotle, Augustine, Avicenna, Averroes and Maimonides meet. Knowing him well makes the later medievals, the early moderns and modern Thomism legible as replies.

## The study plan at a glance

The library's [learning path](../../learning-path.md) meets Aquinas in **Stage 2** (Medieval and Renaissance Bridge): *Summa Theologiae* Ia Q2 (the Five Ways), Q3–11 in selection, Ia-IIae Q90–97 in selection (natural law) and *On Being and Essence* in selection, read in Davies & Leftow's *Questions on God* with McDermott's *Concise Translation* for orientation and Feser's *Aquinas: A Beginner's Guide* alongside. This dossier keeps those editions and that core, and widens it to the soul, knowing, happiness and the relation of faith and reason. [07-study-plan.md](07-study-plan.md) lays out an **8-week plan** at the path's weekly rhythm:

| Week | Reading | Theme |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | ST Prologue; Ia Q1 | Method and setting: sacred doctrine and the disputed question |
| 2 | ST Ia Q2; SCG I.13 | The Five Ways |
| 3 | *On Being and Essence* ch. 4; ST Ia Q3, Q13 | Being and essence, simplicity, analogy |
| 4 | ST Ia Q4–11 sel., Q19, Q25, Q46, Q48–49; *On the Eternity of the World* | God's attributes, creation, evil |
| 5 | ST Ia Q75–76, Q84–86; *On the Unity of the Intellect* sel. | Soul and knowing |
| 6 | ST Ia-IIae Q1–5; Q6–17 and Q55–67 sel. | Happiness, action, virtue |
| 7 | ST Ia-IIae Q90–97; IIa-IIae Q40, Q64 a7 | Law, war and double effect |
| 8 | SCG I.3–8; ST IIa-IIae Q1–2; capstone essay | Faith and reason |

Weeks 1, 2 and 7 also use the library's [study guide to the *Summa* selections](../../study/aquinas-summa.md).

The honest total is about **45 hours: 34.5 at the desk, about 3.5 of assigned media, and about 7.2 of daily card review**, with weekly loads of about 4.8 to 6.4 hours. These hours sit on top of Stage 2's own budget, and Stage 2 is the path's most compressed stage (eight weeks, about 62 hours all-in). The plan file states the net addition and offers two ways to fit it: the whole plan on the path's comfortable calendar, or Weeks 1–4 as the stage's Aquinas row and Weeks 5–8 after its gate.

## On the quotations

The dossier quotes only public-domain translations, so that every quotation can be checked word for word: the *Summa Theologiae* in the translation of the Fathers of the English Dominican Province (Second and Revised Edition, 1920), as printed at [New Advent](https://www.newadvent.org/summa/), and the *Summa contra Gentiles* in the English Dominican translation of 1924. The learning path's rule is not to read the full *Summa* in that old translation, and the dossier keeps it: read Davies & Leftow and McDermott, and use the dossier's quotations to find the passage. *On Being and Essence*, *On the Unity of the Intellect* and *On the Eternity of the World* have no public-domain English translation, so the dossier paraphrases them; where it quotes, it gives the Latin of the [Corpus Thomisticum](https://www.corpusthomisticum.org/) with a plain gloss labelled as the dossier's own. References follow the standard divisions every edition prints: part, question and article for the *Summa Theologiae* (ST Ia Q2 a3; "ad 2" is the reply to the second objection), book and chapter for the *Summa contra Gentiles* (SCG I.13).
