# Life and Times: Roccasecca, Naples, Paris, Cologne, Orvieto, Rome, Naples, Fossanova
**English** | [简体中文](../../zh/focus-studies/thomas-aquinas/01-life-and-times.md)

Thomas Aquinas wrote more than eight million words in about twenty years and almost none of them about himself. The SEP puts it plainly: in his own writings he tells us almost nothing about his personal life. The outline of the career, which comes from university and Dominican records, the dates of the works and the testimony of people who knew him, is fairly secure. The colour comes from later sources: the testimony gathered for his canonization at Naples in 1319 and at Fossanova in 1321, the life written by William of Tocco, who helped prepare the case, and the lives by Bernard Gui, Peter Calo (c. 1300) and others that drew on the same circle. Those sources are where the best-known stories live, among them the hermit's prophecy, the brothers' kidnapping and the temptress, the "dumb ox", and the "straw" of 1273. This file flags them as it goes. The standard modern life is Jean-Pierre Torrell's *Saint Thomas Aquinas*, vol. 1, *The Person and His Work* (French 1993; English translation of the third edition, 2022). Pasquale Porro's *Thomas Aquinas: A Historical and Philosophical Profile* (2012; English 2016) and Dominic Legge's chapter in *The New Cambridge Companion to Aquinas* (ed. Stump and White, 2022) are shorter. The *Summa Theologiae* is quoted from the translation by the Fathers of the English Dominican Province (1920). The 1912 *Catholic Encyclopedia* article, which is public domain, is quoted for the early biographers' stories, which it repeats largely as fact and which are reported here as stories. Works with no public-domain English text in this dossier's corpus are paraphrased, or quoted in Latin from the Corpus Thomisticum with a gloss.

## Roccasecca and Monte Cassino, c. 1225–1239

Thomas was born around 1225 near Aquino, about halfway between Rome and Naples, most likely in the family castle at Roccasecca. The year is not certain. The 1912 *Catholic Encyclopedia* notes that Tolomeo of Lucca, an early biographer, recorded doubt about his age at his death, and gives 1225 or 1227; the IEP gives 1224–1226; the SEP says about 1225. This dossier keeps the conventional c. 1225. Roccasecca lay in the Kingdom of Sicily, the southern kingdom ruled by the emperor Frederick II, close to the border with the Papal States, and that border mattered for the whole of his youth.

His father, Landulf, was a knight in Frederick's service, and his mother, Theodora, came from a Neapolitan noble family. The *Catholic Encyclopedia* makes the father Count of Aquino and gives the family imperial and royal relatives; recent accounts are more modest and say only that the family was well off and held the castle. Thomas was the youngest of at least nine children and the youngest of four sons. His older brothers became soldiers, and the youngest son was meant for the church.

At about five, around 1230, he was given as an oblate to the Benedictine abbey of Monte Cassino, the house founded by Benedict of Nursia. His uncle Sinibald was abbot there, and the family plainly hoped that Thomas would rise to the abbacy in time, which was an ordinary career for a younger son of the southern nobility. The SEP says his parents hoped he would take a leading part in the abbey's life. One story from this period survives in the early lives: the boy kept asking his teacher "What is God?" The story suits the man too well to be relied on, and it is the earliest of many.

In early 1239 the war between Frederick II and Pope Gregory IX spilled into the abbey, and his parents moved him.

## Naples: Aristotle and the Dominicans, 1239–1244

In the autumn of 1239, at about fourteen, Thomas went to study the arts at the university in Naples, the *studium generale* of the emperor's kingdom, which still bears Frederick II's name (the library entry rightly stresses that it was a state foundation, which was unusual). The university was the emperor's, not the church's, and Aristotle's newly translated works, which had been repeatedly restricted at Paris, were taught there openly. One of his teachers was Peter of Ireland (Petrus de Ibernia), who taught logic and natural philosophy and is often credited with introducing Thomas to Aristotle (the IEP hedges the claim). The *Catholic Encyclopedia*, following the early lives, names his grammar teacher as Pietro Martini; Tocco's life is cited elsewhere for Martin of Dacia, and the identification is uncertain.

Much of [Aristotle](../../eras/01-ancient/aristotle.md)'s work, lost to Latin readers for centuries, had reached the universities in the previous hundred years in translations from Arabic and Greek, together with the commentaries of [Avicenna](../../eras/02-medieval/avicenna.md) and [Averroes](../../eras/02-medieval/averroes.md). It was exciting and dangerous. The IEP lists the three teachings that troubled Christian readers: that God did not exercise providence over human affairs, that the world had always existed, and that the human soul was mortal. Thomas's adult work would deal with all three.

In Naples he also met the Order of Preachers, the Dominicans, founded by Dominic de Guzmán (c. 1170–1221) as an order of friars devoted to preaching and study. The friars were mendicants, living on alms rather than estates, and they recruited in the university towns. The SEP describes it as a new order of priests given to preaching and learning. For a young man of Thomas's family a friar's habit was a social step down from a Benedictine abbacy, and it also took him out of the family's reach, since the order would move him wherever it needed him. He took the Dominican habit at Naples, in April 1244 by the IEP's date, at about nineteen, under the influence of the Dominican preacher John of St Julian.

## The family's resistance, 1244–1245

The family did not accept it. The Dominicans, expecting trouble, sent the novice north toward Rome, and from there toward Paris. On the road, near Aquapendente in the early lives, his brothers, who were soldiers in Frederick's army, seized him at his mother's request and brought him back. He was held in the family's castles, at Monte San Giovanni Campano and at Roccasecca, while the family tried to change his mind.

How long he was held is reported differently. The SEP says his parents kept him at Roccasecca for a year; the IEP puts the seizure in late 1244 or early 1245 and his return to the Dominicans in the autumn of 1245; the 1912 *Catholic Encyclopedia*, following the early lives, says "nearly two years" and later "eighteen months or two years". The modern dating gives about a year.

The early biographies add the famous stories. His brothers sent a woman to his room to seduce him; he drove her out with a burning brand from the fire. That night, the story continues, two angels girded him with a cord of perpetual chastity, a secret he told to his companion Reginald of Piperno near the end of his life. The story is in the testimony collected for the canonization and in the early lives, and nothing in Thomas's own writing confirms or contradicts it. The early lives also say that his sisters brought him books, that he read the Bible, Aristotle and Peter Lombard's *Sentences* in captivity, and that he was finally let out of a window to the friars, who found, in Calo's words as the *Catholic Encyclopedia* gives them, that "he had made as much progress as if he had been in a studium generale". The IEP adds a detail with more support: Thomas stayed close to his family for the rest of his life, staying in their castles on his travels and late in life acting as executor of a brother-in-law's will. In 1245 his mother gave way, and he went back to the Dominicans, who sent him to Rome and then north with the order's Master General, John of Wildeshausen (John the Teutonic).

## Paris and Cologne with Albert, 1245–1252

The order sent him to Paris, where he studied for about three years, from 1245 to 1248, probably in the arts and at the Dominican convent of Saint-Jacques. There he met Albert the Great (Albertus Magnus), a German Dominican who was then master of theology at Paris and the leading figure in the Latin effort to absorb the new Greek and Arabic philosophy and science. The SEP calls him the ideal mentor for Thomas. In 1248 the Dominicans founded a new *studium generale* at Cologne and sent Albert to run it. Thomas went with him and spent the next four years there as his student and assistant. He began his own teaching there, with literal lectures on Isaiah, Jeremiah and Lamentations, and was probably ordained priest at Cologne around 1250.

The best-known story of these years is the "dumb ox". Fellow students took the large, silent Italian for slow; when Albert heard him defend a hard thesis, the *Catholic Encyclopedia* reports, he said: "We call this young man a dumb ox, but his bellowing in doctrine will one day resound throughout the world." The story comes from the early lives and cannot be checked. The reports of his size, from Calo and others, may well be accurate: they say he was tall and heavy, with a large head and a complexion "like the colour of new wheat".

Albert's influence went deeper than the anecdote. Thomas took from him the conviction, as the SEP summarises it, that deep engagement with philosophy and science could only do the Christian faith good. Where Albert was encyclopaedic, collecting and paraphrasing, Thomas would be analytic and selective. Both stood against the many theologians who thought the new Aristotle a danger to the faith.

## Bachelor at Paris, 1252–1256

In 1252 the Master General asked Albert to name a student to train for the doctorate in theology at Paris. Albert chose Thomas, then about twenty-seven, though, the SEP notes, he was two years under the minimum age. From 1252 to 1256 Thomas was a bachelor of the *Sentences*, the advanced student who lectured on the *Sentences* of Peter Lombard (c. 1150), the standard theology textbook of the medieval university. His lectures became the *Scriptum super libros Sententiarum*, his first large work, and the first of his four theological syntheses.

He also wrote two short works for his fellow students in these years. *On the Principles of Nature* (*De principiis naturae*) summarises Aristotle's physics of matter, form and change. *On Being and Essence* (*De ente et essentia*), addressed *ad fratres et socios suos*, to his brothers and companions, sets out the distinction between what a thing is and that it is, which week 3 of this dossier studies (Argument 6 in [the arguments file](04-arguments.md)). Dates vary within the bachelor years: the IEP gives 1252–1253, the SEP 1252–1256.

## Master of theology and the mendicant controversy, 1256–1259

Thomas became a master just as the university was trying to push the friars out. The quarrel began as a dispute between the university and the city after the city watch killed a student. The secular masters, the teachers who were not members of religious orders, suspended their lectures and required every new doctor to swear to join such strikes in future. The Dominicans and Franciscans kept teaching and refused the oath. The secular master William of Saint-Amour then widened the attack into a denial that friars had any right to university chairs, or to live by begging at all, in his *On the Dangers of the Last Times* (*De periculis novissimorum temporum*). Thomas answered in *Against Those Who Attack the Worship of God and Religion* (*Contra impugnantes*). Pope Alexander IV condemned William's book on 5 October 1256 and ordered the friars admitted.

Thomas incepted as master in the spring of 1256, at about thirty-one, again under the statutory age, and by the pope's direct order. His inaugural lecture, by the early lives' account, took as its text Psalm 103:13 (104:13 in modern numbering), on God watering the hills from above. The older lives date his promotion to 23 October 1257, and the quarrel delayed his and the Franciscan Bonaventure's full acceptance by the secular masters, which is why the library entry gives the year as 1256/57. The story that the two friends contested which should be promoted first is a tradition.

As regent master, holding the Dominican chair, he had three duties: to lecture on the Bible, to dispute, and to preach. The disputed question was the method of the university. A master put a question; a bachelor answered the objections raised from the floor; on a later day the master "determined" it, setting out the arguments on each side and his own answer. The *Disputed Questions on Truth* (1256–1259), twenty-nine questions in 253 articles, come from these years, as do Quodlibets 7–11, the open sessions in Advent and Lent at which anyone could ask anything. The *Summa Theologiae* would later keep the shape of the disputation in its articles: objections, a *sed contra* ("on the contrary"), Thomas's answer, and replies (see [the *Summa* study guide](../../study/aquinas-summa.md) for how to read one). He also commented on Boethius's *De trinitate*, with its account of the division of the sciences, and began the *Summa contra Gentiles* by the end of the regency.

## Italy: Naples, Orvieto, Rome, Viterbo, 1259–1268

The Dominicans rotated their masters. In 1259 Thomas sat with Albert and Peter of Tarentaise (later Pope Innocent V) on a commission at the order's general chapter at Valenciennes that drew up a plan of studies for the order, and then he went back to Italy, where he stayed for nine years. The years are less well documented than the Paris terms, and the accounts differ in detail. He was probably at Naples first (the provincial chapter made him a preacher general in September 1260). From September 1261 he was lector at the Dominican house at Orvieto, responsible for the training of friars who could not attend a *studium generale*. There he finished the *Summa contra Gentiles*, which the SEP dates 1259–1265. A tradition reported in the 1912 *Catholic Encyclopedia* says that Raymond of Peñafort asked for the work for missionaries working among Muslims and Jews in Spain; scholars disagree about how far this explains the book's purpose. For Pope Urban IV he also wrote the *Catena aurea*, a running commentary on the Gospels assembled from the Church Fathers; *Against the Errors of the Greeks*; and the liturgy for the new feast of Corpus Christi (1264), including the hymns *Pange lingua* and *Lauda Sion*. Mandonnet's case for his authorship is now generally accepted.

In 1265 the Roman provincial chapter at Anagni sent him to Rome to open a house of studies for the province at the convent of Santa Sabina. He taught there from 1265 to 1268, and there he began the *Summa Theologiae*. Its prologue says who it was for:

> Because the Master of Catholic Truth ought not only to teach the proficient, but also to instruct beginners (according to the Apostle: As Unto Little Ones in Christ, I Gave You Milk to Drink, Not Meat-- 1 Cor. iii. 1, 2)--we purpose in this book to treat of whatever belongs to the Christian Religion, in such a way as may tend to the instruction of beginners.

— *Summa Theologiae*, Prologue, tr. Fathers of the English Dominican Province (1920)

Who the "beginners" were is debated. The older view made the *Summa* a university textbook meant to replace Lombard's *Sentences*; recent scholarship, the IEP reports, holds that he wrote it for Dominican students preparing for priestly ministry, who needed theology set out "according to the order of the subject-matter", as the prologue goes on to say, rather than in the order of a commentary. The first part was finished by about 1268. Rome also produced the disputed questions *On the Power of God* (1265–1266) and *On the Soul* (1266–1267), the unfinished *Compendium of Theology* for Reginald of Piperno, his companion and secretary from about this time, and the commentary on Aristotle's *De anima* (1267–1268). The 1912 *Catholic Encyclopedia* reports from the early lives that Clement IV named him archbishop of Naples in 1265 and that he refused. In 1268 he was at Viterbo, and in November he set out from there for Paris with Reginald.

## Paris again: Averroists and Franciscans, 1268–1272

A second regency at Paris was unusual, as the SEP points out. The reasons are not stated in any document; the likeliest are three disputes that were running when he arrived. The secular masters had renewed the attack on the friars. In the arts faculty some masters, Siger of Brabant the most prominent, taught Aristotle as Averroes read him, including conclusions that contradicted the faith: that the world is eternal and that there is one intellect for all human beings. And many theologians, a good number of them Franciscans, had come to suspect Aristotle himself, and Thomas with him.

He wrote against all three. *On the Unity of the Intellect against the Averroists* (1270) argues, from Aristotle's own text and the Greek commentators, that each human being has their own intellect (Argument 12 in [the arguments file](04-arguments.md)). It ends with a challenge to its unnamed opponent:

> non loquatur in angulis nec coram pueris qui nesciunt de tam arduis iudicare; sed contra hoc scriptum rescribat, si audet

— *De unitate intellectus*, ch. 5 (Latin, Corpus Thomisticum). Gloss (this dossier's own): "let him not talk in corners, or before boys who do not know how to judge such difficult matters, but let him write a reply to this writing, if he dares."

On 10 December 1270 the bishop of Paris, Stephen Tempier, condemned thirteen Aristotelian and Averroist propositions, the unity of the intellect and the eternity of the world among them by the usual account, and excommunicated anyone who taught them. *On the Eternity of the World* (1271), subtitled *against the grumblers* (*contra murmurantes*), turns to the other front. In the *Summa* (Ia Q46 a2) Thomas holds that the world's beginning is known by faith and cannot be demonstrated; the treatise argues that a world created by God and yet without a beginning involves no contradiction (Argument 9). The "grumblers" were theologians who said that it did; the Franciscan John Pecham is often named as the main target, though the identification is argued.

The output of these four years is very large. He wrote most of the second part of the *Summa*, the Ia-IIae and IIa-IIae; the disputed questions *On Evil* (1270–1271) and *On the Virtues*; commentaries on the *Physics*, *Metaphysics*, *Nicomachean Ethics*, *On Interpretation* and other works of Aristotle; and Quodlibets 1–6 and 12. He worked with several secretaries, and the early witnesses say he could dictate to more than one at a time.

## Naples, 1272–1273

In 1272 the Dominicans of his home province asked Thomas to set up a *studium generale* wherever he chose and to staff it as he pleased. He chose Naples and went there as regent master. There he wrote much of the third part of the *Summa*, continued the Aristotle commentaries (the *Metaphysics* to 1273, the *Liber de causis* in 1272–1273) and lectured on Paul. In Lent of 1273 he preached to the people of Naples on the Creed, the Lord's Prayer, the Hail Mary and the Ten Commandments.

## The silence of December 1273

On or about 6 December 1273, while saying Mass at Naples, he had an experience that ended his writing. He stopped work on the *Summa* in the questions on penance, at IIIa Q90. When Reginald urged him to go on, he refused. The 1912 *Catholic Encyclopedia*, following Prümmer's edition of the early sources, gives his reply as: "I can do no more. Such secrets have been revealed to me that all I have written now appears to be of little value" (the Latin *modica* in that edition). The better-known version, and the one the library entry and the SEP give, has "straw": all he had written seemed to him like straw compared with what he had seen. The words go back to Reginald and reached the record through the early biographies and the testimony given at the canonization inquiry of 1319; the SEP judges the authenticity of such stories impossible to know, and the wording varies with the witness and the edition. Readers have taken the event as a mystical experience, a physical collapse, or both; the sources do not decide between them. A related story, from earlier in 1273, says that friars at Naples (the sacristan Dominic of Caserta in one account, three of the brethren in the version the *Catholic Encyclopedia* gives) saw him raised in prayer before a crucifix and heard a voice tell him "Thou hast written well of me, Thomas", to which he asked for no reward but Christ. The *Summa* was completed after his death by a "Supplement" compiled from his earlier writings, chiefly the *Sentences* commentary; Mandonnet thought the compiler was probably Reginald.

## Fossanova, March 1274

Pope Gregory X had called a general council to meet at Lyon on 1 May 1274, chiefly to restore union with the Greek church, and asked Thomas to attend and bring *Against the Errors of the Greeks*. He set out in January or February 1274 with Reginald. On the road he struck his head on a fallen branch, and he fell ill. He stayed for some days at the castle of Maenza with his niece, Countess Francesca of Ceccano, and then asked to be taken to the Cistercian abbey of Fossanova nearby. The early lives say that he quoted Psalm 131:14 on entering it: "This is my rest for ever and ever: here will I dwell, for I have chosen it". They also say that he dictated a short commentary on the Song of Songs for the monks, and give a profession of faith he made on receiving the last sacraments, which ends: "if I have written anything erroneous concerning this sacrament or other matters, I submit all to the judgment and correction of the Holy Roman Church" (as the *Catholic Encyclopedia* gives it). He died at Fossanova on 7 March 1274, aged about forty-nine. The cause is unknown. A modern suggestion is a subdural haematoma from the blow to the head. Dante's charge in the *Divine Comedy* that Charles of Anjou had him poisoned has no support in the accounts of his companions.

## After the death: 1277, 1323, 1325

Veneration began at once. The SEP notes that claims of miracles arose at once and that a long quarrel began over who would keep the body. The Cistercians of Fossanova held the body for almost a century.

His doctrine was more contested than his holiness. On 7 March 1277, three years to the day after his death, Bishop Tempier condemned 219 propositions taught at Paris. Pope John XXI had written to him on 18 January asking him to investigate, but the SEP's account of the condemnation doubts that Tempier was acting on the pope's initiative. Most of the propositions were aimed at the arts masters, but the SEP judges that a significant number seem to touch Thomas's teaching. Wippel argues that some were taken from his writings, against Hissette, who found fewer. Later in March 1277, at Oxford, Archbishop Robert Kilwardby condemned a list that included two of Thomas's distinctive doctrines: the unity of substantial form in a human being (Argument 11) and prime matter as pure potentiality. Robert Wielockx argued that Tempier began a separate process against Thomas himself in March 1277 and that the papal curia stopped it later that year; most scholars accepted the thesis, and Wippel and Hans Thijssen have since questioned it (SEP, "Condemnation of 1277", §6). Around the same time the Franciscan William de la Mare published his *Correctorium fratris Thomae*, which set quotations from Thomas beside "corrections", and Dominican disciples answered in a series of works called *Correctorium corruptorii Thomae*. For the next quarter century, in the SEP's summary, scholasticism took shape around Thomas's Dominican supporters and his Franciscan critics, and the Dominicans were eventually bound by their own statutes to promote his teaching.

In 1317 the Dominicans of the Sicilian province began the case for canonization. William of Tocco gathered the life and the miracles; the papal inquiries sat at Naples in July–September 1319 and at Fossanova in November 1321. John XXII canonized Thomas at Avignon on 18 July 1323. In 1325 the bishop of Paris declared that the 1277 condemnation should not be held to apply to Thomas's teaching, while making clear, the SEP notes, that this was not an endorsement. On 28 January 1369, by order of Urban V, the body was handed to the Dominicans and moved to their church at Toulouse, where it lies today. Pius V named him a Doctor of the Church in 1567, and Leo XIII's encyclical *Aeterni Patris* (4 August 1879) made his philosophy the guide of Catholic teaching. The later history belongs to [the reception file](06-reception-and-debates.md).

## Weighing the sources

**What is solid.** The SEP judges the chronology of the career fairly well attested. The career moves are documented: the order's chapters and appointments, the university's records, the papal letters, the dates of the works worked out by the Leonine editors and summarised in Torrell's "Brief Catalogue". The works themselves are the best evidence of where he was and what he was arguing with. For the most important of them we have more than usual: much of the *Summa contra Gentiles* survives in his own hand (Vatican Library, Vat. lat. 9850), in a hand that the SEP notes even experts find hard to read. The *Summa Theologiae*'s own prologue states its audience, and Ia Q1 a1 states its reason for existing: that the truths about God that reason can reach "would only be known by a few, and that after a long time, and with the admixture of many errors" (ST Ia Q1 a1, tr. Fathers of the English Dominican Province, 1920). That sentence is as close as Thomas comes to explaining his own life's work.

**What rests on the early lives and the inquiries.** Nearly every vivid story comes from sources written to support, or drawn from, the canonization case: William of Tocco's life, written while he was promoting the cause from 1317, Bernard Gui's life, which draws on the same material, Peter Calo's life (c. 1300), the remarks of Tolomeo of Lucca, and the depositions of 1319 and 1321. These were people who knew Thomas or knew those who did, Reginald above all, and some of the detail is surely right. But they were assembling a saint's life, with the conventions of the genre. Stories in that class include the hermit's prophecy, "What is God?", the kidnapping's details and the temptress with the angels' cord, the basket or window, the "dumb ox", the crucifix that spoke, the levitations, the "straw" and the Song of Songs at Fossanova. The 1912 *Catholic Encyclopedia* repeats them as fact. Modern biographers keep the stories but report them as testimony, and so does this dossier.

**What is disputed.** The birth year (1224–1227); how long the family held him (a year, or up to two); whether he studied at Paris or went straight to Cologne in 1245 (the *Catholic Encyclopedia* reports both routes); the date and place of the SCG and the purpose behind it; the exact order of the Italian years; what happened on 6 December 1273; whether Tempier's 1277 list was aimed at Thomas and whether a separate process against him was begun and stopped. The [reception file](06-reception-and-debates.md) takes up the later myths about the life and the work.

**What the library entry says, and where this file differs.** This file agrees with [the library entry](../../eras/02-medieval/thomas-aquinas.md) on the outline. Two points are hedged here that the entry states flatly. The entry has the family holding him "for over a year"; the SEP and IEP datings give about a year, and only the early lives give up to two. The entry reports the "straw" remark as fact; here it is reported as testimony from Reginald, and the 1912 *Catholic Encyclopedia*'s edition of the early sources gives different wording.
