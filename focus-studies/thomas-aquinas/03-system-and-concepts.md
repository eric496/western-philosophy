## The order of the system

Unlike Augustine, Aquinas did write a system, or two. The *Summa contra Gentiles* (1259–65) and the unfinished *Summa Theologiae* (1265–74) each set out the whole of Christian teaching in a planned order, and the *Summa Theologiae* says what the order is. Part I treats God and the procession of creatures from God. Part II (split into Ia-IIae and IIa-IIae) treats the rational creature's return to God by action, virtue, law and grace. Part III treats Christ, the way of that return, and breaks off in the questions on penance. Scholars often describe this outgoing and returning as an *exitus–reditus* scheme, after the Neoplatonic pattern of procession and return; the label is the readers', not Aquinas's, and how far the plan really follows it is debated (M.-D. Chenu proposed it in the 1930s, and later readers have qualified it).

The central question underneath is simple to state. What can a human mind, which knows only by starting from the senses, know about a God who is not a sensible thing, and how does a bodily, rational animal reach its end in that God? Almost every load-bearing concept in Aquinas answers part of that question. Act and potency explain change and set up the arguments for a first cause. Essence and existence explain why creatures depend on a cause for their being and why God does not. Analogy explains how words learned from creatures can be truly said of God. The soul as form of the body explains why human knowing starts with the senses. Happiness, law and virtue explain how such a creature is directed to its end, and grace explains why nature alone cannot get it there.

This file sets out those concepts in their own terms, in roughly the order of the *Summa*, before any criticism. The arguments that use them are reconstructed in [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), numbered 1–16; the scholarly fights over them are in [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md).

**Texts and quotation.** The *Summa Theologiae* is quoted from the translation by the Fathers of the English Dominican Province (Second and Revised Edition, 1920), as printed on newadvent.org, and cited by part, question and article (ST Ia Q2 a3; "ad 2" is the reply to the second objection). The learning path rules that this old translation is not the reading text: read Davies and Leftow's *Questions on God* (Cambridge Texts) and McDermott's *Concise Translation*, with Feser's *Aquinas: A Beginner's Guide* alongside. The 1920 text is quoted here only because it is public domain and anyone can check it. The *Summa contra Gentiles* is quoted from the English Dominican translation (1924), cited by book and chapter (SCG I.3). *On Being and Essence*, *On the Unity of the Intellect* and *On the Eternity of the World* have no public-domain English translation; they are paraphrased, and the one passage quoted from *On Being and Essence* is given in Latin from the Corpus Thomisticum with a plain gloss of my own.

## Sacred doctrine and philosophy

The *Summa Theologiae* opens by asking why there should be any teaching beyond philosophy at all. The answer sets the boundary on which the whole system rests. "It was necessary for man's salvation that there should be a knowledge revealed by God besides philosophical science built up by human reason" (ST Ia Q1 a1). There are two reasons. First, human beings are directed to an end, the vision of God, that exceeds what reason can grasp, and one cannot steer toward an end one does not know. Second, even the truths about God that reason can reach would, left to reason, be known poorly: "the truth about God such as reason could discover, would only be known by a few, and that after a long time, and with the admixture of many errors" (ST Ia Q1 a1).

So truths about God come in two kinds. The *Summa contra Gentiles* puts it plainly: "in those things which we hold about God there is truth in two ways" (SCG I.3, tr. English Dominican Fathers, 1924). Some, such as the Trinity, exceed reason entirely. Others, such as God's existence and unity, can be demonstrated, and philosophers have demonstrated them. Later Thomists call the second kind the *preambles of faith* (*praeambula fidei*): truths that faith presupposes and reason can prove, though most believers hold them on faith because they have neither time nor training for the proofs (ST IIa-IIae Q2 a4, paraphrased). The first kind are the *articles of faith*.

Revealed teaching (*sacra doctrina*) still counts as a science in the Aristotelian sense, because it reasons from principles to conclusions. Its principles are not self-evident to us; they are known to a higher knower: "sacred doctrine is a science because it proceeds from principles established by the light of a higher science, namely, the science of God and the blessed" (ST Ia Q1 a2). The comparison is with optics taking its principles from geometry, or music from arithmetic.

Two rules govern the border. Faith and reason cannot conflict, since both come from the same God: "those things which are naturally instilled in human reason cannot be opposed to this truth", the truth of faith (SCG I.7, tr. English Dominican Fathers, 1924). So any argument against the faith must contain a mistake somewhere. "Since faith rests upon infallible truth, and since the contrary of a truth can never be demonstrated, it is clear that the arguments brought against faith cannot be demonstrations, but are difficulties that can be answered" (ST Ia Q1 a8). And theology may use philosophy freely, because nature is not the enemy of grace: "Since therefore grace does not destroy nature but perfects it, natural reason should minister to faith as the natural bent of the will ministers to charity" (ST Ia Q1 a8 ad 2). That sentence is the nearest thing Aquinas has to a motto. The last section of this file returns to it.

The method that carries all this is the disputed question. Each article of the *Summa* states objections, a counter-authority (*sed contra*), Aquinas's answer (*respondeo*) and replies to the objections. The form came from the university practice of public disputation at Paris, where masters of theology were required to dispute as well as lecture and preach. The [study guide](../../study/aquinas-summa.md#edition-note) explains the four steps; learn them before reading any argument. [01-life-and-times.md](01-life-and-times.md) covers the Paris setting and the arrival of the newly translated Aristotle.

## Act and potency

Aquinas's metaphysics starts from Aristotle's analysis of change. A cold kettle can become hot; it is actually cold and potentially hot. Change is the passage from potency (*potentia*, capacity) to act (*actus*, actuality) in some respect. The First Way states the principle in its classic form: "For motion is nothing else than the reduction of something from potentiality to actuality. But nothing can be reduced from potentiality to actuality, except by something in a state of actuality" (ST Ia Q2 a3). "Motion" (*motus*) here means change of any kind, not only change of place, and the example is fire, actually hot, making wood actually hot.

Two features of the pair matter for everything that follows. First, act is prior to potency. A potency is always a potency *for* some act and is explained by it, and only what is already actual can actualize. Second, the pair reaches well beyond physical change. Matter is potency for form; a power such as sight is potency for its operation; an essence is potency for existence. Aquinas uses the same structure at each level, which is why the pair is the backbone of the system. The highest application is to God, who has no potency at all and is "pure act" (*actus purus*). A being with no unrealized capacity cannot change, cannot be improved, and does not depend on anything to actualize it.

The pair also explains the series in the First and Second Ways. When a hand moves a stick that moves a stone, the stick moves the stone only while and because the hand moves it. Each intermediate member is a moved mover, actualized by another here and now. Aquinas calls a series like this one ordered *per se*, and later writers call it *essentially ordered*. A series ordered *per accidens*, like a line of fathers and sons, does not need its earlier members to be acting now. The Ways argue that a *per se* series cannot run back without a first member, but Aquinas does not claim to prove that a temporal series must have had a first member (see below on creation). Arguments 1 and 2 in [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md) reconstruct the reasoning.

## Matter and form, substance and accident

A material thing, for Aquinas, is a composite of matter and form (hylomorphism, from the Greek for matter and form). Its substantial form makes it the kind of thing it is: this lump of matter is a dog, not a stone, because of the dog's form. Accidental forms, such as size, colour or position, qualify a substance that already exists. When a dog grows or turns grey it undergoes accidental change and remains the same dog. When it dies it undergoes substantial change: the dog ceases to exist and something else, a corpse, comes to be.

What persists through a substantial change? Aquinas's answer is "prime matter" (*materia prima*), matter with no form of its own, pure potency for form. Prime matter never exists by itself; it is the bare capacity in material things to become something else. This is one of his most debated doctrines, and opponents at Oxford included the thesis that prime matter is pure potency among the propositions condemned there in 1277 (SEP, "Thomas Aquinas," §9).

Matter also individuates. Two dogs share one form in kind, and what makes them two is that the form is received in different parcels of matter. Aquinas calls this "designated matter" (*materia signata*), matter considered under determinate dimensions. Hence an immaterial being cannot be one of many in a species. Every angel is its own species, a conclusion Aquinas draws explicitly (*On Being and Essence* ch. 4, paraphrased).

A substance has a strong unity that an aggregate lacks. The form of a house arranges bricks that already exist, so it is an accidental form and a house is not a substance. A living thing's substantial form makes each part what it is: a hand cut off is a hand only in name. This conviction drives the doctrine of the unity of substantial form discussed under the soul below.

## Essence and existence

The doctrine most often called Aquinas's own is the distinction between essence (*essentia*, what a thing is) and existence (*esse*, the act by which it is). He takes much of it from Avicenna and from the Neoplatonic *Book of Causes*, but gives it a new role. The early treatise *On Being and Essence* (c. 1252–56) states the starting point in a sentence:

> possum enim intelligere quid est homo vel Phoenix et tamen ignorare an esse habeat in rerum natura.
>
> (*De ente et essentia*, ch. 4 in the usual numbering; cap. 3 in the Corpus Thomisticum text.) Gloss (this dossier's, not a published translation): "For I can understand what a human being or a phoenix is and still not know whether it has existence in the nature of things."

From this Aquinas argues in stages. Whatever does not belong to the understanding of an essence comes to it from outside. Existence does not belong to the understanding of any essence we know, so existence is other than essence, unless there is a thing whose essence just is its existence. There can be at most one such thing, since pure existence, with nothing added to it, could not be multiplied. Everything else receives its existence. Nothing can cause its own existence, so whatever receives existence receives it from another, and the series of receivers leads back to a first being that is "existence alone" (*esse tantum*), and that is God (*De ente* ch. 4, paraphrased). Argument 6 in [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md) reconstructs the steps and the dispute over whether the first step proves a real distinction or only a distinction in thought.

Two points keep the doctrine from being misread. First, *esse* is not a property among others, like being red. It is the act that makes any form or nature actual, and Aquinas compares it to essence as act to potency. The *Summa* puts it this way: "existence is that which makes every form or nature actual" (ST Ia Q3 a4). An essence without existence is not a ghostly half-thing; it is nothing at all. Second, the doctrine builds a hierarchy of being. Material things are doubly limited: their essence is received in matter and their existence is received in their essence. Angels have no matter, but their existence is still received and limited by their essence. God alone is subsistent existence itself (*ipsum esse subsistens*). The usual Thomist summary is that act is limited only by the potency that receives it.

The distinction also grounds the language of *participation*. A creature has existence without being existence, as an iron bar in a forge is on fire without being fire. Hence "all beings apart from God are not their own being, but are beings by participation" (ST Ia Q44 a1). Creation, on this view, is not only a first event but the continuing gift of existence. A creature depends on God for its being at every moment it exists, as the light in the air depends on the sun.

## God as subsistent being: the Five Ways and simplicity

The *Summa*'s treatise on God runs: whether God exists (Q2), how God is, or rather how God is not (Q3–11), how God is known and named (Q12–13), and then God's knowledge, will and power (Q14–26). The Five Ways of Q2 a3 reach a first unmoved mover, a first efficient cause, a being necessary through itself, a maximum that causes the perfections of all things, and an intelligence that directs natural things to their ends. Each ends with a formula such as the First Way's "and this everyone understands to be God" or the Fifth's "and this being we call God" (ST Ia Q2 a3). The Ways are reconstructed as Arguments 1–5, and the [study guide](../../study/aquinas-summa.md) gives them question by question. Here the point is what they set up.

The conclusion of each Way is thin: a first something. Questions 3–11 then argue that this first cause must have a list of attributes. The first and most important is simplicity. God is not a body, not composed of matter and form, not distinct from his own nature, not composed of essence and existence, not in a genus, and has no accidents. "Therefore, it is clear that God is nowise composite, but is altogether simple" (ST Ia Q3 a7). The fourth denial carries the weight. If God's existence differed from his essence it would be received from a cause, and God is the first cause; it would be related to the essence as act to potency, and God has no potency; and God would be a being by participation, not the first being. "Therefore His essence is His existence" (ST Ia Q3 a4).

The other attributes follow largely from simplicity and pure act. God is perfect (Q4), since to be in act is to be perfect, and he contains the perfections of all things in a higher way. God is good (Q6), infinite (Q7), present in all things as the cause of their existence (Q8), unchangeable (Q9), eternal (Q10) and one (Q11). Argument 7 reconstructs the case for simplicity and the modern objection, pressed by Alvin Plantinga in *Does God Have a Nature?* (1980), that if God is identical with each of his attributes, the attributes must be identical with one another and God becomes a property.

Simplicity is also a rule about knowledge. Because God has no composition, no human concept, which always grasps one feature distinct from others, can capture what God is. Aquinas says at the start of Q3 that we can consider how God is not, rather than how he is, and the denials of Q3 are in fact all negations. This *way of remotion* (negation), which Aquinas takes partly from Pseudo-Dionysius (see [Pseudo-Dionysius and Eckhart](../../eras/02-medieval/pseudo-dionysius-and-eckhart.md)), leads straight to the problem of the divine names.

## Analogy and the divine names

If we know God only through creatures and cannot grasp what God is, can we say anything true about him beyond denials? Aquinas thinks we can say that God is good, wise and living, and mean it positively. Question 13 explains how. Words such as "good" signify a perfection and also carry a creaturely mode of signifying. "As regards what is signified by these names, they belong properly to God, and more properly than they belong to creatures, and are applied primarily to Him. But as regards their mode of signification, they do not properly and strictly apply to God" (ST Ia Q13 a3). Words whose very meaning includes a bodily mode, such as "rock" or "lion", apply to God only as metaphors.

Positive names are not said of God and creatures in the same sense. "Univocal predication is impossible between God and creatures" (ST Ia Q13 a5), because a perfection that exists in creatures divided and limited exists in God undivided and without limit. "Wise" said of a human being picks out one quality distinct from the person's being and power; said of God it picks out nothing distinct from God's essence. Nor are such names purely equivocal, like "bank" for a river's edge and a lender, because then no argument from creatures to God could work. The middle way is analogy: "these names are said of God and creatures in an analogous sense, i.e. according to proportion" (ST Ia Q13 a5). The ground of the analogy is causal. Creatures resemble God as effects resemble a cause that exceeds them, so a perfection found in creatures is in God first, in a higher way (ST Ia Q13 a6).

The result is a qualified agnosticism about God's nature. We know *that* God is wise and that wisdom is truly in God, but not *what* divine wisdom is. Kinds of analogy are a long-running dispute. Cajetan (Thomas de Vio, 1498) classified Aquinas's view as an "analogy of proper proportionality"; Ralph McInerny and others argued that this reading distorts Aquinas, who uses several kinds of analogy and treats analogy as a matter of how words signify. Duns Scotus answered with a different theory: some concepts, being above all, apply to God and creatures in one sense, or the arguments of natural theology would equivocate. Argument 8 sets out Aquinas's case and Scotus's reply; see [Duns Scotus](../../eras/02-medieval/john-duns-scotus.md).

## Creation and the eternity question

Creation, in the strict sense, is giving existence to a thing from nothing, with no prior matter or subject. Only God can do it, since only the being whose essence is existence can give existence as such (ST Ia Q45). Creation is therefore not a change, because a change needs something that persists, and before creation there is nothing to persist. It is a relation of dependence on God in the creature.

That analysis separates two questions that most of Aquinas's contemporaries ran together: whether the world depends on God, and whether the world had a beginning in time. The first, Aquinas holds, can be proved. The second cannot be proved either way. "Hence that the world began to exist is an object of faith, but not of demonstration or science" (ST Ia Q46 a2). An argument from the world fails because a thing's definition abstracts from here and now, so nothing in the nature of a human being or a heaven shows that there was a time when none existed. An argument from God fails because God creates by will, and reason cannot know what God freely wills except where he wills of necessity, which creating is not. Aquinas adds a warning that tells much about his method: defending the faith with bad proofs gives unbelievers occasion to laugh, "thinking that on such grounds we believe things that are of faith" (ST Ia Q46 a2).

The short treatise *On the Eternity of the World* (c. 1271, paraphrased) presses the point against Christian opponents. They argued that an eternal created world is self-contradictory, since what is created must come after nothing. Aquinas replies that "after nothing" can mean an order of nature rather than of time. A created thing has its existence from another and has nothing of itself, so in the order of nature nothing comes before its being. That does not require a time when it did not exist. So far as reason can tell, God could have created a world that always existed. Aquinas believes the world began, because Genesis says so. Argument 9 reconstructs the case. The separation also explains why the Five Ways are about present dependence, not about a first moment. It puts Aquinas between the Latin Averroists, who appeared to hold that Aristotle had proved the world eternal, and theologians such as Bonaventure, who thought its beginning could be demonstrated. See [Maimonides](../../eras/02-medieval/maimonides.md), whose *Guide* had reached a similar verdict.

## Goodness, evil and providence

Being and good are, for Aquinas, the same thing considered in two ways. "Goodness and being are really the same, and differ only in idea" (ST Ia Q5 a1). A thing is good insofar as it is desirable; it is desirable insofar as it is perfect; and it is perfect insofar as it is in act, which means insofar as it exists. "Good" adds to "being" a relation to desire. Later Thomists call terms like *being*, *one*, *true* and *good*, which apply across every category, the *transcendentals*. Aquinas gives a list of them in the *Disputed Questions on Truth* Q1 a1. The order matters. Aquinas holds that "the first thing conceived by the intellect is being" (ST Ia Q5 a2), and the other transcendentals add a relation or a negation to being.

Evil follows by contrast. If being as such is good, evil cannot be a being or a nature. "Therefore it must be that by the name of evil is signified the absence of good" (ST Ia Q48 a1). Not every absence is evil. A stone's lack of sight is a mere negation; a human being's lack of sight is a privation, the absence of a good that ought to be there. In Aquinas's words, "the absence of good, taken in a privative sense, is an evil; as, for instance, the privation of sight is called blindness" (ST Ia Q48 a3). This is Augustine's *privatio boni*, which Aquinas takes over and sharpens with the distinction between negation and privation. See the [Augustine dossier](../augustine-of-hippo/README.md).

Evil exists only in a good subject and is caused only by good things acting deficiently, since "only good can be a cause; because nothing can be a cause except inasmuch as it is a being, and every being, as such, is good" (ST Ia Q49 a1). Aquinas divides evil into natural defects, such as corruption and disease, and evil in voluntary agents. The second kind splits into fault (*culpa*), the disordered act of the will, and penalty (*poena*), the privation the agent suffers. God causes natural corruption incidentally, by willing the order of a universe that includes corruptible things, but God in no way causes fault (ST Ia Q49 a2). Argument 10 reconstructs the doctrine and the objection that pain seems positively bad, not merely a lack.

Providence completes the picture. God governs all things directly in plan and through secondary causes in execution (ST Ia Q22, Q103). Creatures are real causes, not occasions for God's action; Aquinas holds that God's causing a creature's act does not compete with the creature's causing it, since the two causes work at different levels. How God's universal causality fits with human free choice, and with fault, is one of the system's hardest points (see Known internal tensions).

## The soul as form of the body

A soul (*anima*), for Aquinas as for Aristotle, is the first principle of life in a living body, and plants and animals have souls too. The human soul is unusual because it is both the form of a body and the subject of an activity, understanding, that uses no bodily organ. Aquinas argues that the intellect can know the natures of all bodies, and a knower that had any bodily nature of its own would be blocked from knowing others, as a tongue coated with bile tastes everything as bitter. So the principle of understanding is "a principle both incorporeal and subsistent" (ST Ia Q75 a2): it has an activity of its own and so exists in its own right. It cannot decay with the body, and it survives death (ST Ia Q75 a6).

But a human being is not a soul using a body, as the Platonists held. Sensing is something a human being does, and sensing is a bodily act, so "man is not a soul only, but something composed of soul and body" (ST Ia Q75 a4). And the intellectual soul is the substantial form of the body: "Therefore this principle by which we primarily understand, whether it be called the intellect or the intellectual soul, is the form of the body" (ST Ia Q76 a1). The supporting argument is from the unity of the person: "it is one and the same man who is conscious both that he understands, and that he senses" (ST Ia Q76 a1), and that would be impossible if the knower and the sensor were two things.

Aquinas then takes the step that made his anthropology controversial. A human being has only one substantial form, the intellectual soul, which does the work of vegetative, sensitive and intellectual life at once. If the soul is the body's substantial form, "it is impossible for another substantial form besides the intellectual soul to be found in man" (ST Ia Q76 a4). Most of his contemporaries were pluralists: they posited a "form of bodiliness" (*forma corporeitatis*) or several souls, partly because it explained why a corpse looks like the same body as the living one. Aquinas accepts the cost: the corpse is a new substance, and only prime matter persists. The unity thesis was among the doctrines targeted after his death, in Archbishop Kilwardby's Oxford prohibitions of March 1277, and the Franciscan William de la Mare attacked it in his *Correctorium fratris Thomae* (SEP, "Thomas Aquinas," §9). Argument 11 reconstructs the case.

The separated soul after death is in an odd position. It exists and understands, but it is not a complete human being, and Aquinas uses this to argue that resurrection of the body is needed for the person to live again. Whether the person survives at all between death and resurrection divides recent readers. "Corruptionists" (Patrick Toner, Christina Van Dyke) read him as saying the person ceases to exist; "survivalists" (Eleonore Stump, Jeffrey Brower) read the person as surviving, reduced, in the soul (SEP, "Thomas Aquinas," §5). The library entry's line that the separated soul is not the whole person draws on Aquinas's commentary on 1 Corinthians 15, a text the corruptionists often cite.

## Knowing: senses, phantasms and the agent intellect

Because the soul is the form of a body, human knowing begins with the senses. There are no innate ideas in the possible intellect. Aquinas follows Aristotle's comparison of it to a tablet on which nothing is yet written (ST Ia Q79 a2). The external senses receive the sensible forms of things. The internal senses, of which imagination is the main one, combine and store them as phantasms (*phantasmata*), sense images of particular things. The proper object of the human intellect in this life is "the quiddity of a material thing", the nature of a sensible thing, which it draws out of phantasms (ST Ia Q85 a8; compare Q84 a7).

That drawing-out is abstraction. A phantasm is particular and material; a concept is universal and immaterial. So a power is needed that makes what is potentially intelligible in sense images actually intelligible. That is the agent intellect (*intellectus agens*), and Aquinas insists against the Arabic Aristotelians that each human being has one of their own: "The active intellect, of which the Philosopher speaks, is something in the soul" (ST Ia Q79 a4). The possible (or receptive) intellect then receives the intelligible species and is actualized by it. Aquinas states the conclusion directly: "our intellect understands material things by abstracting from the phantasms" (ST Ia Q85 a1). Even after concepts are formed, the intellect keeps needing images: "it is impossible for our intellect to understand anything actually, except by turning to the phantasms" (ST Ia Q84 a7). Anyone who has needed an example to grasp a definition has experienced what Aquinas calls this turning to the phantasms (*conversio ad phantasmata*). Argument 13 reconstructs the theory.

Two consequences tie the epistemology into the rest of the system. First, it explains the shape of natural theology. If all human concepts come from sensible things, God can be known only as the cause of sensible effects, by arguments that start from what we observe, never from a definition of God. That is why Aquinas rejects Anselm's argument as a proof for us (ST Ia Q2 a1; see [Anselm](../../eras/02-medieval/anselm-of-canterbury.md)) and why every Way starts from the world. Second, it reinterprets Augustine. Aquinas keeps the language of divine illumination but identifies the light with the agent intellect, a created share in God's light, rather than with a direct sight of truths in God (ST Ia Q84 a5).

Against Averroes, Aquinas also argues that the possible intellect is not one separate intellect shared by all human beings. If it were, the thinking would not be this human being's thinking, and the plain fact that this particular human being understands could not be explained (ST Ia Q76 a1–2). *On the Unity of the Intellect* (1270, paraphrased) argues the case at length and mainly from Aristotle's own text, to show that the Averroist reading is bad exegesis as well as bad theology. Argument 12 reconstructs it; see [Averroes](../../eras/02-medieval/averroes.md).

## Will, action and happiness

The will (*voluntas*) is the appetite that follows the intellect, as sense appetite follows the senses. It is drawn to what the intellect presents as good. It wills one thing necessarily: happiness, the good as such, to which it clings as the intellect clings to first principles. It does not will particular goods necessarily, because none of them is so tied to happiness that one cannot be happy without it (ST Ia Q82 a1–2). Free choice (*liberum arbitrium*) comes from this gap. A sheep flees the wolf by a natural judgment fixed to one outcome. A human being judges by comparing reasons, and in contingent matters reason can go either way. "And forasmuch as man is rational is it necessary that man have a free-will" (ST Ia Q83 a1).

Intellect and will move each other in different ways. The intellect moves the will by presenting its object, the good understood. The will moves the intellect, and every other power, to act or not to act (ST Ia Q82 a4). Asked which power is higher, Aquinas answers with a distinction: "the intellect in itself and absolutely is higher and nobler than the will" (ST Ia Q82 a3), though love of God is better than knowledge of God in this life, because the will goes out to its object as it is, while the intellect takes its object in according to the intellect's own limited mode. This ranking of intellect over will is why Aquinas is called an intellectualist. The label hides a debate over how much room he leaves the will (see Known internal tensions).

The Ia-IIae starts from action. Human acts in the strict sense are those done from deliberate will, as opposed to mere acts of a human being, such as scratching one's head absent-mindedly. As Aquinas puts it, "man differs from irrational animals in this, that he is master of his actions" (ST Ia-IIae Q1 a1). Every agent acts for an end. A rational agent acts for an end it knows, and all its ends are ordered to one last end, which everyone wants under the description "happiness" (*beatitudo*) though people disagree about what it is (Ia-IIae Q1 a4–7).

Question 2 then removes candidates one by one: wealth, honour, fame, power, bodily goods, pleasure, even goods of the soul. The verdict is general: "It is impossible for any created good to constitute man's happiness" (ST Ia-IIae Q2 a8). The will's object is the universal good, and only the universal good, God, can rest it. Happiness itself, the possession of that good, is an activity of the intellect. "Final and perfect happiness can consist in nothing else than the vision of the Divine Essence" (ST Ia-IIae Q3 a8). The argument is from the natural desire to know causes. Anyone who knows an effect wants to know what its cause is, so a mind that knows of God only "that He is" still has something left to desire. Aquinas also allows an imperfect happiness possible in this life, in contemplation and virtuous action, which Aristotle described (Ia-IIae Q3 a2 ad 4; Q5 a3). Argument 14 reconstructs the case.

Questions 6–21 analyse the structure of a human act: what makes it voluntary, and the steps of intention, deliberation, choice, consent, command and use, divided between intellect and will. An act's moral goodness depends on its object, its end and its circumstances (Ia-IIae Q18, paraphrased). One application has had a long later history. In defending a person who kills an attacker, Aquinas notes that "nothing hinders one act from having two effects, only one of which is intended, while the other is beside the intention" (ST IIa-IIae Q64 a7). Moral acts take their species from what is intended, so defending one's life is lawful if the force used is proportionate. Later moralists built the "principle of double effect" from this article. Whether Aquinas himself held that principle, or only made a narrower point about self-defence, is disputed; Argument 16 covers it.

## Virtue

Between a power and its acts stands a habit (*habitus*), a stable disposition that makes a power act well or badly with ease. Virtue is a good habit: "human virtue which is an operative habit, is a good habit, productive of good works" (ST Ia-IIae Q55 a3). Aquinas also defends Peter Lombard's definition, built from Augustine: virtue is a good quality of the mind by which one lives rightly, which no one uses badly, and which God works in us without us (Ia-IIae Q55 a4). He accepts the last clause only for infused virtue, and he notes that "habit" would be a better genus than "quality".

The virtues fall into three groups. Intellectual virtues perfect the intellect: understanding of first principles, scientific knowledge, wisdom, art and prudence. Moral virtues perfect the appetites. Four of these are "cardinal" (from Latin *cardo*, a hinge): prudence, justice, fortitude and temperance (Ia-IIae Q61). Prudence, right reason about what to do, is an intellectual virtue by its subject and a moral one by its matter, and no moral virtue exists without it; this is why Aquinas holds that the moral virtues are connected (Ia-IIae Q58 a4, Q65 a1, paraphrased). The third group are the theological virtues: faith, hope and charity. They exist because human beings have two ends. One is a happiness proportionate to human nature. The other is "a happiness surpassing man's nature, and which man can obtain by the power of God alone, by a kind of participation of the Godhead" (ST Ia-IIae Q62 a1). Natural powers, even well trained, are not proportioned to that end, so God gives additional principles. These are the theological virtues, which have God as their object and are infused, not acquired by practice.

Infusion goes further. Aquinas holds that God also infuses moral virtues with charity, distinct in kind from the acquired moral virtues that Aristotle describes (Ia-IIae Q63 a3–4). Charity is "a kind of friendship of man for God" (ST IIa-IIae Q23 a5), and it is called the form of all the virtues because it directs their acts to the last end (IIa-IIae Q23 a8, paraphrased). Whether acquired virtue without charity is true virtue, and how the two kinds of moral virtue live in one person, are disputed questions among recent Thomists.

## Law

Law joins the ethics of ends and virtues to the metaphysics of providence. The [study guide](../../study/aquinas-summa.md) works through the treatise on law (Ia-IIae Q90–97) question by question, so this section gives only its structure.

Law is "an ordinance of reason for the common good, made by him who has care of the community, and promulgated" (ST Ia-IIae Q90 a4). Each element excludes something. Law belongs to reason, not to bare will. It aims at the common good, not the ruler's private good. It needs a proper author and must be made known. God's governing reason for the whole universe is the eternal law. Non-rational things share in it by being moved, and rational creatures share in it in a higher way, by understanding it and directing themselves: "this participation of the eternal law in the rational creature is called the natural law" (ST Ia-IIae Q91 a2). Natural law is not a code written somewhere in nature. It is human practical reason itself, seen as a share in God's reason.

Practical reason has a first principle, as speculative reason has non-contradiction. "Hence this is the first precept of law, that 'good is to be done and pursued, and evil is to be avoided'" (ST Ia-IIae Q94 a2). Its content comes from the human inclinations, which reason recognizes as goods: "all those things to which man has a natural inclination, are naturally apprehended by reason as being good" (ST Ia-IIae Q94 a2). They run in order: to preserve one's life, shared with all substances; to mate and raise offspring, shared with animals; to know the truth about God and to live in society, proper to reason. Argument 15 reconstructs this article and the main dispute over it. Germain Grisez (1965) and John Finnis (*Natural Law and Natural Rights*, 1980) read the first principles as self-evident goods grasped by practical reason without inference from human nature; Ralph McInerny, Anthony Lisska and others read them as grounded in a metaphysics of human nature. The SEP entry describes the question as contentious (SEP, "Thomas Aquinas," §8.2).

Human law derives from natural law in two ways: by conclusion, as the rule against killing follows from the principle that one should harm no one, or by determination, as a particular penalty specifies a general principle that leaves the detail open (Ia-IIae Q95 a2). A human law that contradicts natural law is a corruption of law. Such a law does not bind in conscience, though it may be obeyed to avoid scandal or disorder, and a law commanding what divine law forbids must never be obeyed (Ia-IIae Q96 a4). Divine law, revealed in the Old and New Testaments, is needed because human beings are directed to a supernatural end, because human judgment is uncertain, because human law cannot reach interior acts, and because human law cannot punish every evil (Ia-IIae Q91 a4). The same structure governs the treatment of war in IIa-IIae Q40: a war is just only with the authority of the ruler, a just cause and a right intention.

## Grace perfects nature

The last piece of the architecture is grace. In Aquinas's technical sense, grace is a created gift by which God heals human nature and lifts it to an end above nature. It is not a replacement for nature. Aquinas distinguishes two conditions of human nature. Unfallen ("integral") nature could do the good proportionate to it, such as acquired virtue, but needed grace for supernatural good. Fallen nature cannot even do all the natural good it is capable of without help: "in the state of corrupt nature man needs, even for this, the help of grace to heal his nature" (ST Ia-IIae Q109 a3), where "this" is loving God above all things. Grace therefore has two jobs: healing (*gratia sanans*) and elevating (*gratia elevans*), later scholastic labels for a distinction Aquinas draws in Q109.

Faith fits the same pattern. Belief is not a feeling and not a demonstration. It is "an act of the intellect assenting to the Divine truth at the command of the will moved by the grace of God" (ST IIa-IIae Q2 a9). The intellect assents without seeing, because the will, moved by grace, commands assent to what God reveals. Reason prepares the way through the preambles and defends the articles against objections. It does not prove them, and if it could, faith would not be faith (IIa-IIae Q2 a10, paraphrased).

The phrase "grace does not destroy nature but perfects it" (ST Ia Q1 a8 ad 2) therefore runs through the whole system. Philosophy keeps its own principles inside theology. Acquired virtue keeps its place under infused virtue, and natural law keeps its place under divine law. The natural end keeps its place under the supernatural one. In each pair the higher term presupposes the lower and completes it without cancelling it.

## How the pieces interlock

Act and potency explain change, and change in a *per se* ordered series leads to a first cause that is pure act. Essence and existence explain why every creature receives its being and why the first cause must be subsistent existence itself, which makes it simple. Simplicity puts God's essence beyond human concepts, so the divine names are analogical, true of God in a higher way that we cannot comprehend. Creation is the continuing gift of existence, which reason can prove, while a temporal beginning is known only by faith. Being and good are the same, so evil is a privation in a good subject. The human soul is the single form of a body and also subsistent; so human knowledge starts in the senses and rises by abstraction, which is why natural theology argues from effects to cause. The will follows the intellect toward the universal good, and so nothing created can make a person happy; happiness is the vision of God. Law is reason's share in God's governance, and virtue is the habit of acting well under it. Grace heals and lifts nature toward an end that nature desires but cannot reach. A good self-test is to trace any doctrine back to act and potency.

## Key terms

In the format of the repo [glossary](../../glossary.md), restricted to Aquinas's usage, with later or textbook senses flagged.

- **Act and potency (*actus*, *potentia*):** actuality and capacity. Change is potency brought to act by something already in act (ST Ia Q2 a3). The pair applies to matter and form, power and operation, essence and existence.
- **Pure act (*actus purus*):** act with no potency at all, which only God is. It does not mean "pure activity" in the sense of busy motion; God is unchangeable.
- **Essentially ordered series (*per se* ordered):** a series in which each member causes only while it is caused here and now, like hand, stick and stone. "Essentially ordered" is the later label for Aquinas's *per se*. Not a chain stretching back in time.
- **Matter and form (*materia*, *forma*):** what a material thing is made of and what makes it the kind of thing it is. A substantial form makes a substance what it is; accidental forms (colour, size, place) qualify a substance that already exists. "Form" is not shape.
- **Prime matter (*materia prima*):** matter with no form of its own, pure potency, never existing alone.
- **Designated matter (*materia signata*):** matter under determinate dimensions, the principle that individuates members of one species (*De ente* ch. 2, paraphrased).
- **Essence and existence (*essentia*, *esse*):** what a thing is, as expressed by its definition, and the act that makes that essence actual (ST Ia Q3 a4). In Aquinas *esse* is not a property or predicate; the English "existence" can mislead.
- **Real distinction:** the later name for Aquinas's claim that essence and *esse* differ in every creature, and not only in thought. The phrase is mostly the commentators'.
- **Subsistent being itself (*ipsum esse subsistens*):** God as the one being whose essence is its existence.
- **Participation (*participatio*):** having a perfection, above all existence, received from a source that has it essentially (ST Ia Q44 a1).
- **Divine simplicity:** God's lack of any composition: of parts, matter and form, essence and existence, genus and difference, substance and accident (ST Ia Q3 a7).
- **Analogy (*analogia*):** a predication neither univocal nor equivocal, by proportion to one source (ST Ia Q13 a5). "Analogy of proper proportionality" is Cajetan's later classification, not Aquinas's term.
- **Sacred doctrine (*sacra doctrina*):** the teaching based on revelation, a science with principles known to God and the blessed (ST Ia Q1 a2). Wider than the later academic subject "theology".
- **Preambles of faith (*praeambula fidei*):** truths faith presupposes that reason can prove, such as God's existence. Distinct from the articles of faith.
- **Creation (*creatio*):** giving existence from nothing, which is not a change. Proving creation does not require proving a first moment.
- **Transcendentals:** being, one, true and good, which apply across all categories. "Transcendental" here has nothing to do with Kant's later sense.
- **Privation (*privatio*):** the absence of a good that ought to be present, as blindness in an animal, as opposed to mere negation (ST Ia Q48 a3).
- **Phantasm (*phantasma*):** a sense image held in the internal senses, from which the intellect abstracts and to which it turns (ST Ia Q84 a7).
- **Agent and possible intellect (*intellectus agens*, *intellectus possibilis*):** the active power that abstracts the intelligible from phantasms, and the receptive power that is actualized by it. Both belong to each human soul (ST Ia Q79 a4).
- **Free choice (*liberum arbitrium*):** the power of choosing, grounded in reason's judgment not being fixed to one option (ST Ia Q83 a1). Not identical with modern "libertarian free will".
- **Happiness (*beatitudo*):** the last end, perfectly the vision of God's essence, imperfectly a life of contemplation and virtue (ST Ia-IIae Q3 a8). Not a feeling of well-being.
- **Habit (*habitus*):** a stable disposition of a power. Virtue is a good operative habit (ST Ia-IIae Q55 a3). Not "habit" in the sense of a mindless routine.
- **Natural law (*lex naturalis*):** the rational creature's participation in the eternal law (ST Ia-IIae Q91 a2). Not a set of biological regularities.
- **Synderesis (*synderesis*):** the natural habit by which practical reason holds the first principles of natural law (ST Ia Q79 a12). Distinct from conscience, which is an act applying knowledge to a case (Ia Q79 a13).
- **Grace (*gratia*):** God's created gift that heals nature and lifts it toward a supernatural end (ST Ia-IIae Q109). "Grace perfects nature" is the summary of its relation to nature.

## Known internal tensions

The system strains at five joints. [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md) gives the history of each.

**(1) Intellect, will and freedom.** The will follows the intellect's judgment of the good, and Aquinas accepts that a contingent cause must be determined to its effect by something outside it (ST Ia Q19 a3 ad 5). He also says God moves voluntary causes without taking away their voluntariness (ST Ia Q83 a1 ad 3). Does this make him a compatibilist? Some readers say yes (Kenny, *Aquinas on Mind*, 1993; Thomas Loughran; Robert Pasnau). Others read a libertarian Aquinas for whom the will is a self-mover, either as an ultimate source (Eleonore Stump, *Aquinas*, 2003, ch. 9) or with the ability to choose otherwise as well (SEP, "Thomas Aquinas," §7). Within a generation of his death, voluntarists such as Henry of Ghent, Peter John Olivi and Duns Scotus gave the will more independence than Aquinas did.

**(2) Simplicity and the freedom of creation.** If God's will is identical with God's necessary essence, it is hard to see how God could have willed not to create. Aquinas answers that God wills his own goodness necessarily and creatures only freely, since his goodness lacks nothing without them (ST Ia Q19 a3). Critics ask how one simple, necessary act can have contingent objects without itself being contingent. Plantinga's *Does God Have a Nature?* (1980) presses the general version of the worry. Norman Kretzmann (*The Metaphysics of Theism*, 1997) argued that Aquinas's own principle that goodness spreads itself pushes toward a necessity that God create something.

**(3) The natural desire to see God.** Aquinas argues that the natural desire to know causes rests only in the vision of God (ST Ia-IIae Q3 a8). He also teaches that the vision exceeds every natural power (Ia-IIae Q62 a1). If nature desires an end it cannot reach, either nature is in vain or the end is in some sense owed to it, and Aquinas denies both. Cajetan and later Thomists built a theory of "pure nature" with a proportionate natural end. Henri de Lubac (*Surnaturel*, 1946) argued that this distorted Aquinas, who knew only one actual end for human beings. Lawrence Feingold (*The Natural Desire to See God according to St. Thomas Aquinas and His Interpreters*, first published 2001) defended a version of the older reading. The debate shaped twentieth-century Catholic theology.

**(4) The soul that is both form and subsistent.** A form exists as the form of something, yet the human soul exists and thinks after its body has died. Aquinas holds both claims (ST Ia Q75 a2, Q76 a1). Critics from the Franciscan pluralists to modern dualists and materialists have said he must give one of them up. Within Thomism the tension reappears as the corruptionist–survivalist debate over whether the person survives death (see the soul section above).

**(5) Universal causality and sin.** God moves every will and causes every act insofar as it is being, yet God in no way causes fault (ST Ia Q49 a2). Aquinas locates the defect in the creature, not in God's motion. In the sixteenth century the question split Dominican and Jesuit Thomists in the *de auxiliis* controversy (1598–1607). Domingo Báñez defended God's physical premotion of the will. Luis de Molina defended God's "middle knowledge" of what free creatures would do. The papal commission that heard the case declined to condemn either side. Each claimed to be reading Aquinas.
