# Works and Texts: The Corpus in Reading Order

*Focus study: Thomas Aquinas · [Dossier home](README.md) · Library entry: [Thomas Aquinas](../../eras/02-medieval/thomas-aquinas.md) · Study guide: [*Summa Theologiae* selections](../../study/aquinas-summa.md) · Arguments in full: [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md)*

Aquinas wrote for about twenty years, from his first Paris lectures in 1252 to December 1273, and left more than eight million words. The SEP points out that this is eight times what survives of Aristotle. Unusually for a medieval author, essentially all of it survives, and nearly all of it has been edited and translated. The library entry adds that much of it was dictated to secretaries. The SEP sorts the output into five kinds: the two *summae*; disputed questions; short treatises (*opuscula*) such as *On Being and Essence*; commentaries on Aristotle and other philosophical texts; and commentaries on Scripture. The IEP uses a finer list of nine kinds, which adds devotional works, sermons, polemics and letters giving an expert opinion.

Two facts shape how the corpus is read. Most of it is school writing. Aquinas wrote as a master of theology, in the forms of the university: the lecture on a set text, the disputed question, the teaching *summa*. And he returned to the same problems again and again, in the *Sentences* commentary, the *Summa contra Gentiles*, a disputed question and the *Summa Theologiae*. The SEP's advice is to compare the parallel treatments before taking any one of them as his view. The *Summa Theologiae* usually gives his most considered position, but it is deliberately brief, and the disputed questions often argue the same point at more length. Because he wrote all of it in two decades, the SEP adds, the parallel treatments tend to support each other more often than they conflict. Whether his views changed on particular topics is still debated.

## The forms Aquinas wrote in

**The article.** The *Summa Theologiae* and the disputed questions are built from articles, and every article has the same four parts: objections, a *sed contra* ("on the contrary"), the body (*respondeo*, "I answer that"), and a reply to each objection. The [study guide](../../study/aquinas-summa.md) explains the form; read its edition note before Week 1. The SEP notes that the form comes from classroom procedure, but that Aquinas's carefully composed works are not transcripts of real classroom debates. They are written texts laid out in the shape of a debate. The SEP's author reads the body of the article first, and that is a good habit for a first reading.

**The disputed question.** Presiding over disputations was part of a master's job, and the written *quaestiones disputatae* grew out of it. Aquinas's include *On Truth* (*De veritate*), *On the Power of God* (*De potentia*), *On the Soul* (*De anima*) and *On Evil* (*De malo*). Each is a set of questions divided into articles, and they are often more detailed than the *Summa*. There are also twelve *Quodlibetal Questions*, which Corpus Thomisticum prints in two series.

**The commentary.** On Aristotle, Aquinas wrote the kind of commentary called *sententia* or *expositio*, which works through the text phrase by phrase in paraphrase. The SEP warns that a sentence in a commentary may be Aquinas restating Aristotle rather than stating his own view. Scholars dispute whether the paraphrases speak for Aquinas at all. The surest guides to his own thought are the places where he breaks off the paraphrase to give a short lecture of his own. The commentaries on Scripture (Job, the Gospels in the *Catena aurea*, Paul's letters, and others) are not set reading here, though the SEP notes that they also contain philosophy.

## The first Paris years (1252–1259)

### Commentary on the *Sentences* (1252–1256)

Peter Lombard's *Sentences* was the set text of theology at Paris, and university rules required a bachelor of theology to lecture and write on it. Aquinas's *Scriptum super libros Sententiarum* came out of those lectures at Paris, 1252–1256. The IEP compares it to a doctoral dissertation in theology. It is the earliest of the three long treatments the SEP says to compare, and it is the place to look for his first view of a topic. It is not set reading here.

### *On Being and Essence* (c. 1252–1256)

*De ente et essentia* is a short treatise. Its full title, *ad fratres et socios suos*, dedicates it to his brothers and companions. The library entry dates it c. 1252–56; the IEP narrows it to 1252–53. The prologue takes from Avicenna the claim that being and essence are the first things the intellect conceives. It then sets the task: to say what the words "essence" and "being" mean, how essence is found in different things, and how it stands to the logical notions of genus, species and difference. The chapters run from composite material substances to the separated substances (the soul, the intelligences, the first cause) and then to accidents.

Chapter 4 is the one this dossier reads closely, because it argues for the real distinction of essence and existence (Argument 6). It holds that every essence can be understood without understanding anything about its existence: "possum enim intelligere quid est homo vel Phoenix et tamen ignorare an esse habeat in rerum natura" (*De ente* ch. 4; this dossier's gloss: "for I can understand what a human being or a phoenix is and still not know whether it has existence in the nature of things"). The argument goes on to the possibility of a thing whose essence just is its existence, and argues that there can be only one such thing, the first cause. **Difficulty:** high for its length. The vocabulary (*quiditas*, *esse*, *intentiones logicae*) is unfamiliar and the argument is compressed. **Place:** the young Aquinas's metaphysics, already in the shape the *Summa* will use. The companion treatise *On the Principles of Nature* (1248–1252 or 1252–1256, IEP) is a primer on matter, form and the causes. It makes a useful warm-up but is not set.

### *On Truth* and the Boethius commentary (1256–1259)

As master from 1256 Aquinas held the disputations collected as *On Truth* (1256–1259). The first question, which gives the collection its name, asks what truth is; the rest range much wider. In the same years, 1257–1258 or 1259 by the IEP's dating, he wrote a commentary on Boethius's *On the Trinity* that he never finished. Its questions 5 and 6, translated by Armand Maurer as *The Division and Methods of the Sciences*, are the standard text for how Aquinas divided natural philosophy, mathematics and metaphysics. Neither work is set reading; [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md) draws on both.

## Italy and the *Summa contra Gentiles* (1259–1268)

The Dominicans rotated their masters, and in 1259 Aquinas was sent back to Italy. The IEP places him at Naples (1259–1261), Orvieto (1261–1265) and Rome (1265–1268); the SEP names only Orvieto and Rome.

### *Summa contra Gentiles* (1259–1265)

The *Summa contra Gentiles* (SCG), in four books, was begun at Paris in 1259 and finished in Italy by 1265. In I.2 Aquinas says his aim is to set out the truth the Catholic faith professes and to weed out the errors opposed to it. Since some opponents, "like the Mohammedans and pagans", accept no Scripture, "it is necessary to have recourse to natural reason, to which all are compelled to assent. And yet this is deficient in the things of God" (SCG I.2). Who the book was written for is disputed. It may have been meant to equip missionaries, as an old account has it, or it may be a theologian's book, written to go as far as reason can. [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md) gives the positions.

The plan follows from I.3. Some truths about God "wholly surpass the capability of human reason", such as that God is three and one. Others, such as that God exists and is one, "even the philosophers proved demonstratively of God" (SCG I.3). SCG I.9 lays out the order. The work first argues by reason about God in himself (Book I), the procession of creatures from God (Book II), and the relation of creatures to God as their end (Book III). Then it turns to the truth that surpasses reason, which it defends by answering objections and by probable arguments and authority (Book IV). SCG I.13 gives the argument from motion at much greater length than the *Summa*, working through Aristotle's proofs in the *Physics*. Week 2 reads it beside ST Ia Q2 a3, and Week 8 reads I.3–8 on faith and reason. **Difficulty:** moderate. The chapters are continuous prose, without the article form, but I.13 is long and technical. **Place:** the fullest statement of what reason can show about God.

The text has a history worth knowing. The translator's preface to the 1924 English Dominican edition explains why the SCG was translated after the ST. The 1857 Latin text of P. A. Uccelli was "extremely defective, owing to the editor's inability to read St. Thomas's handwriting correctly", and the Leonine editors did not publish the first two books until 1918 (SCG, tr. English Dominican Fathers, 1924, preface). The preface reports that one Leonine editor took over two years to learn to read Aquinas's autograph.

### Other Italian works

The IEP dates these works of the Italian decade: the disputed questions *On the Power of God* (1265–1266) and *On the Soul* (1266–1267); the *Compendium of Theology* (1265–1267, unfinished), written for his companion Reginald; a commentary on Pseudo-Dionysius's *Divine Names* (1265–1268); the *Catena aurea*, a continuous gloss on the Gospels compiled from the Church Fathers (1262–1268); the literal commentary on Job (1263–1265); the liturgy for the feast of Corpus Christi, commissioned by Pope Urban (1264); and *On Kingship* (*De regno*), addressed to the king of Cyprus (1266–1267). None of these is set reading. *On the Power of God* and *On Evil* (below) are the first places to go when the *Summa* is too brief.

## The *Summa Theologiae* (1265–1273, unfinished)

Aquinas began the *Summa Theologiae* (ST) in Italy, perhaps in Rome, around 1265. Its prologue states the aim: "Because the doctor of Catholic truth ought not only to teach the proficient, but also to instruct beginners ... we purpose in this book to treat of whatever belongs to the Christian religion, in such a way as may tend to the instruction of beginners" (ST, Prologue). He blames earlier books for "the multiplication of useless questions, articles, and arguments" and for teaching things in the order of a commented book instead of "the order of the subject matter" (ST, Prologue). The SEP remarks that nobody now thinks the ST is a book for beginners. Its scope and brevity still make it the natural place to start.

The work has three parts, the second divided in two. Question numbers below follow the New Advent index; dates are the IEP's.

- **Prima Pars (Ia), 1265–1268:** sacred doctrine (Q1); God's existence (Q2) and nature (Q3–26); the Trinity (Q27–43); creation (Q44–49), with the beginning of the world's duration (Q46) and evil and its cause (Q48–49); the angels (Q50–64); the six days (Q65–74); the human being (Q75–102), with the soul and its union with the body (Q75–76) and the intellect's knowledge (Q84–89); and God's government of creatures (Q103–119).
- **Prima Secundae (Ia-IIae), 1271:** the last end and happiness (Q1–5); human acts (Q6–21); the passions (Q22–48); habits and virtues, with the gifts (Q49–70); vice and sin (Q71–89); law (Q90–108); and grace (Q109–114).
- **Secunda Secundae (IIa-IIae), 1271–1272:** the virtues one by one: faith (Q1–16), hope (Q17–22), charity (Q23–46, with war at Q40), prudence (from Q47), justice (from Q57, with murder and self-defence at Q64), fortitude (from Q123) and temperance (from Q141), then prophecy and the states of life (from Q171).
- **Tertia Pars (IIIa), 1271–1273:** Christ and the sacraments, complete through Q90, in the treatise on penance.

Most printings, New Advent's among them, complete the work with a **Supplement**. The SEP says Aquinas's followers assembled it from his earlier writings to bring the work to its planned end. It is not the ST as Aquinas wrote it.

Why he stopped is reported by the early biographies and the testimony at the 1319 canonization inquiry, not by Aquinas himself. Around December 1273 he stopped writing. The saying that everything he had written now seemed to him like straw comes from that tradition. The SEP repeats it, and also warns that the colourful stories from the canonization testimony cannot be authenticated. [01-life-and-times.md](01-life-and-times.md) sets out the sources.

**Difficulty:** varies. Q2 a3 is short and dense, the law questions are easier, and Q75–89 are hard. **Place:** the mature synthesis, and the text read in every week of the dossier. The order of the whole is deliberate. Reading Q2 without Q1, or Q94 without Q90–93, means reading Aquinas out of his own order.

## The second Paris regency: polemical treatises (1268–1272)

In 1268 Aquinas returned to Paris for a second term as master, which the SEP calls unusual. The SEP says he used his reputation to steer the debates away from extreme positions that divided the scholars. The radical Aristotelians of the arts faculty, the Latin Averroists of the library entry, held two of these positions: that the world is eternal, and that all human beings share one intellect. Aquinas wrote a short treatise against each. Together they fix the line he drew between Aristotle and Aristotle's Averroist readers. In the same years he wrote the Ia-IIae and IIa-IIae, the disputed questions *On Evil* (1269–1271), and most of the Aristotle commentaries.

### *On the Unity of the Intellect, against the Averroists* (1270)

*De unitate intellectus contra Averroistas* opens by tracing the error to Averroes. It attacks the view that the possible intellect is a single separate substance shared by all human beings. Aquinas fights on his opponents' ground, in five chapters. Chapter 1 reads Aristotle's *De anima*, and chapter 2 the other Peripatetics, Greek and Arab, to show that the view is not Aristotle's. Chapter 3 argues from reason that the intellect is a power of the soul that is the body's form, starting from the fact that this human being understands. Chapter 4 argues against one possible intellect in all, and chapter 5 answers the arguments for it. He says he has argued "non per documenta fidei, sed per ipsorum philosophorum rationes et dicta" (*De unitate* ch. 5; this dossier's gloss: "not by the teachings of the faith but by the reasons and statements of the philosophers themselves"). The treatise ends with a challenge. Whoever disagrees should not speak "in angulis nec coram pueris" but "contra hoc scriptum rescribat, si audet" (*De unitate* ch. 5; this dossier's gloss: not "in corners or before boys", but "let him write back against this treatise, if he dares"). The library entry dates it 1270; some sources give 1269. **Difficulty:** moderate to high, since it presumes Aristotle's *De anima* III. **Place:** Week 5, beside ST Ia Q76 a2 (Argument 12).

### *On the Eternity of the World* (1271)

*De aeternitate mundi*, dated 1271 by the IEP, is the shortest of the three set treatises, a few pages long. Its traditional subtitle is *contra murmurantes*, "against the grumblers". Its opening states the question: "Supposito, secundum fidem Catholicam, quod mundus durationis initium habuit, dubitatio mota est, utrum potuerit semper fuisse" (*De aeternitate mundi*, opening; this dossier's gloss: "Supposing, as the Catholic faith holds, that the world had a beginning of its duration, a doubt has been raised whether it could always have existed"). Aquinas answers that God could have created a world that always existed: being created does not by itself require a beginning in time. ST Ia Q46 a2 gives the same verdict in article form: "By faith alone do we hold, and by no demonstration can it be proved, that the world did not always exist" (ST Ia Q46 a2). The SEP calls this a middle ground. Aristotle's followers thought they could prove the world eternal, and many of Aquinas's contemporaries thought they could prove it had a beginning. It was among the bitterest disputes of the early universities. Aquinas held that neither side has a demonstration. **Difficulty:** low to moderate. **Place:** Week 4 (Argument 9).

## The Aristotle commentaries (1267–1273)

The SEP says these years were dominated by the effort to finish the ST and, at the same time, to comment on all of Aristotle's principal works. The IEP's dates: *De anima* 1267–1268, *Physics* 1268–1270, *On Interpretation* 1270–1271, *Metaphysics* 1270–1273, *Nicomachean Ethics* 1271–1272. Alongside them came commentaries on Boethius's *De hebdomadibus* (1271–1272, with a query) and on the *Book of Causes* (1272–1273). The dossier does not set the commentaries as reading. When a later file cites one, keep the SEP's caution about paraphrase in mind.

## Naples and the end (1272–1274)

When the second Paris term ended in 1272, Aquinas went to Naples and taught for about a year. The SEP says that in these last years he nearly finished both the ST and the commentary series, but not quite. After about a year and a half in Naples he stopped writing. He died at the Cistercian abbey of Fossanova on 7 March 1274, on his way to the Second Council of Lyon. Several works stand unfinished: the IIIa, the *Compendium of Theology* and the Boethius *On the Trinity* commentary, along with some of the Aristotle commentaries. Before citing a late or unfinished work, check which parts are Aquinas's own.

## A note on citation

**Summa Theologiae:** part, question, article, and where needed the part of the article. The dossier writes ST Ia Q2 a3 (Part I, question 2, article 3), ST Ia-IIae Q94 a2 and ST IIa-IIae Q64 a7. Within an article, "ad 2" is the reply to the second objection and "s.c." the *sed contra*. So ST Ia Q1 a8 ad 2 is the reply that says "grace does not destroy nature but perfects it" (ST Ia Q1 a8 ad 2). Other books write the same reference as "I, q. 2, a. 3", "1a.2.3" or "I-II.94.2". The library entry writes "I q.2 a.3" and the study guide "Ia q.2, a.3"; all name the same place.

**Summa contra Gentiles:** book and chapter, as in SCG I.13. Translations divide long chapters into paragraphs differently, so give the opening words when the paragraph matters.

**Disputed questions:** title, question, article, as in *De veritate* q. 1 a. 1.

**Opuscula:** title and chapter. The dossier gives *De ente* a prologue and six chapters, the numbering of the standard translations, so the real distinction is in chapter 4. Corpus Thomisticum labels its sections differently and calls that chapter "cap. 3". Find passages there by their opening words; chapter 4 begins "Nunc restat videre per quem modum sit essentia in substantiis separatis". *De unitate intellectus* has five chapters. *De aeternitate mundi* is a single short text.

**The Latin editions.** The critical edition is the Leonine. It was one fruit of Pope Leo XIII's call in 1879 for a revival of Aquinas's teaching (SEP), began publishing in 1882, and is still not complete (IEP). Corpus Thomisticum (corpusthomisticum.org), edited by Enrique Alarcón for the Fundación Tomás de Aquino, puts the Latin of the corpus online. Each page names the edition it follows. Its *De ente*, for example, is L. Baur's 1933 text as corrected by J. Koch, not the Leonine.

**Why the translation matters.** Aquinas's Latin is plain but technical, and its fixed terms do not map one-to-one onto ordinary English. *Esse* is the act of being; "existence" is the usual rendering, and it loses the verb. *Ratio* can mean reason, nature, meaning or argument. *Intellectus* is the power of understanding or its act. *Scientia* is demonstrated knowledge, not "science". *Passio* covers emotion and also any case of being acted on. A translation that keeps these terms steady matters more than one that reads smoothly. For Argument 12, check each time whether "intellect" means the possible intellect or the agent intellect.

**The dossier's quotations.** The dossier quotes the ST only in the translation by the Fathers of the English Dominican Province, Second and Revised Edition (1920), as printed at New Advent. It quotes the SCG only in the English Dominican translation of the Leonine text (Burns Oates & Washbourne, 1924), checked against the Internet Archive scan. Both are public domain, so every quotation can be checked word for word. *De ente*, *De unitate intellectus* and *De aeternitate mundi* have no public-domain English text in the dossier's sources. The dossier paraphrases them, and where it quotes, it quotes the Latin of Corpus Thomisticum with its own plain gloss, labelled as such. The 1920 translation is literal and old-fashioned ("Holy Writ", "the Philosopher"), and it lays out long articles without help. The learning path's rule is "**Not** the full *Summa* in the old Dominican translation". The dossier quotes that translation because it is public domain and checkable, not because it should be read at the desk.

## The works in the dossier's reading order

The [learning path](../../learning-path.md) sets the following in Stage 2, row 5: ST Ia Q2, Ia Q3–11 in selection, Ia-IIae Q90–97 in selection, and *On Being and Essence* in selection. It names the editions and Feser as companion, and it warns that reading the *Summa* cover to cover means leaving the plan. The dossier keeps the path's core and widens it to eight weeks, about 45 hours all-in, adding the soul, happiness and virtue, and faith and reason. [07-study-plan.md](07-study-plan.md) gives the hours and page targets.

### Week 1. Method and setting: ST Ia Q1

The ST Prologue and Ia Q1 a1–10: why a teaching beyond philosophy is needed (a1), whether it is a science (a2), whether it argues (a8), and the senses of Scripture (a10). Read one article whole, objections and replies included, and then the study guide's edition note on the article form. For background, read [01-life-and-times.md](01-life-and-times.md) on Paris, the Dominicans and the reception of Aristotle. **Difficulty:** moderate; the form is the hard part. **Place:** the method behind everything after it.

### Week 2. The Five Ways: ST Ia Q2 a1–3; SCG I.13

Q2 a1 asks whether God's existence is self-evident, a2 whether it can be demonstrated, and a3 gives the Five Ways. Then read SCG I.13, the long argument from motion with its sources in the *Physics*. The study guide's question-by-question section covers Q2. **Difficulty:** Q2 is a few pages; SCG I.13 is long and technical. **Place:** Arguments 1–5.

### Week 3. Being and essence: *De ente* ch. 4; ST Ia Q3, Q13

*De ente et essentia*: the prologue and chapter 4 at least, the whole if time allows. ST Ia Q3 a1–8 on simplicity; a4 asks whether essence and existence are the same in God. Ia Q13 a1–6 on the divine names, with a5 on analogy. **Difficulty:** the highest of the eight weeks. **Place:** Arguments 6–8, the metaphysics the Five Ways presuppose.

### Week 4. God's attributes, evil, creation: Ia Q4–11 sel., Q19, Q25, Q46, Q48–49; *De aeternitate mundi*

Ia Q4 a1–2 (perfection), Q5 a1 (goodness and being), Q7 a1 (infinity), Q9 a1 (immutability), Q10 a1 (eternity), Q11 a3 (unity). Q19 on God's will and Q25 on God's power, in selection. Q46 a1–2 on the beginning of the world, then *De aeternitate mundi* whole. Q48 a1–3 and Q49 a1–2 on evil and its cause. **Difficulty:** moderate. The attribute questions are short; Q46 and *De aeternitate* need care. **Place:** Arguments 9–10.

### Week 5. Soul and knowing: Ia Q75–76, Q84–86; *De unitate intellectus* sel.

Q75 on the soul's nature and subsistence. Q76 a1–4 on the soul as form of the body, with a2 against one intellect for all and a4 on the single substantial form. Q84 a1–2 and a6–7, Q85 a1–2 and Q86 a1 on knowing through phantasms and abstraction. Then *De unitate intellectus* ch. 3–4 in selection, and the close of ch. 5. **Difficulty:** high; the vocabulary of possible and agent intellect, species and phantasm is new. **Place:** Arguments 11–13.

### Week 6. Happiness, action, virtue: Ia-IIae Q1–5; Q6–17 sel.; Q55–67 sel.

Q1 on the last end. Q2 on the goods in which happiness does not consist (wealth, honours, fame, power, bodily goods, pleasure). Q3 on what happiness is, ending at a8 in the vision of the divine essence. Q4–5 in selection. From the treatise on human acts, Q6 a1 on the voluntary and Q13 on choice. From the virtues, Q55 on their essence, Q58 on moral and intellectual virtue, and Q61–62 on the cardinal and theological virtues. **Difficulty:** moderate. **Place:** Argument 14, and the ethics the law treatise presupposes.

### Week 7. Law: Ia-IIae Q90–97; IIa-IIae Q40, Q64 a7

The treatise on law in the study guide's order. Q90 gives the definition and Q91 the kinds of law. Q93–94 treat eternal and natural law; read Q94 a2 slowly. Q95–97 treat human law. Then IIa-IIae Q40 a1 on just war, and Q64 a7 on killing in self-defence, the text behind the doctrine of double effect. **Difficulty:** moderate; the study guide carries most of the load. **Place:** Arguments 15–16.

### Week 8. Faith and reason; capstone: SCG I.3–8; IIa-IIae Q1–2; the condemnations

SCG I.3–8 on the two kinds of truth about God and why both are fittingly proposed for belief. IIa-IIae Q1 on the object of faith and Q2 on its act, including Q1 a5 (whether what is believed can also be known by demonstration) and Q2 a10 (whether reasons for what is believed lessen the merit of faith). Then the Paris condemnations of 1270 and 1277, as set out in [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md). **Difficulty:** moderate. **Place:** the frame of the whole system, and the capstone essay.

## Editions

The rule follows the learning path. Use Davies and Leftow for the questions on God, McDermott for orientation in the rest of the ST, and Feser alongside. The path is silent on some texts: *De ente* beyond a selection, the SCG, the two polemical treatises, and the ST beyond the opening questions of the Ia. For those the dossier makes its own ruling, marked below.

**The course texts:**

- **ST Ia Q2–26 (Weeks 2–4):** Brian Davies and Brian Leftow (eds.), *Aquinas: Summa Theologiae, Questions on God* (Cambridge Texts in the History of Philosophy, 2006). Cambridge's description says it gives most of the first 26 questions of the *Summa*, including all those on God's existence and nature. Check its table of contents for which articles of Q19 and Q25 it prints.
- **The rest of the ST (Weeks 1, 3–8):** Timothy McDermott (ed.), *Summa Theologiae: A Concise Translation* (Christian Classics, 1989). It abridges the whole *Summa* into continuous modern prose. Because it is an abridgement, use it to see the shape of an argument, and check every premise you reconstruct against a full text. For Q46 a2, Q76, Q84–85 and Q94 a2, use the 1920 text on New Advent as the check. The path's rule is against reading that translation straight through, not against looking up an article.
- **Companion:** Edward Feser, *Aquinas: A Beginner's Guide* (Oneworld, 2009). Its chapters on metaphysics and natural theology go with Weeks 2–4, and its chapter on psychology with Week 5.
- ***On Being and Essence* (dossier ruling):** Armand Maurer's translation (Pontifical Institute of Mediaeval Studies), or Peter King's in Jeffrey Hause and Robert Pasnau (eds.), *The Hackett Aquinas: Basic Works* (Hackett, 2014). King's volume also has *On the Principles of Nature*.
- ***Summa contra Gentiles* (dossier ruling):** Anton C. Pegis, James F. Anderson, Vernon J. Bourke and Charles J. O'Neil (trans.), *Summa contra Gentiles* (1955; University of Notre Dame Press reprint, 1975). Book I is Pegis's. The 1924 English Dominican translation, which the dossier quotes, is serviceable for the short chapters set here and is free.
- ***On the Unity of the Intellect* (dossier ruling):** Ralph McInerny, *Aquinas Against the Averroists: On There Being Only One Intellect* (Purdue University Press, 1993). It prints the Leonine Latin with McInerny's translation, an introduction and interpretive essays.
- ***On the Eternity of the World* (dossier ruling):** Cyril Vollert, Lottie H. Kendzierski and Paul M. Byrne (trans.), *St. Thomas Aquinas, Siger of Brabant, St. Bonaventure: On the Eternity of the World* (Marquette University Press, 1964). It prints the opposing texts with Aquinas's.

**The reference set** is the 1920 ST at New Advent, the 1924 SCG on the Internet Archive, and the Latin at Corpus Thomisticum. For a second, modern reading of an ST passage, Alfred J. Freddoso's translation is free online (see [08-bibliography.md](08-bibliography.md)); it is in copyright, so the dossier does not quote it.

**Avoid** reading the 1920 ST straight through, as the path says. Avoid anthologies that print a Way without the objections and replies of its article, and quotation sites that give ST references without an article number. Joseph Rickaby's *Of God and His Creatures* (1905) is an abridged SCG. Since the full 1924 text is free, there is no reason to use it. And do not treat the Supplement to the IIIa as part of the ST.

## The reading order

1. **Week 1, method and setting:** ST Prologue; Ia Q1.
2. **Week 2, the Five Ways:** ST Ia Q2 a1–3; SCG I.13.
3. **Week 3, being and essence:** *De ente* (esp. ch. 4); ST Ia Q3, Q13.
4. **Week 4, attributes, creation, evil:** ST Ia Q4–11 sel., Q19, Q25, Q46; *De aeternitate mundi*; Q48–49.
5. **Week 5, soul and knowing:** ST Ia Q75–76, Q84–86; *De unitate intellectus* sel.
6. **Week 6, happiness, action, virtue:** ST Ia-IIae Q1–5; Q6–17 sel.; Q55–67 sel.
7. **Week 7, law:** ST Ia-IIae Q90–97; IIa-IIae Q40, Q64 a7.
8. **Week 8, faith and reason; capstone:** SCG I.3–8; ST IIa-IIae Q1–2; the 1270 and 1277 condemnations.
