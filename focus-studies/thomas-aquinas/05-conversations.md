# Aquinas in Conversation — Backward, Sideways, Forward
**English** | [简体中文](../../zh/focus-studies/thomas-aquinas/05-conversations.md)

*Focus study: Thomas Aquinas · [Dossier home](README.md) · [Library entry](../../eras/02-medieval/thomas-aquinas.md) · [Study guide](../../study/aquinas-summa.md)*

Aquinas built his conversations into the form of his books. Every article of the *Summa Theologiae* opens with objections taken from Scripture, Augustine, Aristotle, Boethius, Pseudo-Dionysius, Avicenna, Averroes or Maimonides, and closes by answering each one; the [study guide](../../study/aquinas-summa.md) explains how to read that rhythm. So the backward conversation can be read off the page. The sideways one was fought in the University of Paris, against secular masters who wanted the friars out, arts masters who read Aristotle through Averroes, and Franciscan theologians who distrusted Aristotle and Thomas with him. The forward one began with a censure three years after his death and has not stopped. This file traces those exchanges; the scholarly controversies and the reception by era are in [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md), and the life behind the Paris quarrels is in [01-life-and-times.md](01-life-and-times.md).

The *Summa Theologiae* is quoted from the translation by the Fathers of the English Dominican Province (1920), because it is public domain and checkable, not because it is the text to read; read Davies and Leftow's *Questions on God* and McDermott's *Concise Translation*, as the [learning path](../../learning-path.md) advises. *De ente et essentia* and *De unitate intellectus* are quoted, sparingly, in Latin from the Corpus Thomisticum with this dossier's own gloss. Other writers' works are paraphrased.

## Backward: what he inherited and what he revised

### Aristotle

[Aristotle](../../eras/01-ancient/aristotle.md) is "the Philosopher" throughout, and Aquinas commented line by line on the *Physics*, *Metaphysics*, *De Anima*, *Nicomachean Ethics* and more (week 1; the [Aristotle dossier](../aristotle/05-conversations.md) tells the story from the other end). He took from him act and potency, matter and form, the four causes, the virtues and the happiness of contemplation, and used them for ends Aristotle never had. The First Way's premise is Aristotle's physics in one line: "For motion is nothing else than the reduction of something from potentiality to actuality" (ST Ia Q2 a3; Argument 1). Where Aristotle seemed to contradict the faith, Aquinas read him narrowly rather than reject him. On the eternity of the world he says that Aristotle's arguments in *Physics* VIII are not "simply, but relatively, demonstrative", aimed at earlier philosophers' bad accounts of a beginning (ST Ia Q46 a1; Argument 9; week 4). On the soul he defended Aristotle's form of the body against Plato and against Averroes's reading of *De Anima* III (Arguments 11–12; week 5).

### Augustine and the Platonists

[Augustine](../../eras/02-medieval/augustine-of-hippo.md) is everywhere in the objections and the *sed contra*s, and Aquinas usually keeps his words while changing their frame. He explains his method on illumination: "whenever Augustine, who was imbued with the doctrines of the Platonists, found in their teaching anything consistent with faith, he adopted it" (ST Ia Q84 a5). Augustine's knowledge "in the eternal types" becomes the natural light of the agent intellect, a created share in the divine light, which still needs phantasms to work on (Argument 13; week 5). The [Augustine dossier](../augustine-of-hippo/05-conversations.md) tells this exchange from his side.

Three of the dossier's arguments start from Augustine. The reply to the problem of evil in Q2 a3 is his: "Since God is the highest good, He would not allow any evil to exist in His works, unless His omnipotence and goodness were such as to bring good even out of evil" (ST Ia Q2 a3 ad 1, quoting *Enchiridion* 11), and Q48–49 build Argument 10 from his doctrine of privation. The treatise on law takes his line that "that which is not just seems to be no law at all" (ST Ia-IIae Q95 a2, quoting *On Free Choice* I.5; week 7). And Argument 16 begins from a disagreement. Augustine had written to Publicola, "I do not agree with the opinion that one may kill a man lest one be killed by him" (ST IIa-IIae Q64 a7 obj. 1, quoting Letter 47). Aquinas reads the letter as forbidding the *intention* to kill, which leaves room to defend oneself with killing foreseen but not intended. The SEP's "Doctrine of Double Effect" treats this as a real departure from Augustine. Q40 on war, by contrast, is Augustine almost throughout: legitimate authority and right intention both come with quotations from *Against Faustus* XXII.

[Plato](../../eras/01-ancient/plato.md) Aquinas knew mostly at second hand, through Aristotle, Augustine and the Neoplatonists. He uses him as the representative of separate Forms and of the view that a human being is a soul using a body, both of which he rejects (ST Ia Q76 a1, Q84 a1–4). The *Book of Causes* (*Liber de causis*), long taken for a work of Aristotle's, gave him axioms of participation. In commenting on it (1272–1273) he recognised it, in the IEP's words, as the work of an Arab philosopher who borrowed greatly from Proclus's *Elements of Theology*; see the [late Neoplatonists](../../eras/01-ancient/late-neoplatonists.md) and [Plotinus](../../eras/01-ancient/plotinus.md).

### Boethius and Pseudo-Dionysius

[Boethius](../../eras/02-medieval/boethius.md) gave him definitions. Ia Q10 a1 defends Boethius's definition of eternity, "The simultaneously-whole and perfect possession of interminable life" (*Consolation* V), and uses it to say that even a world without beginning would not be eternal as God is (Q46 a2 ad 5; Argument 9). Ia-IIae Q3 accepts Boethius's happiness, "a state made perfect by the aggregate of all good things", as a definition of happiness in general and then argues that the perfect good is reached in an operation, the vision of God (ST Ia-IIae Q3 a2 ad 2; Argument 14; week 6). Aquinas also commented on Boethius's *De trinitate* and *De hebdomadibus*, the second a source for the language of *esse* and participation.

[Pseudo-Dionysius](../../eras/02-medieval/pseudo-dionysius-and-eckhart.md), who wrote under the name of Paul's Athenian convert, supplies the negative theology that Q13 has to answer. The first objection of article 12 quotes him: "negations about God are true; but affirmations are vague." Aquinas replies that affirmations fail only in their mode of signifying, which is the opening that analogy needs (Argument 8; week 3). On evil the two agree, and the *sed contra* of Q48 a1 is Dionysius's: "Evil is neither a being nor a good" (Argument 10).

### Avicenna, al-Ghazali, Averroes, Maimonides

These are the figures the learning path insists you meet before Aquinas, and the reason shows here.

[Avicenna](../../eras/02-medieval/avicenna.md) is at the root of the Third Way's argument from the possible and the necessary (Argument 3) and of the essence–existence distinction (Argument 6). *De ente et essentia* opens by citing him: *ens autem et essentia sunt quae primo intellectu concipiuntur, ut dicit Avicenna* (prologue; gloss, this dossier's own: "being and essence are what the intellect first conceives, as Avicenna says"). Aquinas also rejects him. Avicenna's separate agent intelligence, from which forms flow into our minds, is refused in ST Ia Q84 a4, and Q79 a4–5 make the active intellect a power in each soul, not one for all (Argument 13), and his necessary emanation of the world is replaced by free creation.

[Al-Ghazali](../../eras/02-medieval/al-ghazali.md) appears as "Algazel", a source Aquinas read for the philosophers' own positions; Q46 a2 ad 8 cites his *Metaphysics* on whether an actual infinity of souls is possible. The kalām argument that the past cannot be infinite, which Ghazali defended, reached Paris, on the SEP's account in "Cosmological Argument", through Bonaventure; it is the argument Aquinas denies is demonstrative (Argument 9).

[Averroes](../../eras/02-medieval/averroes.md), "the Commentator", taught Thomas to read Aristotle and was also his chief opponent on the intellect. Q76 a2 argues against him that "it is altogether impossible and unreasonable to maintain that there exists one intellect for all men" (Argument 12). *De unitate intellectus* is sharper. It argues from the Greek commentators, Themistius and Theophrastus, that Averroes had misreported them, and concludes: *Unde merito supradiximus eum philosophiae Peripateticae perversorem* (ch. 5; gloss: "so we rightly called him, above, a perverter of Peripatetic philosophy").

[Maimonides](../../eras/02-medieval/maimonides.md), "Rabbi Moses", shaped two positions. On creation Aquinas follows him: "By faith alone do we hold, and by no demonstration can it be proved, that the world did not always exist" (ST Ia Q46 a2). Demonstrating it with weak arguments, he adds, would only "give occasion to unbelievers to laugh". On the divine names he refuses him: the view that names like "living" only deny a defect in God was "taught by Rabbi Moses", and Q13 a2 argues that such names signify the divine substance, imperfectly (Argument 8).

### Anselm

[Anselm](../../eras/02-medieval/anselm-of-canterbury.md) is not named in ST Ia Q2 a1, but the second objection is his argument: God is "that thing than which nothing greater can be conceived", so to understand the word is to see that God exists. Aquinas replies that the argument shows only that God exists in the mind: "it does not therefore follow that he understands that what the word signifies exists actually, but only that it exists mentally" (ST Ia Q2 a1 ad 2). God's existence is self-evident in itself, not to us, and so must be shown from effects (week 2). The SEP's entry on Descartes's ontological argument reports that this critique was thought decisive for centuries.

### Cicero, the Stoics and the lawyers

Natural law came to Aquinas from the Stoics through [Cicero](../../eras/01-ancient/cicero-and-lucretius.md), Roman law, Isidore of Seville and the canon lawyers. Q91 a3 quotes "Tully": "justice has its source in nature", then custom, then law (*On Invention* II). Q94 a2 orders natural law by human inclinations, a structure of its own (Argument 15; week 7), while Q94 a4 draws its example of a precept a people lost sight of from Roman history: theft "was not considered wrong among the Germans, as Julius Caesar relates". See also the [Roman Stoics](../../eras/01-ancient/roman-stoics.md).

## Sideways: teachers, colleagues and opponents

### Albert the Great

Albert, the German Dominican who was master of theology at Paris when Thomas arrived, taught him at Paris and then at Cologne from 1248 to 1252, and chose him in 1252 for the Paris doctorate ([01-life-and-times.md](01-life-and-times.md)). The SEP calls Albert the ideal mentor and credits him with the conviction that close study of philosophy and science could only help the faith. The difference between them is one of method: Albert paraphrased and collected the whole Aristotelian and Arabic corpus, natural science included, while Thomas, the SEP notes, only occasionally took up what we would call scientific questions. Albert outlived his student by six years. The library has no entry for him; the "dumb ox" story about the two is a legend from the early lives.

### The secular masters: William of Saint-Amour

Thomas's first public dispute was about his right to teach at all. The secular masters, led by William of Saint-Amour, denied that friars could hold university chairs or live by begging. Thomas answered in *Against Those Who Attack the Worship of God and Religion* (*Contra impugnantes*, 1256), Bonaventure in his disputed questions *On Evangelical Perfection*. Pope Alexander IV condemned William's book on 5 October 1256 and ordered the secular masters to receive the two friars; the SEP's "Saint Bonaventure" reports that they did so only on 12 August 1257 (week 1).

### Bonaventure

Bonaventure (1221–1274) was his Franciscan contemporary, promoted with him, and the theologian whose disagreements with him are best documented. On the eternity of the world Bonaventure held, in his commentary on the *Sentences* (II d.1), that a world both eternal and made from nothing is a plain contradiction, and that the difficulties of an infinite past let reason prove a beginning (SEP, "Saint Bonaventure"). Aquinas denied both, in ST Ia Q46 a2 and in *On the Eternity of the World* (1271), though the treatise names no opponent and its "grumblers" are more often identified with Pecham (below) (Argument 9; week 4). Bonaventure also kept a stronger Augustinian illumination than Aquinas allowed (Argument 13), and on the IEP's account he held that even angels are composed of matter and form, the view the real distinction was meant to replace (Argument 6). In his *Collations on the Hexaëmeron* of Easter 1273, Bonaventure named the three errors he thought most dangerous: the eternity of the world, the necessity of fate, and one intellect for all human beings. Two of the three were Thomas's targets too, which suggests the friars disagreed over how to answer the Averroists more than over whether to. Both men died in 1274. The library has no Bonaventure entry.

### Siger of Brabant and the Latin Averroists

Siger of Brabant and Boethius of Dacia were arts masters at Paris who taught Aristotle as a philosopher, following his arguments to their conclusions, even where those conclusions were the eternity of the world and one intellect for all; see the [Averroes](../../eras/02-medieval/averroes.md) entry. *On the Unity of the Intellect against the Averroists* (1270) names no living opponent, though Siger is usually taken to be its target, and it ends by telling him to stop talking *in angulis*, in corners, and answer in writing: *contra hoc scriptum rescribat, si audet* (ch. 5; gloss, this dossier's own: "let him write a reply to this writing, if he dares"; Argument 12; week 5). Bishop Stephen Tempier condemned thirteen propositions on 10 December 1270. In November 1276 the inquisitor of France, Simon du Val, summoned Siger, then at Liège, on grave suspicion of heresy (SEP, "Condemnation of 1277"). The "double truth" charge, that a proposition could be true in philosophy and false in faith, comes from Tempier's prefatory letter of 1277; the SEP reports that scholars now agree no medieval author held it. The library entry's "seemed to license" in its faith-and-reason section is the right hedge.

### The Franciscan theologians: Pecham, Kilwardby, William de la Mare

The opposition that outlasted him came from theologians who held a plurality of substantial forms in a human being and read Augustine as against Aristotle on the soul. The Franciscan John Pecham, later archbishop of Canterbury, is often named as one of the "grumblers" (*murmurantes*) of *On the Eternity of the World*, though the identification is argued. Archbishop Robert Kilwardby condemned at Oxford in March 1277 a list that included Thomas's unity of substantial form and his prime matter as pure potentiality (SEP, "Thomas Aquinas"; Argument 11). Around the same time the Franciscan William de la Mare published the *Correctorium fratris Thomae*, quoting Thomas and appending "corrections", and Dominican disciples answered with works called *Correctorium corruptorii Thomae*, "correction of the corrupter". For the next quarter century, in the SEP's summary, scholasticism took shape around his Dominican supporters and his Franciscan critics ([06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md) takes up whether the 1277 Paris list targeted him).

## Forward: the heirs and the critics

### Dante

Dante's *Paradiso* places Thomas among the wise in the heaven of the Sun, beside Albert and Siger of Brabant (*Paradiso* X.133–138), the arts master whose position Thomas had written against. The library has no Dante entry.

### Duns Scotus

[John Duns Scotus](../../eras/02-medieval/john-duns-scotus.md) is the first great critic and the library's first "Answered by" link. He rejected the analogy of being: unless some concept of being is common to God and creatures, the arguments of natural theology equivocate, so "being" is univocal (Argument 8; week 3). He made the will, not the intellect, the higher power and placed beatitude chiefly in love (Argument 14; week 6). He replaced individuation by designated matter with a positive principle of individuality, later called *haecceitas*, "thisness". He also lent the Ways support: the SEP's "Cosmological Argument" credits him with the point, now used against the Hume-Edwards objection, that even an infinite series of essentially ordered causes would depend as a whole on some prior cause (Arguments 1–2). Henry of Ghent, Peter John Olivi and Scotus gave the will more independence than Thomas had, and the SEP calls the question of how far Thomas went a lively debate ([03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md), "Known internal tensions").

### William of Ockham

[William of Ockham](../../eras/02-medieval/william-of-ockham.md) cut deeper. His nominalism, as the library entry on Aquinas puts it, dissolved much of the realist metaphysics beneath the system. He also narrowed what reason can prove. As the Ockham entry puts it, the soul's immortality strictly construed and the central doctrines of faith are not demonstrable, and theology is not a science in Aristotle's sense, which shrinks the territory Aquinas gave to natural theology (week 8).

### The Thomist commentators and the Spanish schools

The SEP names John Capreolus (early fifteenth century) as the champion of Thomism as a system, and Cardinal Cajetan (Thomas de Vio, 1468–1534) as its next systematic builder. Cajetan's *On the Analogy of Names* (1498) fixed "analogy of proper proportionality" as the Thomist reading, which Ralph McInerny later argued misread Aquinas (Argument 8), and the commentators from Cajetan onward developed the idea of a "pure nature" with a merely natural end, which Henri de Lubac attacked in *Surnaturel* (1946) (Argument 14). In sixteenth-century Spain Thomism found a stronghold among the Dominicans: Francisco de Vitoria, noted for natural law theory, Domingo de Soto, for economics, and Domingo Báñez, who defended God's efficacious grace against the Jesuit Luis de Molina's "middle knowledge", a dispute Rome left undecided. Pius V named Aquinas a Doctor of the Church in 1567, and the new Jesuit order was told to keep to the safest and best-established views, especially his. [Francisco Suárez](../../eras/03-early-modern/suarez-and-grotius.md) followed him with independence. His *Metaphysical Disputations* (1597) were studied in Jesuit and Lutheran universities for a century, and the library entry lists Descartes and Leibniz among those who answered him; his *On Laws and God the Lawgiver* (1612) is one of the two hinges, with Grotius, between Aquinas's natural law and the moderns' (Argument 15; week 7).

### Luther, the humanists and Hobbes

The Reformation and the "moderns" rejected the scholastic form with the content. The SEP quotes Luther's hope that soon no Thomist, Albertist, Scotist or Ockhamist would be left, only simple children of God. The Italian humanists preferred history and philology to metaphysics. [Thomas Hobbes](../../eras/03-early-modern/thomas-hobbes.md) mocked the schoolmen in *Leviathan* (1651, ch. 8) for talk about matters past understanding. The SEP notes that the scholastics were not driven out of the universities until the eighteenth century.

### Descartes, Leibniz and the cosmological argument

[René Descartes](../../eras/03-early-modern/rene-descartes.md) was trained by Jesuits at La Flèche, and the SEP's "Thomas Aquinas" argues that the infallibilist theory of knowledge now called Cartesian comes from the Aristotelian tradition whose chief spokesman was Aquinas. The *Meditations* (1641) met Aquinas directly. The First Objections, by the Dutch Catholic theologian Johannes Caterus, pressed Thomas's criticism of Anselm against the Fifth Meditation's proof; in the First Replies Descartes agreed with St. Thomas that God's existence is not self-evident to everyone, and argued that it can become evident to a careful meditator (SEP, "Descartes' Ontological Argument"; week 2). [Leibniz](../../eras/03-early-modern/gottfried-wilhelm-leibniz.md) and Samuel Clarke rebuilt the argument from contingency on the principle of sufficient reason, asking for the reason of the whole series of contingent things (*Monadology* §§32–38). That is a different argument from the Third Way, which asks for a cause of necessity, not a reason for the series (Argument 3).

### Hume and Kant

[David Hume](../../eras/03-early-modern/david-hume.md) attacked the two supports of the cosmological arguments: the notion of causation as a necessary, productive power and the principle that every contingent thing has a cause (SEP, "Cosmological Argument"). The *Dialogues* (1779) add the reply that a collection explained member by member needs no further cause (Arguments 1–3) and the limits of the design analogy (Argument 5). His "is–ought" passage in the *Treatise* became the standard objection to natural law (Argument 15). [Immanuel Kant](../../eras/03-early-modern/immanuel-kant.md) argued that the cosmological argument, in identifying the necessary being with the most real being, relies on the ontological argument it seems to avoid (*Critique of Pure Reason* B634–35), and that causal reasoning holds only within possible experience. The library entry's third study question asks you to build the Thomist reply to both (week 2).

### Leo XIII and the twentieth-century Thomisms

On 4 August 1879 Leo XIII's encyclical *Aeterni Patris* called for the restoration of Thomas's philosophy in Catholic teaching. The SEP lists two results: the critical Leonine edition of his works, and a revived picture of Aquinas as the summit from which Scotus, Descartes and Kant were so much decline. In 1914 the decree *Postquam sanctissimus* under Pius X approved twenty-four theses as containing his principles. The twentieth century then produced several Thomisms rather than one: the manual Thomism of Réginald Garrigou-Lagrange at the Angelicum; the "existential Thomism" of Étienne Gilson (1884–1978), who stressed the originality of Thomas's *esse* and read him historically (Argument 6); Jacques Maritain, whom the SEP names with Gilson; and the "transcendental Thomism" of Joseph Maréchal, Karl Rahner and Bernard Lonergan, which tried to meet Kant on his own ground. None of these has a library entry; the [reception file](06-reception-and-debates.md) gives the current state of the disputes between them.

### Anscombe, Geach, Kenny and analytic Thomism

[G. E. M. Anscombe](../../eras/05-20th-century/anscombe-and-foot.md) and Peter Geach brought Aquinas into dialogue with analytic philosophy by the middle of the twentieth century, with Wittgenstein, Anscombe's teacher, as the other influence. Anscombe's "Modern Moral Philosophy" (1958) called for a return to Aristotle and, for her, Aquinas, and her address "Action, Intention and 'Double Effect'" (1982) defended the line between intending and foreseeing that Argument 16 draws from IIa-IIae Q64 a7. Philippa Foot's "The Problem of Abortion and the Doctrine of the Double Effect" (1967) tested that line with the case that became the trolley problem (same entry; week 7). Anthony Kenny's *The Five Ways* (1969), a sceptical analytic examination of the arguments, remains a standard reference in the SEP's bibliography, and its point about "necessary" in the Third Way is used in Argument 3. John Haldane named "analytical Thomism" in the early 1990s, and Eleonore Stump's *Aquinas* (2003) is one of its standard books.

### Grisez, Finnis and MacIntyre

Germain Grisez's "The First Principle of Practical Reason" (1965) and John Finnis's *Natural Law and Natural Rights* (1980) read Q94 a2 as a list of basic goods grasped by practical reason, not derived from facts about nature, and so as untouched by Hume's objection. Their critics read the inclinations as natural facts that ground the goods. Argument 15 sets out the dispute. [Alasdair MacIntyre](../../eras/05-20th-century/alasdair-macintyre.md) came to Aquinas from Marxism by way of Aristotle, and Thomism has been the explicit framework of his work since the mid-1980s. *Whose Justice? Which Rationality?* (1988) works through rival traditions of rationality, and *Three Rival Versions of Moral Enquiry* (1990) is his defence of Thomism against encyclopaedia and genealogy (week 6).

## The themes trace

The library's [problem index](../../themes.md) places Aquinas in five of its nine threads.

- [§1 Knowledge & Skepticism](../../themes.md#1-knowledge--skepticism--what-can-we-know-and-how-do-we-know-it) — The intellect abstracts intelligible forms from phantasms; faith completes reason. Weeks 5 and 8; Aristotle and Avicenna behind, Augustine's illumination revised, Ockham and Descartes ahead (Arguments 13, 9).
- [§2 Mind & Body](../../themes.md#2-mind--body--what-is-mind-and-how-does-it-relate-to-the-body) — The soul is the form of the body yet subsists, and each person has their own intellect. Week 5; Plato and Averroes rejected, Kilwardby and the pluralists beside, Descartes ahead (Arguments 11–12).
- [§3 God & Religion](../../themes.md#3-god--religion--does-god-exist-and-what-follows-for-faith-and-reason) — The Five Ways, simplicity, analogy, the undemonstrable beginning. Weeks 2–4 and 8; Anselm, Avicenna and Maimonides behind, Bonaventure beside, Scotus, Hume and Kant ahead (Arguments 1–10).
- [§4 Ethics & the Good Life](../../themes.md#4-ethics--the-good-life--how-should-i-live-and-what-is-right) — Happiness as the vision of God, natural law, the virtues, double effect. Weeks 6–7; Aristotle, Boethius and Augustine behind, Scotus, Anscombe, Foot, Finnis and MacIntyre ahead (Arguments 14–16).
- [§5 Politics & Justice](../../themes.md#5-politics--justice--how-should-we-live-together-and-who-may-rule) — Law as an ordinance of reason for the common good; the unjust law; just war. Week 7; Cicero and Augustine behind, Suárez, Grotius and Hobbes ahead (Argument 15).

He is absent from §6 (Self & Identity), §7 (Language & Meaning), §8 (Science & Method) and §9 (History & Progress). The first three are filing decisions rather than gaps in his thought. The library entry's "my soul is not I" is a claim about personal identity across death (Argument 11). The analogy of divine names (Argument 8) is a theory of how words signify, and the [Language thread](../../themes.md#7-language--meaning--how-do-words-and-thoughts-hook-onto-the-world) is where Scotus's univocity reply would sit. His account of *scientia* as demonstration from first principles, which the SEP calls the source of the "Cartesian" ideal of knowledge, belongs to the [Science thread](../../themes.md#8-science--method--how-should-inquiry-proceed-and-what-does-it-prove). §9 is a fair omission: he wrote no philosophy of history to set beside Augustine's two cities.
