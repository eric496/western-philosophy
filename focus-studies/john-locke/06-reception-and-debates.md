# Locke Received: Oracle of the Whigs, Patron of the Philosophes, Founder, Capitalist, Colonist, and the Quarrels Still Open
**English** | [简体中文](../../zh/focus-studies/john-locke/06-reception-and-debates.md)

*Focus study: John Locke · [Dossier home](README.md) · [Library entry](../../eras/03-early-modern/john-locke.md) · [Study guide](../../study/locke-essay.md)*

Few philosophical books have made their way as fast as the *Essay*. Within fifteen years of 1690 it had five editions, a French and a Latin translation and a student abridgment, and the heads of the Oxford colleges thought it dangerous enough to keep out of tutorials. Bishops attacked it for what deists made of it, Leibniz wrote a book against it, Berkeley and Hume claimed to finish it, and Voltaire and the *Encyclopédie* made Locke the Newton of the mind. The *Two Treatises* had a slower and stranger career: a Whig text in Britain, an appeal of the American colonists, a foil for Burke, Bentham and the British Idealists, and in the twentieth century the most fought-over book in early modern political thought. Peter Laslett redated it (1960) and so changed what it could be for; after him came a Locke for almost every school: a hidden Hobbesian (Strauss), a possessive individualist (Macpherson), a Calvinist natural lawyer (Dunn), a theorist of workmanship and common ownership (Tully), a revolutionary conspirator (Ashcraft), a libertarian (Nozick), a theorist of equality grounded in God (Waldron), and a colonial administrator whose theory served dispossession and whose name sits on a slave code (Arneil, Armitage, Bernasconi). On the philosophy side, the "way of ideas", the chapter on personal identity and the account of real and nominal essences each started a debate that is still running. This file tells the story by era, sets out six live debates with names on both sides, and closes with the standard misconceptions in full form and the myths about the life.

Quotations from Locke follow the saved public-domain texts, which are in largely modernized spelling: the *Essay* from Project Gutenberg #10615–10616 (a posthumous printing, copy-text unnamed; Locke's italics appear as capitals), the *Second Treatise* from Gutenberg #7370 (whose front matter reproduces the London edition of 1764), the *First Treatise* from the 1764 edition on Wikisource, and the *Letter* in a modernized printing of William Popple's 1689 translation on Wikisource. Read the *Essay* in Nidditch or Winkler and the *Two Treatises* in Laslett ([02-works-and-texts.md](02-works-and-texts.md)). The *Essay* is cited by book, chapter and section in Nidditch's numbering (E II.xxvii.9), with the Gutenberg number in brackets where it differs; the *Second Treatise* by section (ST §27), the *First* likewise (FT §42), and the *Letter* by a locating phrase. Arguments are cited by their numbers in [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md). Dates and scholars' positions are checked against the SEP and IEP articles, Leslie Stephen's article in the *Dictionary of National Biography* (1893, cited as DNB), James A. Harris's study of the British reading of the *Two Treatises* (*British Journal for the History of Philosophy*, 2020), and publisher and catalogue pages; points resting on memory are marked "from memory" and listed in the corrections log.

## The *Essay*'s first fifteen years, 1690–1704

The DNB's verdict is that few books that made epochs in philosophy made their way so rapidly, and its dates bear it out. The *Essay* appeared early in 1690 (title page 1690, on sale from December 1689; [02-works-and-texts.md](02-works-and-texts.md)). A second edition was called for in August 1692 and appeared in the autumn of 1694, with the new chapter on identity and the rewritten chapter on power; a third followed in 1695, a fourth (dated 1700, with the chapters on the association of ideas and on enthusiasm) in the autumn of 1699, and a fifth, posthumously, in 1706. Pierre Coste's French translation appeared at Amsterdam in 1700 and Richard Burridge's Latin in 1701 (DNB; the SEP dates Coste to 1704, a discrepancy [01-life-and-times.md](01-life-and-times.md) records). Before any translation, the Continent knew the book through the French abridgment Le Clerc printed in his *Bibliothèque universelle* in January 1688.

The universities split. St George Ashe, provost of Trinity College, Dublin, recommended the *Essay* to students in 1692, and John Wynne, later bishop of St Asaph, made an abridgment for students with Locke's approval, published in 1696 (DNB). At Oxford the heads of colleges, in the DNB's words, "agreed in 1703 that tutors should not read it with their pupils"; the prohibition, Stephen remarks, "seems to have acted only as an additional advertisement". The ban was informal and did not last: the IEP reports that the *Essay* was, before long, incorporated into the curriculum at Oxford and Cambridge. What alarmed the heads in 1703 was less the theory of ideas than its neighbors: a book that denied innate principles of morality and religion, made assent proportional to evidence, and suggested that God might give matter the power to think (E IV.iii.6) looked like a charter for the deists and Socinians who were already citing it.

## Stillingfleet, Edwards and the deists

The deists made the first use of Locke that his critics feared. John Toland's *Christianity not Mysterious* (1696) claimed, in the DNB's words, "(with some reason)" to be an application to theology of Locke's philosophy: if knowledge is the perception of the agreement of ideas, a doctrine whose terms convey no ideas, such as a mystery above reason, cannot be believed at all (Toland's argument from memory). Edward Stillingfleet, Bishop of Worcester, answered in his *Discourse in Vindication of the Doctrine of the Trinity* (dated 1697; year from memory), and attacked Locke and Toland "as common adversaries" (DNB). The charge that mattered, in the SEP's summary, was that Locke had put substance out of the reasonable part of the world, and with it the support for the doctrine of the Trinity. Stillingfleet's name for the new philosophy, the new way of ideas, stuck (the phrase from memory; not in the saved sources). Locke, "not a little irritated by Toland's claim to philosophical affinity" (DNB), answered in *A Letter to the Bishop of Worcester* (1697) and two replies, the second of which the DNB dates 1697 in its list of works and 1699 in its narrative (see the corrections log). The exchange is the best commentary Locke wrote on his own *Essay*: on substance (Argument 6), on certainty and faith (Arguments 10 and 12) and on thinking matter (E IV.iii.6). The IEP calls it his most famous and philosophically important controversy, and Voltaire's later verdict, that the bishop "argued as a schoolman, and Locke as a philosopher" (*Letters on the English*, XIII), became the Enlightenment's view of it.

The second controversy was about the *Reasonableness of Christianity* (1695), which argued from the Gospels that the faith required for salvation is belief that Jesus is the Messiah, with repentance and a good life. John Edwards attacked it "vehemently" (DNB), charging that a creed so short left out the Trinity and the atonement and was Socinian, or worse (the content of Edwards's charge from memory). Locke answered in two *Vindications* (1695, 1697). Whether Edwards was right about Locke's own beliefs is still argued ([02-works-and-texts.md](02-works-and-texts.md) gives the SEP's evidence on Locke and Newton's anti-Trinitarian tract). Stephen's judgment on the outcome is the standard one: the *Reasonableness* "struck the keynote of the most popular theology of the eighteenth century as represented both by the deists and the latitudinarian divines" (DNB). Anthony Collins, Locke's young friend in the last years, became the most philosophical of the deists, and the IEP names Collins and Toland as figures deeply influenced by Locke's work. Collins pushed the thinking-matter suggestion in the materialist direction in his correspondence with Samuel Clarke (1707–08), which the SEP's article on personal identity treats at length. Stephen saw the pattern that would recur for two centuries: Locke was "the teacher of many disciples who drew from his works conclusions from which he shrank".

The *Letter Concerning Toleration* had its own critic. The Oxford clergyman Jonas Proast argued (1690, from memory) that force, though it cannot produce belief directly, can bring people to consider arguments they would otherwise ignore; Locke answered in a *Second* (1690) and *Third Letter* (1692), and a *Fourth* was left unfinished at his death (DNB). Proast's objection is the starting point of the modern debate (Argument 16).

## Leibniz and the empiricist succession

The most remarkable contemporary criticism, the DNB judged, was Leibniz's *Nouveaux essais sur l'entendement humain*, a chapter-by-chapter dialogue with the *Essay*, written in 1704 and withheld when Locke died; it was first published in 1765. Some shorter "Reflexions" by Leibniz had reached Locke earlier, and Locke mentioned them "rather slightingly" in letters to Molyneux of 1697 (DNB). Leibniz's dispositional innatism, the mind as veined marble, is the standing reply to Book I (Argument 1); [05-conversations.md](05-conversations.md) follows it, and the succession through Berkeley, Hume and Reid, in detail.

The shape of that succession is already in the DNB. "His spiritual descendant, J. S. Mill", called Locke the "unquestioned founder of the analytic philosophy of mind" (*System of Logic*, as quoted by the DNB). Hume's skepticism was "the most famous application of Locke's method", and Reid and Dugald Stewart, while holding that the theory of ideas Locke accepted "would logically lead to Hume", still regarded him as one of the great teachers. Stephen's own summary is that Locke's authority as a philosopher "was unrivalled in England during the first half of the eighteenth century, and retained great weight until the spread of Kantian doctrines". The IEP adds the necessary qualification: those who followed in his footsteps "were not unquestioning followers", and Berkeley, Hume and Reid all offered serious critiques. The label "British Empiricism", with Locke, Berkeley and Hume as its three members, is a later classification, and the [Hobbes dossier](../thomas-hobbes/06-reception-and-debates.md) notes the SEP's suggestion that Hobbes has a better claim than Locke to be the first British empiricist.

## Britain in the eighteenth century: the Whig canon and its critics

In politics the DNB's verdict is that Locke's theories, in the *Two Treatises* and the letters on toleration, "supplied the whigs with their political philosophy for the next century". The IEP is more cautious: the book had some success in England among those favorable to the Revolution, but "its primary impact was abroad". Both are true. Locke's name became a Whig authority, cited in sermons and pamphlets on the Revolution settlement, while the radical parts of the argument, the people as judge and the dissolution of government (Argument 16), were mostly left alone by the governing Whigs (Mark Goldie's multi-volume *The Reception of Locke's Politics*, 1999, collects the texts; the SEP cites its fifth volume). The first Whig use outside England came at once: William Molyneux's *The Case of Ireland* (1698) argued, on what the IEP calls the principles of the *Two Treatises*, that Ireland could be bound only by laws its own parliament had consented to, and the book was condemned by the English House of Commons in 1698 (*Books Condemned to be Burnt*, ch. 6, on Wikisource; the IEP says it was burned, which that source finds no contemporary evidence for).

Philosophical attack came from two directions. Hume's essay "Of the Original Contract" (1748, from memory) argued that almost no government began in consent and that allegiance rests on habit and utility; tacit consent by residence, he objected, is like saying that a man carried on board ship asleep consents to the captain's authority because he does not leap into the sea (Hume's example from memory). The objection is now the first one students meet (Argument 15). The second came from Josiah Tucker, dean of Gloucester, whose *The Notions of Mr. Locke ... Examined and Confuted* (1778) Harris treats as the first full-length discussion of Locke's political philosophy. Tucker wrote against the use of Locke to justify the American cause, though he himself thought America should be granted independence, and argued that society is natural and grows from the family, not from contract. J. G. A. Pocock called his attack the first major refutation of Lockean politics since the nonjuror Charles Leslie's, nearly eighty years before (as Harris quotes him). The striking fact Harris draws from these debates is that eighteenth-century critics still argued with Locke over Filmer: whether subjection is natural, whether paternal and political power differ. None of them treated the *Two Treatises* as a reply to Hobbes.

**Burke.** Edmund Burke's *Reflections on the Revolution in France* (1790) was provoked by Richard Price's sermon *A Discourse on the Love of Our Country* (1789), which Harris calls a display of radicalized Lockeanism: the Revolution of 1688, Price had said, established the people's right to choose their governors, to cashier them for misconduct and to frame a government for themselves (Price's three rights from memory). Burke's reply was that Price misread the settlement of 1688, which had been a careful preservation of hereditary succession, not an act of popular choice. He kept the Lockean idea that civil society is a convention in which a man "gives up his right of determining what is in most points the most essential to him" (*Reflections*, as Harris quotes it), but denied that liberty rests on abstract rights at all: it rests on prescription, inheritance and the common law. Burke never names Locke in the *Reflections*, a puzzle Harris notes, and Pocock judged part of the argument "Lockean enough". The *Appeal from the New to the Old Whigs* (1791) set out to show that the true Whig principles of 1688 were Burke's, not Price's (from memory). The result was a divided inheritance: two Whig canons, one radical and one conservative, each claiming the Revolution and each able to quote Locke, and a habit, which Harris traces to the *Reflections*, of blaming the philosophy of the Enlightenment for the French catastrophe.

## France: Voltaire, Condillac and the *Encyclopédie*

In France, the DNB reports, Locke's name was first made popular by Fontenelle; he was "enthusiastically admired by Voltaire and by d'Alembert, Diderot, Helvetius, and their contemporaries". The IEP gives Voltaire the instrumental role in making Locke, beside Newton, an embodiment of Enlightenment values.

**Voltaire.** The *Letters on the English* (published in English in 1733 and in French, as the *Lettres philosophiques*, in 1734; dates from memory) give Locke the thirteenth letter. Its central image is the one the philosophes adopted: after so many reasoners had written "the romance of the soul, a sage at last arose, who gave, with an air of the greatest modesty, the history of it" (*Letters on the English*, XIII, in an anonymous English translation). Locke is the anatomist of the mind, proceeding step by step from the infant. But the letter's real subject is the passage in Book IV where Locke says we may never know whether a being purely material thinks (E IV.iii.6). Voltaire makes it a manifesto for the separation of philosophy from theology: the question is "purely philosophical, altogether independent of faith and revelation", and the divines who sounded the alarm are compared to bad poets (*Letters*, XIII). In France the book was condemned and burned in 1734 (from memory), and the thinking-matter suggestion became, in Voltaire's hands, the most famous thing about Locke.

**The *Encyclopédie*.** D'Alembert's *Preliminary Discourse* to the *Encyclopédie* (1751) puts Locke beside Newton. Locke, he wrote, created metaphysics "à peu - près comme Newton avoit créé la Physique" (*Discours préliminaire*, as transcribed by the ARTFL project, which keeps its spacing), found the chief causes of our errors in abstractions and the abuse of signs, and reduced metaphysics to what it ought to be, "la Physique expérimentale de l'ame", the experimental physics of the soul. Later in the same survey Leibniz is "moins sage que Locke & Newton" for trying to dissolve doubts the two Englishmen had been content to state. Locke's place at the head of the encyclopedists' map of knowledge, as the founder of a modest, experimental science of the mind, was fixed for the rest of the century.

**Condillac.** Étienne Bonnot de Condillac's *Essai sur l'origine des connaissances humaines* (1746) began as a reworking of Locke, and his *Traité des sensations* (1754) went further, imagining a statue that receives the senses one at a time, beginning with smell, and building every faculty out of sensation (dates from the Larousse encyclopedia and Johns Hopkins catalogue summaries; content from memory). The DNB calls Condillac Locke's "most conspicuous disciple in philosophy", who gave his teaching "the exclusively sensational turn which Locke would have apparently disavowed". The point is exact: Condillac dropped reflection as a second source of ideas and treated the mind's operations themselves as transformed sensations, which is the "blank slate" doctrine in the strong form often wrongly pinned on Locke (misconceptions, below). Condorcet and the *idéologues*, Cabanis and Destutt de Tracy among them, carried the line into the Revolutionary period; Joseph de Maistre attacked Locke as a source of revolutionary views, and Victor Cousin's eclectics criticized him "with great severity and probably much unfairness" (all DNB).

## The American colonies and the Declaration

The IEP's summary is that during the American Revolution, and to a lesser extent during the French, Locke's views "were often appealed to by those seeking to establish more representative forms of government". The SEP places Locke's version of natural rights and contract on "the radical side of the spectrum" and says such theories influenced the ideologies of both revolutions. The IEP's article on the politics adds a route of transmission: Molyneux's Lockean case for Ireland reached the colonies and James Otis and Jefferson. Locke's own ties to America were administrative. He had been secretary to the Lords Proprietors of Carolina from 1669 and, from 1696 to 1700, the most influential member of the Board of Trade, which Laslett called, in the phrase the SEP quotes, "the body which administered the United States before the American Revolution" (SEP; [01-life-and-times.md](01-life-and-times.md)).

The Declaration of Independence (1776) speaks of unalienable rights to "Life, Liberty and the pursuit of Happiness", of governments deriving their just powers from the consent of the governed, and of the right of the people to alter or abolish a government after "a long train of abuses and usurpations" (National Archives transcript). The last phrase echoes Locke's "a long train of abuses, prevarications and artifices, all tending the same way" (ST §225), and "the pursuit of happiness" is a phrase Locke used in the *Essay*, though about the will, not about politics: "the highest perfection of intellectual nature lies in a careful and constant pursuit of true and solid happiness" (E II.xxi.51 [Gutenberg §52]). Locke's own political triad was "lives, liberties and estates, which I call by the general name, property" (ST §123). Jefferson himself, writing to Henry Lee in 1825, denied that the Declaration aimed at originality: its authority rested "on the harmonizing sentiments of the day", expressed in conversation, letters, essays "or in the elementary books of public right, as Aristotle, Cicero, Locke, Sidney, &c." (letter of 8 May 1825, as printed by the National Park Service).

How much the founding owed to Locke is one of the field's standing debates (debate 4 below). Carl Becker's *The Declaration of Independence: A Study in the History of Political Ideas* (1922) set the classic view: the Declaration states the natural-rights philosophy of Locke, which Jefferson had absorbed. Louis Hartz's *The Liberal Tradition in America* (1955) generalized it into a thesis about the whole country: a society "born free", without feudalism, in which an unchallenged Lockean liberalism set the limits of political thought (Hartz's phrase and thesis as summarized in an OUP chapter abstract and catalogue descriptions). The revisionists came in the 1960s and 1970s. Bernard Bailyn's *The Ideological Origins of the American Revolution* (1967) found in the pamphlets a political language drawn above all from the English opposition writers of the early eighteenth century, the commonwealthmen, in which Locke was cited often but used thinly (position from memory). J. G. A. Pocock's *The Machiavellian Moment* (Princeton, 1975) traced a language of civic virtue and corruption from Renaissance Florence through the English Civil War to America, and became, in Princeton's own description, the emblem of the so-called republican synthesis: the view that the new republic was founded in fear of corruption and in pursuit of classical virtue. Garry Wills's *Inventing America* (1978), working from Jefferson's drafts, argued that Jefferson's thought and phrasing came less from Locke than from the Scottish moral-sense philosophers, above all Francis Hutcheson (PhilPapers and publisher summaries). Each claim drew a counterattack: Ronald Hamowy's critique of Wills (1979; author and venue from memory, the critique itself reprinted by the Independent Institute) was severe on his reading of the sources, and the Straussians Thomas Pangle (*The Spirit of Modern Republicanism*, 1988, in the SEP's bibliography) and Michael Zuckert (*Natural Rights and the New Republicanism*, 1994, also there) argued that the republican and Lockean languages were fused, with Lockean natural rights doing the founding work (Pangle's and Zuckert's positions on the founding from memory).

## The nineteenth century: utilitarians, Idealists and the Hobbes frame

Stephen thought that Locke's politics, though "content with a partial application", "laid the foundation of the more thoroughgoing doctrines of Bentham and the later radicals" (DNB). Bentham himself was less grateful. In a manuscript Harris dates to the early 1780s he sided with Filmer on the facts: "Filmer's origin of government is exemplified everywhere: Locke's scheme of government has not ever, to the knowledge of any body, been exemplified any where" (Bentham, "Filmer and Locke", as Harris quotes it). Every human being is trained to subjection in the family, so the state of natural equality is a fiction, and the utilitarians replaced natural rights and consent with utility as the test of government. William Paley, in the *Principles of Moral and Political Philosophy* (1785, from memory), rejected the original contract on the same grounds. On the philosophy side, the empiricist school continued to regard Locke as its founder, though, the DNB notes, it was more directly shaped by Berkeley, Hume and David Hartley.

The Idealists turned the empiricist inheritance into a target. T. H. Green's long Introduction to the 1874 edition of Hume's *Treatise* (edited with T. H. Grose; date and co-editor from memory), which the DNB lists among recent expositions of Locke, read Locke, Berkeley and Hume as one argument whose end was Hume's skepticism: if the mind begins with nothing but feelings, the relations that make experience intelligible can never be got out of them, so the way of ideas must give way to a theory of the mind's constructive activity (Green's argument from memory). In politics Green's *Lectures on the Principles of Political Obligation*, delivered at Oxford in 1879–80 and published posthumously (dates from memory; Project Gutenberg #61889 reprints them from the *Philosophical Works*), treated natural rights and the original contract as confusions to be overcome on the way to a state that realizes freedom. Harris shows what that framing did to Locke. In Green's lectures Filmer is not mentioned at all; Green moves from Hobbes directly to the *Second Treatise*, and the *First Treatise* disappears. Fitzjames Stephen thought the refutation of Filmer "a mere weariness to the flesh"; Leslie Stephen found it odd that Locke did not engage Hobbes, whose ideas he was really challenging; Frederick Pollock (1890) went from Hobbes to Locke without Filmer at all (all as Harris quotes them). By the early twentieth century, Harold Laski and George Sabine taught a Locke who answered Hobbes, wrote to justify 1688, and wasted his intelligence on a feeble opponent. That textbook picture is what the next generation overturned. Henry Fox Bourne's two-volume *Life of John Locke* (1876), the first full biography, had already argued that the *First Treatise* belonged to the early 1680s (Harris), but the point made no impression until Laslett.

## The twentieth century: redating and the rival Lockes

**Laslett.** Peter Laslett's article "The English Revolution and Locke's 'Two Treatises of Government'" (*Cambridge Historical Journal*, 1956) and his critical edition (Cambridge, 1960) changed the subject. From the dates of the books Locke cites and his use of Filmer's works as republished in 1679–80, Laslett argued that the book was written before 1683, probably between 1679 and 1681, to support Shaftesbury's campaign to exclude James from the throne: "John Locke's Two Treatises is an Exclusion Tract, not a Revolution Pamphlet" (Laslett 1956, as Harris quotes it). Filmer, "the man of the moment", not Hobbes, was the target, and it is "almost as mistaken to suppose that he was arguing deliberately against Leviathan" as to believe he wrote to rationalize the Revolution (Laslett's Introduction, as Harris quotes it). Laslett also held that the *Second Treatise* was begun before the *First* and that the *Two Treatises* should be kept apart from the *Essay*: "Locke the doctor rather than Locke the epistemologist" wrote it, and to call it political philosophy is "inappropriate" (as Harris quotes it). Laslett's dating has been challenged in its details by Richard Ashcraft, John Marshall and David Wootton (Harris), and the SEP now gives 1681; the Exclusion setting is no longer in serious dispute. Laslett's separation of the two Lockes is still argued (debate 1 and [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md), "Known internal tensions").

**Strauss.** Leo Strauss's *Natural Right and History* (1953) had already offered the opposite kind of Locke. Strauss read the *Second Treatise* as a cautious work whose Christian and natural-law surface conceals a Hobbesian core: Locke "had the good sense to quote only the right kind of writers and to be silent about the wrong kind, although he had more in common, in the last analysis, with the wrong kind than with the right" (*Natural Right and History*, as Harris quotes it). The SEP sets out the evidence Strauss used: Locke's hedonist psychology (E II.xx), the priority of self-preservation over duties to others, and the contradictions between the *Essay*'s demonstrable morality and the *Treatises*' unproved law of nature, which Strauss read as signals to the attentive reader that Locke did not believe in natural law. On property Strauss's Locke is the theorist of unlimited acquisition, emancipated from the old moral restraints (from memory). Michael Zuckert (1994) and Thomas Pangle (1988) developed the reading, Zuckert granting more difference from Hobbes, since Locke grounds property in self-ownership, which Hobbes denied (SEP).

**Macpherson.** C. B. Macpherson's *The Political Theory of Possessive Individualism: Hobbes to Locke* (1962) gave the Marxist version. In the SEP's summary, Macpherson found three limits on appropriation in the state of nature, spoilage, sufficiency and one's own labor, and argued that each is transcended as the chapter proceeds: money removes spoilage, productivity makes sufficiency irrelevant, and "the turfs my servant has cut" (ST §28) show that labor can be sold. Locke's theory is thus a justification of unlimited capitalist accumulation, whose coherence depends on assuming that capitalists and wage laborers differ in rationality, and Locke's political society includes only property owners as full members. Alan Ryan (1965) answered that "property" for Locke includes life and liberty, so the propertyless are members too; Tully answered with the duty of charity (both SEP; debate 3).

**Dunn.** John Dunn's *The Political Thought of John Locke* (1969) took Laslett's history and drew the opposite conclusion from Strauss's. Locke's political theory is unintelligible without his Calvinist theology: human beings are God's workmanship, bound to preserve themselves and others, and the right of resistance is the conclusion of a theology of individual responsibility before God ([04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Arguments 13 and 16). Dunn's preface famously doubted that one could build an analysis of any issue in contemporary political theory around anything Locke said (the gist from memory), and his later essay "What Is Living and What Is Dead in the Political Theory of John Locke?" (1990, from memory) qualified it. Dunn, Tully and Ashcraft are the SEP's names for the majority view that natural law, not natural rights, is primary in Locke: rights exist so that we can fulfill our duties.

**Tully and Ashcraft.** James Tully's *A Discourse on Property: John Locke and His Adversaries* (1980) read chapter 5 through a "workmanship model": makers have rights in what they make, as God has in us, and labor is purposive making according to a rational plan, not a substance poured into things (SEP). The original community of the earth is inclusive; the duty of charity (FT §42) and the provisos keep binding; and once land becomes scarce, property is legitimate only as political society regulates it. Tully's book was the first sustained answer to Macpherson from inside the Cambridge historical method. Richard Ashcraft's *Revolutionary Politics and Locke's Two Treatises of Government* (1986) pushed the history further: the book belongs to the radical Whigs of 1680–82 who were moving from parliamentary agitation toward armed resistance, and it reads as a call to revolution, addressed to an audience that included artisans and tradesmen (IEP; SEP; the last point from memory). Ashcraft's Locke is deep in conspiracy, in England and among the exiles; Woolhouse's biography (2007) and Marshall (1994) are more cautious ([01-life-and-times.md](01-life-and-times.md)).

**The philosophers' Locke.** Analytic political philosophy took Locke up as a contemporary. Robert Nozick's *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* (1974) made a Lockean state of nature and Lockean rights the starting point of a libertarian theory, and the SEP notes that Nozick reads Locke as a libertarian; yet his most quoted pages are a critique of the labor-mixing argument (the tomato juice poured into the sea) and a reworking of the proviso (Argument 14). Jeremy Waldron's *The Right to Private Property* (1988) denied that Locke held a sufficiency proviso at all, and his *God, Locke, and Equality* (2002) argued that the theological premise is what gives Locke's equality its force, a position Waldron offered against secular Rawlsian readings (SEP; the Rawls point from memory). A. John Simmons's *The Lockean Theory of Rights* (1992) and *On the Edge of Anarchy* (1993) reconstructed a Lockean theory of rights and consent and concluded that, by its own standards, almost no existing state is legitimate, since almost no one has consented in the required way (SEP for the titles; the conclusion from memory; Argument 15).

## The colonial and slavery questions

Since the 1990s the most charged debates have been about America; the library entry already flags the critical readings of the property theory as a justification for taking Indigenous land. The texts are in chapter 5: "Thus in the beginning all the world was America" (ST §49), and the "inland, vacant places of America" where a planter may still take land (ST §36). Tully's "Rediscovering America: The *Two Treatises* and Aboriginal Rights" (1993; title from memory) and Barbara Arneil's *John Locke and America: The Defence of English Colonialism* (Oxford, 1996) argued that Locke's theory of labor yields the convenient conclusion that Native Americans' labor gave them property only in the animals they caught, not in the land they hunted, which Locke treated as vacant and open to the industrious (SEP). David Armitage's "John Locke, Carolina, and the *Two Treatises of Government*" (2004) found evidence that Locke was revising the *Fundamental Constitutions of Carolina* at the time he was drafting the property chapter (SEP). Mark Goldie (1983) cautions that English politics remained Locke's primary focus (SEP), and defenders such as Simmons argue that the theory, applied fairly, protects Indigenous holdings, since hunting and gathering are labor too and conquest gives no title to land: the descendants of the conquered "retain a right to the possession of their ancestors" (ST §192, a passage the IEP's article on the politics applies to Native land claims).

On slavery the text and the life pull hard against each other. ST ch. 4 allows slavery only as the condition of a captive taken in a just war who has forfeited his life, and ch. 16 denies a conqueror any power over the captive's wife, children or estate. The *Fundamental Constitutions* of 1669, a draft of which survives in Locke's hand, give every freeman "absolute power and authority over his negro slaves" (as the SEP quotes it; [01-life-and-times.md](01-life-and-times.md)), and Locke was, the SEP says, an investor as well as a colonial administrator (his shares in the Royal African Company and the Bahama Adventurers from memory). The SEP sets out the positions. A "steady stream" of work over sixty years holds that the theory of slavery was meant to justify Afro-American slavery; Robert Bernasconi and Anika Maaza Mann's "The Contradictions of Racism: Locke, Slavery, and the *Two Treatises*" (in Andrew Valls, ed., *Race and Racism in Modern Philosophy*, Cornell, 2005; Library of Congress contents) is a much-cited statement, arguing that the philosophy was shaped to rationalize the practice (position as summarized in course and journal notes, from memory in detail). The SEP's own judgment is that the narrow conditions for legitimate slavery and the limits on conquerors would condemn the actual slave trade. James Farr's "Locke, Natural Law, and New World Slavery" (2008) argues that Locke's theory was aimed at English absolutism, not African slavery, while granting that his involvement has ruined his reputation as the champion of liberty; William Uzgalis (2017) argues that Locke has a theory of illegitimate slavery under which the Atlantic trade falls; Holly Brewer (2017) shows that the Stuarts, James above all as governor of the Royal African Company, promoted the slave trade for revenue, so that Locke's attack on absolutism was an attack on the regime that ran it (all SEP). Woolhouse's verdict, which the SEP quotes and [01-life-and-times.md](01-life-and-times.md) repeats, is that there "certainly seems to be 'a glaring contradiction between his theories and Afro-American slavery'".

## The philosophers' quarrels: ideas, persons, essences

Three debates on the *Essay* side run from Locke's own century to now. Each is set out with its texts in [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md); this section gives the history and the current line-up.

**The way of ideas and the veil of perception.** Stillingfleet named the new way of ideas; Reid made it the villain of modern philosophy. On Reid's reading, which the DNB echoes, Locke's theory that the mind perceives only its own ideas leads by logical steps to Berkeley's idealism and Hume's skepticism, since nothing but ideas can ever be compared with ideas (Arguments 2, 10 and 11). The twentieth century divided over whether that is Locke. Jonathan Bennett (*Locke, Berkeley, Hume: Central Themes*, 1971) gave the inner-object reading its modern form and its name, the veil of perception, following Berkeley; J. L. Mackie (*Problems from Locke*, 1976) accepted that Locke was a representative realist and defended representative realism as a scientifically respectable position, adding that Locke's semantics, unlike Berkeley's, lets him speak of unobserved causes such as atoms (SEP). On the other side, A. D. Woozley and John Yolton denied that Locke's ideas are objects standing between mind and world: Yolton (*John Locke and the Way of Ideas*, 1956; *Perceptual Acquaintance from Descartes to Reid*, 1984) read them as perceptual acts or contents through which things are known, and argued that the veil was largely Reid's construction. Michael Ayers (*Locke: Epistemology and Ontology*, 1991) fits neither camp neatly: he reads ideas of sensation as sensory states caused by things and gives sensitive knowledge a perceptual, not an inferential, basis (position from memory). The 2003 American Philosophical Association symposium, in the SEP's report, found most participants holding that Locke had a representative theory but was not a skeptic of the kind the veil doctrine implies.

**Personal identity, from Butler and Reid to Parfit.** The chapter added in 1694 (E II.xxvii) was attacked at once by those who thought persistence must be the persistence of a soul: Butler, Reid and Samuel Clarke, in the SEP's list. Joseph Butler's dissertation "Of Personal Identity", appended to *The Analogy of Religion* (1736), gave the circularity objection its classic form: "consciousness of personal identity presupposes, and therefore cannot constitute, personal identity; any more than knowledge, in any other case, can constitute truth, which it presupposes" (Butler, as the SEP quotes him). Thomas Reid's *Essays on the Intellectual Powers of Man* (1785) gave the transitivity objection, the brave officer who remembers being flogged as a boy and is remembered by the old general who has forgotten the flogging (Argument 7). Locke had defenders too: Edmund Law (1769) read "person" as a mode, not a substance, a reading the SEP finds popular today; Anthony Collins took up the consciousness view in his exchange with Clarke; and William Hazlitt (1805) argued from it that concern for one's own future self has no special rational standing, a move the SEP notes Parfit repeated "almost one hundred and eighty years later" without knowing Hazlitt. In the twentieth century John Perry (1975), David Lewis (1976), Sydney Shoemaker (1984) and Derek Parfit (*Reasons and Persons*, 1984) built psychological continuity theories that their authors regard as Lockean, chaining overlapping memories to meet Reid; Parfit defended what he called a "Lockean view" as late as 2016 (all SEP). The opposition is now led by animalists, who hold that we are human organisms; Eric Olson calls even his animalism Lockean, because it relies on Locke's account of the persistence of living things, while Stephen Blatti and Paul Snowdon (2016) blame Locke for separating persons from human animals in the first place (SEP). Marya Schechtman's narrative account (*The Constitution of Selves*, 1996) starts from Locke as well. Whether Locke's "consciousness" is memory at all, and whether his persons are substances or modes, are the current interpretive questions (SEP).

**Real and nominal essences.** Leibniz's *New Essays* objected that Locke's nominal essences made kinds arbitrary, and the twentieth century returned to the question when Saul Kripke and Hilary Putnam (1970s; from memory) revived the idea of hidden essences that our words pick out before we know them, a view that some readers count as Locke's real essences with the skepticism removed. Interpreters divide over whether Locke denies that nature has kinds or only that we can know them: Ayers (1991) and William Uzgalis (1988) read him as denying sharp natural kinds at any level, while Arthur Lovejoy (*The Great Chain of Being*, 1936) and David Wiggins read him as holding that there are real kinds at the atomic level beyond our reach (SEP; Argument 9). Stephen Gaukroger (2010) connects the doctrine to the experimental philosophy of Boyle and Sydenham, which explains phenomena by phenomena without waiting for a matter theory (SEP).

## Live debates

The philosophical debates are above; these six are the political and historical ones, summarized with pointers to the arguments that carry their texts.

**(1) When, why, and how far one book?** That the *Two Treatises* were written in the Exclusion Crisis is settled; the details are not. Laslett put the main composition in 1679–81 and held the *Second Treatise* to be the earlier; Ashcraft (1986) placed it in 1680–82 among radicals preparing for armed resistance; the SEP gives 1681 and allows that the book may have been meant in part to justify a planned rising ([01-life-and-times.md](01-life-and-times.md); [02-works-and-texts.md](02-works-and-texts.md)). Marshall and Wootton dispute Laslett's order (Harris). The 1689 Preface then reframes an Exclusion tract as a vindication of William, and Goldie's work on 1689 reads publication as an argument that William's authority was de jure as well as de facto (Harris's note). The second question is whether the *Treatises* and the *Essay* form one philosophy. Laslett and Strauss both see contradiction, the *Essay* promising a demonstrable morality and denying innate principles while the *Treatises* assume a law of nature they never derive; Laslett keeps the two Lockes apart, Strauss reads the gap as a signal. Yolton, Ashcraft, Ruth Grant, Simmons, Alex Tuckness and others answer that nothing is strictly inconsistent: that no one has deduced the whole law of nature does not mean none of it can be (SEP; Arguments 1 and 13).

**(2) A Hobbesian in disguise, or a Christian natural lawyer?** Strauss (1953), and with modifications Pangle (1988) and Zuckert (1994), take rights to be primary and self-preservation to trump duty, so that Locke's differences from Hobbes are of tone and prudence. Dunn (1969), Tully (1980) and Ashcraft (1986), now the majority in the SEP's judgment, take natural law and duty to be primary, with rights as the means of doing one's duty to preserve oneself and mankind, and Waldron (2002) makes the theology the ground of equality. Simmons (1992) holds rights and duties equally basic, with a "robust zone of indifference" for free choice; Brian Tierney (2014) questions whether the choice is needed at all (SEP). The Hobbes dossier's [conversations file](../thomas-hobbes/05-conversations.md) has the other side of the comparison (Argument 13).

**(3) Property: capitalist charter, constrained right, or no proviso?** Macpherson (1962): the limits fall away and Locke justifies unlimited accumulation by a propertied class. Ryan (1965) and Tully (1980): property in the broad sense includes life and liberty, the duty of charity (FT §42) survives, and scarcity hands the question to political society. Waldron (1988): "enough, and as good" is a sufficient condition, a description of early plenty, not a restriction; Gopal Sreenivasan (1995) defends a version of the proviso as a guarantee of the opportunity to preserve oneself. Nozick (1974): the mixing metaphor fails, and a strict proviso would undo every appropriation (SEP; Argument 14).

**(4) How Lockean was the founding?** Becker (1922) and Hartz (1955): the Declaration and American political culture are Lockean through and through. Bailyn (1967), Pocock (1975) and Wills (1978): the operative languages were opposition Whig, classical republican or Scottish, with Locke one name among many. Pangle (1988), Zuckert (1994) and others: the republican synthesis was overdrawn, and Lockean natural rights were the frame the other languages fitted into. Steven Dworetz (*The Unvarnished Doctrine*, 1990) and Jerome Huyler (*Locke in America*, 1995) argued that the colonial clergy carried a theological Locke into the Revolution (both from memory). Most historians now describe an amalgam, which leaves the weight of each strand open. Jefferson's own list, "Aristotle, Cicero, Locke, Sidney", suggests he would have said the same.

**(5) Colonialism and slavery.** On the land: Tully (1993), Arneil (1996) and Armitage (2004) read the property chapter as shaped by, and serving, English colonization; Goldie (1983) and defenders such as Simmons stress English politics as the primary target and argue that the principles, fairly applied, protect Native holdings. On slavery: Bernasconi and Mann (2005) and a long line before them read the theory as rationalizing the practice; Farr (2008), Uzgalis (2017) and Brewer (2017) argue that it condemns Atlantic slavery and was aimed at Stuart absolutism, which ran the slave trade. Both sides agree on the record of Locke's involvement, as investor, secretary and drafter; they disagree over whether that record explains the text or contradicts it (SEP; Arguments 13 and 14).

**(6) Toleration and its limits.** Proast's objection, that force can make people attend to reasons, was restated by Waldron ("Locke: Toleration and the Rationality of Persecution", dated 1993 in the SEP; first published, from memory, in 1988) as the charge that Locke's central argument shows only that persecution aimed directly at belief is irrational. Whether Locke has an answer is the current debate: Tuckness (2008) and Tate (2016) argue that Locke himself de-emphasized the rationality argument in his later writings, and Susan Mendus (1989) and Paul Bou Habib (2003) try to save it by arguing that coerced belief, or inquiry under duress, is not the genuine article the magistrate would be seeking (SEP). On the exclusions of atheists and Catholics, John Marshall's *John Locke, Toleration and Early Enlightenment Culture* (2006) places them in a European campaign for toleration whose limits were common ground; others, Waldron among them, treat them as inconsistent with Locke's own principle that belief cannot be compelled (SEP; [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 16).

## Misconceptions, in full form

The [library entry](../../eras/03-early-modern/john-locke.md)'s "Misreadings to avoid" and the [study guide](../../study/locke-essay.md)'s "Common misreadings" give the short forms.

- **"Locke says the mind is a blank slate with no innate capacities."** Book I denies innate *ideas and principles*, not innate powers. Its program is to show "how men, barely by the use of their natural faculties may attain to all the knowledge they have, without the help of any innate impressions" (E I.ii.1), and the faculties do the work: perception, retention, discerning, comparing, compounding and abstracting (E II.ix–xi). Locke even grants innate practical tendencies: "Nature, I confess, has put into man a desire of happiness and an aversion to misery: these indeed are innate practical principles", though they are "INCLINATIONS OF THE APPETITE to good, not impressions of truth on the understanding" (E I.iii.3). Reflection is a second source of ideas alongside sensation (E II.i.4). The IEP calls the no-capacities reading the one misunderstanding "important to avoid" in Locke's anti-nativism. The strong doctrine, that every faculty is transformed sensation, is Condillac's, and the DNB already notes that Locke "would have apparently disavowed" it (Arguments 1–2).

- **"'Tabula rasa' is Locke's phrase."** It does not occur in the *Essay* (checked in the saved Gutenberg text). Locke's image is "white paper", which appears twice: once in Book I, of the principles adults instil in children "(for white paper receives any characters,)" (E I.iii.22), and once in the programmatic sentence of Book II, "Let us then suppose the mind to be, as we say, white paper, void of all characters, without any ideas" (E II.i.2). The words "as we say" mark it as a common figure, not a doctrine. The Latin phrase belongs to the scholastic tradition, rendering Aristotle's writing tablet in *De Anima* III.4 (from memory), and modern summaries, the SEP and IEP among them, use it as a convenient label for Locke's view; whether Locke ever wrote the Latin phrase himself outside the *Essay*, in drafts or early writings, is not settled here (corrections log, "Doubts").

- **"Locke defended unlimited accumulation."** This is one reading, Macpherson's, not the text's plain statement, and it has to be argued for. The law of nature that gives property "does also bound that property too": "As much as any one can make use of to any advantage of life before it spoils, so much he may by his labour fix a property in: whatever is beyond this, is more than his share, and belongs to others" (ST §31). Money changes the spoilage limit, since durable things can be heaped up, "the exceeding of the bounds of his just property not lying in the largeness of his possession, but the perishing of any thing uselesly in it" (ST §46), and men "have agreed to a disproportionate and unequal possession of the earth" by a "tacit and voluntary consent" to money (ST §50). But the *First Treatise* keeps a floor: "charity gives every man a title to so much out of another's plenty, as will keep him from extreme want, where he has no means to subsist otherwise" (FT §42). Whether "enough, and as good" (ST §27) survives money is debate 3. Locke allows great inequality; whether he allows it without limit is exactly what Macpherson, Tully and Waldron dispute (Argument 14).

- **"Locke's toleration included atheists and Catholics."** It excluded both, and for civil, not doctrinal, reasons. "Lastly, those are not at all to be tolerated who deny the being of a God. Promises, covenants, and oaths, which are the bonds of human society, can have no hold upon an atheist" (*Letter*, on the limits of toleration). And no church may be tolerated "which is constituted upon such a bottom that all those who enter into it do thereby ipso facto deliver themselves up to the protection and service of another prince" (*Letter*, same passage); Locke's illustration is a Muslim who owes "blind obedience to the Mufti of Constantinople", and the SEP reads the clause as aimed at Roman Catholics. The *Letter* also excludes churches that teach that faith need not be kept with heretics. Its reach was nonetheless wide for 1689: "neither Pagan nor Mahometan, nor Jew, ought to be excluded from the civil rights of the commonwealth because of his religion" (*Letter*, on the rights of all peaceable men). The library entry's summary is exact: limits "his own principles have since been used to criticize and extend beyond" (debate 6; Argument 16).

- **"The *Two Treatises* was written to justify 1688."** It was published to justify it. The Preface hopes the book will "establish the throne of our great restorer, our present King William; to make good his title, in the consent of the people" (TT, Preface), and for two and a half centuries readers took it at its word. Since Laslett (1956, 1960) it has been agreed that the book was written in substance during the Exclusion Crisis, in support of Shaftesbury's cause, a decade before the Revolution; the disputes are over the years within 1679–83 and over how far it was meant to justify armed resistance (debate 1). The library entry's wording, written during the Exclusion Crisis and published in 1689 as a justification of the Revolution, is correct.

- **"The Declaration copied Locke's 'life, liberty and property'."** Locke's formula is "lives, liberties and estates, which I call by the general name, property" (ST §123); the Declaration has "Life, Liberty and the pursuit of Happiness". The swap is not a departure from Locke's vocabulary, since the *Essay* says "the highest perfection of intellectual nature lies in a careful and constant pursuit of true and solid happiness" (E II.xxi.51 [Gutenberg §52]), but nor is it a quotation from the *Two Treatises*; Wills traced Jefferson's wording to the Scots instead (debate 4). The one clear verbal echo is "a long train of abuses" (ST §225).

- **"Locke wrote his politics to answer Hobbes."** The *Two Treatises* name Filmer on almost every page of the *First* and argue with him throughout; Hobbes is not named (from memory), and Laslett judged it almost as mistaken to read the book as an attack on *Leviathan* as to read it as a defense of 1688. Harris shows that the Hobbes frame was a nineteenth-century creation, carried into the textbooks of Laski and Sabine. The logical contrast with Hobbes is real and the dossier uses it ([03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md)); Strauss's claim of a hidden debt is a live interpretation (debate 2). What is a misconception is the claim that the *Second Treatise* was written as a reply to *Leviathan*.

## Myths about the life

[01-life-and-times.md](01-life-and-times.md) ("Weighing the sources") sorts the stories by witness. The common myths:

- **"Locke wrote the Carolina constitutions."** A draft of the *Fundamental Constitutions* (1669) survives in Locke's hand and he worked on later revisions, but he was secretary to the Lords Proprietors, and the scheme is theirs, above all Ashley's; how much is Locke's is disputed (Armitage against Farr and others; the DNB reads the church clause as his own). He neither wrote it alone nor had nothing to do with it.

- **"Locke was a Rye House plotter" and "Locke was a cautious don who kept out of politics."** Both outrun the evidence. Ashcraft's Locke was deep in radical Whig conspiracy; the DNB judged him not privy to the plots, and Woolhouse is cautious. What is certain is that he fled in 1683, lived under a false name and appeared on the government's list of 1685, and that Dean Fell, ordered to expel him from Christ Church in 1684, reported that no one had heard him speak a word for or against the government ([01-life-and-times.md](01-life-and-times.md)).

- **"Oxford expelled Locke for his opinions" and "Oxford banned the *Essay*."** The expulsion of 1684 was from his Christ Church studentship, by royal order, for his connection with Shaftesbury, not by the university for his books. The 1703 measure was an informal agreement of the heads of colleges that tutors should not read the *Essay* with pupils (DNB), and the book was soon on the curriculum (IEP).

- **"Dr Locke the physician."** He practiced medicine, worked with Sydenham and managed the operation of 1668 on Ashley's abscess, but he never took the doctorate: the MB came only in 1674 (DNB; [01-life-and-times.md](01-life-and-times.md)). That the operation saved Ashley's life is the household's belief, which the SEP accepts; the silver tube became a joke for the satirists (DNB).

- **The edifying death.** That Locke died quietly on 28 October 1704 while Lady Masham read the Psalms to him rests on the household at Oates, as do most stories of his character. It is not implausible, but it is a friend's report.
