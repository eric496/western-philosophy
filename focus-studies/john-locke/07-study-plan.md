## What this plan is, and where it plugs in

The [learning path](../../learning-path.md) meets Locke in **Stage 3 (Early Modern to Kant)** at row 7, as a core (●) figure: *Essay* Book II (II.i–viii, II.xxi, II.xxvii) with Book I as a summary pass, then *Second Treatise* chs. 2, 5 and 7–9, in Nidditch or Winkler for the *Essay* and Laslett for the *Two Treatises*, with the [study guide to the *Essay*](../../study/locke-essay.md) as companion. The path sets him straight after [Hobbes](../../eras/03-early-modern/thomas-hobbes.md) (row 6) so that the state-of-nature contrast lands, and before [Berkeley](../../eras/03-early-modern/george-berkeley.md) (row 8), who attacks Arguments 3 and 8, [Montesquieu](../../eras/03-early-modern/montesquieu.md) (row 9) and [Rousseau](../../eras/03-early-modern/jean-jacques-rousseau.md) (row 11), who rewrite Arguments 13–16. This dossier keeps that core, order and guide, and goes deeper on purpose: the path's selections are the heart of Weeks 2, 3, 4, 6 and 7, and around them come Book I and the Epistle with the life (Week 1), substance (II.xxiii) in Week 3, words and essences (Book III) in Week 4, knowledge, faith and enthusiasm (Book IV) in Week 5, the rest of the *Second Treatise* with the *First Treatise* in selection (Weeks 6–7), and the *Letter Concerning Toleration* (Week 7). [02-works-and-texts.md](02-works-and-texts.md), "The works in the dossier's reading order," gives the reasons.

Run it as one block in place of row 7. It assumes the [Hobbes dossier](../thomas-hobbes/README.md) or at least the path's row 6, since Weeks 3 and 6 set Locke against Hobbes's compatibilism (Hobbes's Argument 16) and his state of nature (Hobbes's Argument 6), and [Descartes](../../eras/03-early-modern/rene-descartes.md) (row 1), whom Week 1 reads Locke against. [Hume](../../eras/03-early-modern/david-hume.md) (row 10) picks up Weeks 2, 4 and 5.

**Total cost, stated honestly: ≈40 hours all-in across seven weeks: 30.0 hours at the desk, ≈3.4 hours of assigned media, and ≈6.5 hours of the daily [Anki](https://apps.ankiweb.net) [FSRS](https://docs.ankiweb.net/deck-options.html) queue (about 8 minutes a day for 49 days, 392 minutes).** The table rounds each week's 56 minutes of cards to 0.9 hours and each week's media to one decimal, so its columns sum to 30.0 + 3.5 + 6.3 = 39.8; unrounded, 30.0 + 3.43 + 6.53 = 39.96. Weekly totals run from 4.4 to 6.7 hours; optional items are not counted. Running times are those of [08-bibliography.md](08-bibliography.md), §5: *Philosophize This!* eps. 36 and 35 (29 min 5 s and 31 min 50 s), *In Our Time*'s "Empiricism" (45 minutes), and two Yale PLSC 114 lectures, whose pages state no length; the course page gives 50-minute sessions, so each is priced at 50 minutes, an upper estimate. 29 min 5 s + 31 min 50 s + 45 + 100 = 205 min 55 s. The Wi-Phi lessons state no lengths and sit inside desk hours, as the path does with Wi-Phi.

**Card time is an estimate tied to deck size.** The path's daily queue is about 10 minutes, about six days a week, for the whole cumulative deck (§2). This plan counts 8 minutes a day as the share this dossier's cards add, the rate the [Hobbes plan](../thomas-hobbes/07-study-plan.md) uses for a deck of 42 cards, here a deck of 40: 16 argument cards, one per argument in [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), and 24 concept cards keyed to the key terms of [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md). At a different rate, card hours are rate × 49 days ÷ 60.

**The accounting against the path, stated plainly. Locke is a core row, so the path already pays for part of this plan.** The path fixes no week count for the row. At the stage's rate (72 desk hours over 16 weeks, 4.5 a week), and with the study guide's own reading plan for the *Essay* selections running "roughly a week and a half," plus about half a week for *Second Treatise* chs. 2, 5 and 7–9, this dossier estimates the row at about two weeks and 9 desk hours, its own estimate, not the path's. Those 9 hours fall inside this plan: Book I's summary pass in Week 1, II.i–viii in Week 2, II.xxi in Week 3, II.xxvii in Week 4, and the *Second Treatise* chapters in Weeks 6–7. Of the media, only *Philosophize This!* eps. 35 and 36 are in the path's budget, since it hands the audio spine to West from Locke on (≈+9 hours for the stage's arc); the *In Our Time* programme is not, and the Yale lectures are the path's optional PLSC 114 set. The Hobbes dossier's own ≈33 hours are separate: running both takes Stage 3 to roughly 172.

The path (§1.4) allows a stage at most two extra weeks; seven weeks for a two-week row is about five. Choose deliberately. (a) Run the whole plan in the comfortable version (Calendar B, path §10). (b) Run Weeks 2, 3, 4 and 6 as the row, plus one 1.5-hour session on *Second Treatise* chs. 7–9, the path's last assigned chapters, taken from Week 7: 19.25 desk hours, 25.1 all-in, which is four working weeks and about two weeks' extension, everything the path and guide set, with Locke's politics straight after Hobbes's. Take Weeks 1, 5 and the rest of Week 7 (10.75 desk hours, 14.9 all-in) after Stage 3's gate as new Calendar B time, not buffer weeks, which path §10 keeps for illness, holidays, two named re-reading passes and media spill. Under (b), read the Epistle and E I.ii–iv at the start of Week 2 (about 45 minutes, the path's Book I summary pass with the guide's §1.1 and reconstruction (a)), since Argument 2 leans on Argument 1.

**Reading texts.** Follow [08-bibliography.md](08-bibliography.md), §1. The *Essay* in Nidditch (Clarendon, 1975), the edition the scholarship cites, or Winkler's Hackett abridgement if Nidditch stalls you; check Winkler's contents before Weeks 4–5, since the bibliography did not verify that it keeps III.vi, IV.iii, IV.xi or IV.xv–xix. The *Two Treatises* in Laslett (Cambridge Texts); the *Letter* in Goldie (Liberty Fund) or Tully (Hackett). The dossier quotes the free Project Gutenberg texts because they are public domain and checkable, not because you should read them at the desk. They are in largely modernized spelling, and this plan quotes them exactly as saved. Gutenberg leaves IV.xix ("Of Enthusiasm") empty: read it in Nidditch, in the 1823 *Works*, vol. III, or in the Wikisource 1853 printing (08, §1).

**Cite by book, chapter and section.** (E II.xxvii.9) is Book II, chapter xxvii, section 9, in Nidditch's numbering, which readers' editions share. The saved Gutenberg text numbers some chapters differently: II.xxvii runs two ahead from Nidditch's §9, II.xxi one ahead from about §43, and I.iv one ahead near the end (02, "A note on citation and editions"). If a section cited here does not match your free text, look one or two sections later. The *Second Treatise* is cited by section (ST §27), which runs continuously through the book, the *First Treatise* as FT §n, and the *Letter* by a locating phrase, since it has no standard numbers.

**The guide is your companion, by design.** Its chapter sections (§1.1–1.9), three reconstructions ((a) innate ideas, (b) primary and secondary qualities, (c) personal identity), fourteen checkpoint questions (§3, answers in §5) and eight misreadings (§4) are assigned by number in Weeks 1–5 and not duplicated; the checkpoints printed here are the dossier's own. The guide stops at the *Essay*, so it has nothing on Weeks 6–7; the dossier's own checkpoints carry those. Its six-session reading plan is folded into Weeks 1–4.

## Week 1 — Life; against innate ideas: the Epistle to the Reader; E I.i–iv (~4.4 hrs: 3 desk + 0.5 media + 0.9 cards)

**Primary.** About an hour and a half, the lightest week of text. The Epistle to the Reader: the self-portrait as an under-labourer clearing rubbish for the master-builders of the new science, the plain, historical method, and the edition history the later editions add. Then Book I as a summary pass, as the path asks: I.i (the purpose, to inquire into the original, certainty and extent of human knowledge, E I.i.2) at a steady pace, and I.ii twice, the week's center, from the universal-assent argument (E I.ii.1–5) through the reply that principles are known when reason matures (E I.ii.6–14). I.iii on practical principles and I.iv on ideas steadily, skimming the examples; the guide's §1.1 says the shape matters more than the detail. Read the Epistle and I.ii side by side: one tells you what the book is for, the other what it clears away first.

**Alongside.** [01-life-and-times.md](01-life-and-times.md) to "Lord Ashley and Exeter House, 1666–1672" at a steady pace, then "The meeting of 1671 that began the *Essay*," "Exile, 1683–1689," "Return and publication, 1689" and "Weighing the sources" closely, with the "Chronology" as a self-test at the end of the week; [02-works-and-texts.md](02-works-and-texts.md), "The forms Locke wrote in," "Origins and the drafts (1671–1688)," "The five editions," "The four books" and "A note on citation and editions"; [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md), "The shape of the system" and "The project of the Essay: the under-labourer and the limits of understanding," with the key terms "Under-labourer" and "Innate principles"; [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), the opening note and Argument 1; [05-conversations.md](05-conversations.md), "Aristotle and the Schoolmen" and "Descartes" (the rival account Book I removes), then "Leibniz and the *New Essays*" after Argument 1; [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md), "The *Essay*'s first fifteen years, 1690–1704" and "Myths about the life." The library entry on [John Locke](../../eras/03-early-modern/john-locke.md) as the map. In Session B, the SEP's "John Locke" §§1–2, the guide's §1.1 and reconstruction (a), and Woolhouse's biography.

**Media.** *Preview, before the reading:* [*Philosophize This!*](https://philosophizethis.libsyn.com/episode-036-john-locke-pt-2-the-blank-slate), ep. 36 (29 min 5 s), the dangers of dissent in Locke's day and nature versus nurture; West's title is the popular slogan, not a Lockean phrase (Week 2 settles it). *Optional, not counted:* *In Our Time*, ["The Glorious Revolution"](https://www.bbc.co.uk/programmes/p00547fk) (45 minutes), for the dating of 1688–89.

**Checkpoints (the dossier's own).**
1. *What is the under-labourer image, and what does the* Essay *propose to examine?* Locke's modest self-description: he clears ground and removes rubbish for the builders of the new science, Boyle, Sydenham and Newton. The aim is the original, certainty and extent of human knowledge and the grounds of belief (E I.i.2), a survey of the understanding's reach, not a system of nature. [03](03-system-and-concepts.md), "The project of the Essay"; [05](05-conversations.md), "Sydenham and Newton."
2. *Why does the argument from universal assent fail by Locke's lights, and why is the maturity reply no rescue?* If a principle were stamped on the mind it would be perceived by all who can understand its terms, yet children and the unlearned assent to none (E I.ii.5); if the innatist says it is known when reason matures, every truth reason can discover becomes innate and the word marks nothing. [04](04-arguments.md), Argument 1; guide checkpoints 1–2.
3. *Whom is Locke attacking, and is it a straw man?* Name Yolton's reply that innatism was widely held in seventeenth-century England, and the opposing charge that no serious philosopher held the crude version; then say where Leibniz's dispositional innatism stands. [04](04-arguments.md), Argument 1, "Where scholars disagree"; [05](05-conversations.md), "Leibniz and the *New Essays*."
4. *Why does Locke include practical principles, and what does that leave the later* Second Treatise *to explain?* If whole peoples break supposed moral rules without remorse, morality is no inborn stamp; yet the *Second Treatise* rests on a law of nature. How is that law known? That is tension (1), which returns in Week 6. [03](03-system-and-concepts.md), "Known internal tensions"; [04](04-arguments.md), Argument 1.
5. *Which parts of the life are well documented, and which rest on one source?* The meeting of 1671 and the date on the title page (published December 1689, dated 1690) against the stories the "Weighing the sources" section flags. [01](01-life-and-times.md), "Weighing the sources"; [06](06-reception-and-debates.md), "Myths about the life."

**Reconstructions.** Argument 1 in full, book closed, then checked against the guide's (a) and the dossier. It gets its +2-day touch in Session C.

**Cards.** +3: Argument 1; two concept cards, idea and the under-labourer. Import after the week's reading; if you use the shipped decks in [`study/cards/`](../../study/cards/README.md), suspend any Locke card these duplicate. Deck at 3.

## Week 2 — Ideas and qualities: E II.i–viii, II.xii; Molyneux's problem, II.ix.8 (~6.2 hrs: 4.5 desk + 0.8 media + 0.9 cards)

**Primary.** About two and a half hours. II.i whole, twice: the sentence about white paper (E II.i.2), the two sources, sensation and reflection (E II.i.3–5), and the note that the mind is furnished with ideas before it thinks about them. II.ii–vii at a steady pace for the simple ideas (II.ii.1–2: simple ideas are received passively and cannot be made or destroyed by the mind), skimming the catalogue chapters on the ideas of one sense, of more than one, and of reflection. II.viii closely, twice: ideas against qualities (E II.viii.8), the primary list and inseparability (E II.viii.9), the secondary qualities as powers (E II.viii.10; the Gutenberg text leaves that section empty, so use Nidditch), the argument from variation in the exhibits of the porphyry, the pounded almond and the warm and cold water (E II.viii.19–21), and the three sorts of qualities (E II.viii.23). II.ix.8, the Molyneux case, about twenty minutes. II.xii.1–6, the three acts of the mind that make complex ideas, combining, relating and abstracting, closely; the rest steadily.

**The guide.** Its §§1.2–1.4 and 1.5 for II.xii; reconstruction **(b)**, written book closed first; **checkpoints 3–7**; misreadings 1, 3 and 4.

**Alongside.** [02](02-works-and-texts.md), "The four books"; [03](03-system-and-concepts.md), "Ideas: the term, simple and complex, sensation and reflection," "Qualities and powers" and "Perception, retention, discerning and abstraction," with the key terms "Idea" through "Abstraction"; [04](04-arguments.md), Arguments 2–4; [05](05-conversations.md), "Gassendi and Boyle: the corpuscularians," "Limborch and Molyneux," "Berkeley" and "Condillac and the French Enlightenment"; [06](06-reception-and-debates.md), "Leibniz and the empiricist succession" and "The philosophers' quarrels: ideas, persons, essences," with the first two entries of "Misconceptions, in full form." In Session B, the SEP's ["Molyneux's Problem"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/molyneux-problem/), the Cambridge Companion by topic, and Uzgalis or Lowe's guidebook on II.i–viii.

**Media.** *Review, after the reading:* *In Our Time*, ["Empiricism"](https://www.bbc.co.uk/programmes/p004y28g) (priced at 45 minutes, with Judith Hawley, Murray Pittock and Jonathan Rée; the page opens with the passage from II.i.2, and the podcast file is shorter, about 28 minutes). *Optional, not counted:* ["Bishop Berkeley"](https://www.bbc.co.uk/programmes/b03y36vr) (47 minutes), for the reply to Argument 3.

**Checkpoints (book closed).**
1. *What is "white paper" claiming, and what is it not?* The mind starts with no ideas, not with no capacities: perceiving, retaining, comparing and abstracting are native equipment, and the answer to the question how the mind is furnished is experience, through sensation and reflection (E II.i.2–5). The phrase "white paper" is Locke's; the Latin *tabula rasa* does not occur in the saved text. [04](04-arguments.md), Argument 2; [06](06-reception-and-debates.md), "Misconceptions," first two entries; guide checkpoint 3.
2. *Why does the audit bind, and what do the three acts of II.xii add?* Simple ideas are received, never made, so experience measures our concepts; complex ideas are built from them by combining, relating and abstracting (E II.xii.1), and any word claiming an idea with no such history can be audited. Leibniz's reply from the intellect itself, and the answer that reflection yields no truths of its own. [04](04-arguments.md), Argument 2; guide checkpoint 4.
3. *Reconstruct the primary/secondary distinction, and say what it owes.* Qualities are powers; the primary ones (E II.viii.9) are inseparable from body however divided and are resembled by our ideas; colors, sounds and tastes vary with conditions that leave the body unchanged (E II.viii.19–21), so they are powers, grounded in the first kind, to produce ideas that do not resemble them. Owed: the corpuscular hypothesis. [04](04-arguments.md), Argument 3; guide (b) and checkpoints 5–7.
4. *State Berkeley's objection in its three stages, and the best reply.* The line cannot be held, since extension and figure vary with perceiver as color does; resemblance holds only between ideas; and nothing remains of matter. Reply: separate the argument from the illustrations, and the claim that primary ideas are exactly like their qualities from the weaker claim that they are the ideas by which structure is known. Read Berkeley at row 8 against your own answer. [04](04-arguments.md), Argument 3; [05](05-conversations.md), "Berkeley."
5. *What does Locke say of Molyneux's blind man, and what does the case test?* The man born blind and made to see could not tell, at first sight, which was the cube and which the globe, because sight and touch give different ideas and the link is learned (E II.ix.8). Name Leibniz's opposing "yes." Whether the answer fits the rest of the *Essay* is disputed. [04](04-arguments.md), Argument 4, "Where scholars disagree."

**Reconstructions.** Arguments 2 and 3 in full, and 4 in skeleton (premises without objection and reply); all three get their +2-day touches in Session C, and Argument 1 its +1-week touch.

**Cards.** +8: Arguments 2–4; sensation and reflection, simple and complex ideas, the three heads of complex ideas (modes, substances, relations), primary and secondary qualities with the resemblance thesis, qualities as powers and the three sorts of qualities. Deck at 11.

## Week 3 — Power, freedom, substance: E II.xxi, II.xxiii (~4.9 hrs: 4 desk + 0 media + 0.9 cards)

**Primary.** About two and a half hours. II.xxi, the longest chapter of the *Essay* and the one Locke rewrote, in two sittings. First §§1–29: power from change and volition (E II.xxi.1–7), liberty as belonging to the agent and not to the will (E II.xxi.14–21), with the locked room (E II.xxi.10), and the definition of freedom (E II.xxi.8). Then the second half, the rewrite: uneasiness as what determines the will (E II.xxi.31) and the power to suspend (E II.xxi.52), closely. Look at Nidditch's notes on what the first edition said, that the greater good determines the will, so that you see what Locke gave up. The free text runs one section ahead from about Nidditch's §43; look one later. II.xxiii at a steady pace for its first three sections (the idea of substance as supposed support, E II.xxiii.1–3) and closely for §2 and §15, where he calls substance in general "a supposed I know not what" (E II.xxiii.15, the Gutenberg wording).

**The guide.** Its §§1.5 (II.xxiii) and 1.6; **checkpoints 8–11**; the passage on II.xxi in the "Main argument" paragraph.

**Alongside.** [02](02-works-and-texts.md), "The five editions" (the 1694 rewrite); [03](03-system-and-concepts.md), "Power, will, liberty, uneasiness and suspension" and "Substance and the corpuscular hypothesis," with the key terms "Power" to "Suspension," "Mode" and "Substance (idea of)"; [04](04-arguments.md), Arguments 5–6; [05](05-conversations.md), "Hobbes," "Stillingfleet and Proast," "Leibniz and the *New Essays*" and "Norris and Astell"; [06](06-reception-and-debates.md), "Stillingfleet, Edwards and the deists," which belongs to the substance letters. Set the chapter beside Hobbes: [Argument 16 of the Hobbes dossier](../thomas-hobbes/04-arguments.md) and Lev. 21.4. In Session B, the SEP's ["Locke On Freedom"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/locke-freedom/) and, for substance, Jolley and Stuart (08, §3). *Optional, not counted:* Millican's Oxford *General Philosophy* lectures keyed to this stage, which the path lists inside its budget.

**Media.** None assigned.

**Checkpoints (the dossier's own).**
1. *Why is the question "whether the will be free" improper, and what does the locked room show?* Power belongs to agents, not faculties; a man who stays in a room he does not know is locked in stays voluntarily and is still not free (E II.xxi.10), so the voluntary is not the free. The same distinction answers Hobbes's water in its channel. [04](04-arguments.md), Argument 5; guide checkpoint 9.
2. *What did the second edition change?* The first edition had the will determined by the greater good; the second makes uneasiness the determiner (E II.xxi.31), which explains the drunkard who judges sobriety best and drinks, and then makes liberty a power to suspend the satisfaction of a desire while reason weighs it (E II.xxi.52). Say what the weakness-of-will evidence does in the argument. [04](04-arguments.md), Argument 5; [02](02-works-and-texts.md), "The five editions"; guide checkpoints 10–11.
3. *State the dilemma against suspension, and the reply.* Is the act of suspending itself determined, or is it undetermined and so unexplained? Locke's text takes the first horn and says that is no loss, since freedom is a power to act on one's own judgment; the literature disagrees about whether suspension is a distinct freedom or only an instance of freedom of action. Name the sides in the SEP entry. [04](04-arguments.md), Argument 5, "Where scholars disagree."
4. *Tell the story of the Indian philosopher in II.xxiii.2, and say what the chapter claims and withholds.* The world sits on an elephant, the elephant on a tortoise, and the tortoise on something; we suppose a support for qualities because we cannot conceive them floating free, yet we have no clear idea of it. Locke does not deny substance; he denies a clear idea of it. [04](04-arguments.md), Argument 6; guide checkpoint 8.
5. *Stillingfleet's charge, Locke's reply, and the Ayers/Jolley dispute.* The bishop thought the account threatened the Trinity by discarding substance; Locke replied that he had not denied it. Ayers reads substance in general as the unknown real essence, and Jolley and the SEP criticize that for the text. Why is II.xxiii the loose thread Berkeley and Hume pull? [04](04-arguments.md), Argument 6; [05](05-conversations.md), "Stillingfleet and Proast."

**Reconstructions.** Argument 5 in full and Argument 6 in full, as the guide's II.xxiii and II.xxi sections allow, each checked against the dossier. Their +2-day touches in Session C; Arguments 2–4 their +1-week touches.

**Cards.** +4: Arguments 5–6; uneasiness and suspension, the idea of substance. Deck at 15.

## Week 4 — Personal identity; words and essences: E II.xxvii; III.ii–iii, III.vi (~5.4 hrs: 4.5 desk + 0 media + 0.9 cards)

**Primary.** About two and a half hours. II.xxvii whole, twice, the path's third named target and the chapter Locke added in 1694: the opening on identity relative to a kind (the oak, the animal, the man), the definition of a person (E II.xxvii.9), consciousness as what makes the same person (E II.xxvii.13–16, with the prince and the cobbler), Socrates waking and sleeping (E II.xxvii.19–20), and "person" as a forensic term (E II.xxvii.26). The free text runs two sections ahead of Nidditch from §9, so §9 is Gutenberg's §11. Then Book III: III.ii (words as signs of ideas, E III.ii.1–2) closely, III.iii (general terms and abstraction; real and nominal essence, E III.iii.15) closely, and III.vi (the names of substances; species as the workmanship of the understanding) at a steady pace for its first nine sections and by the guide's outline after that (§1.8).

**The guide.** Its §1.7 and §1.8; reconstruction **(c)**, written book closed first; **checkpoints 12–14**; misreadings 5 and 6.

**Alongside.** [03](03-system-and-concepts.md), "Identity: bodies, organisms, men and persons," "Language: words as signs of ideas, and the abuse of words" and "Essences and kinds," with the key terms "Person" to "Nominal essence"; [04](04-arguments.md), Arguments 7–9; [05](05-conversations.md), "Butler, Reid and personal identity," "Personal identity: Parfit and his rivals," "Natural kinds: Kripke and Putnam," "Aristotle and the Schoolmen" (the essences Locke deflates) and "Berkeley"; [06](06-reception-and-debates.md), "The philosophers' quarrels: ideas, persons, essences." In Session B, the SEP's ["Locke on Personal Identity"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/locke-personal-identity/), read after the chapter, with Winkler's article first (08, §3) and Gordon-Roth's entry for the substance, mode and appropriation readings; the general ["Personal Identity"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/identity-personal/) after Argument 7; Stuart on essences.

**Media.** *Watch-before, inside desk hours:* Wi-Phi's [*Locke on Personal Identity*](https://www.wi-phi.com/series/locke-on-personal-identity/) (two short episodes by Michael Della Rocca; no lengths are stated). *Optional, not counted:* Yale, PHIL 176, [Lectures 10](https://oyc.yale.edu/philosophy/phil-176/lecture-10) and [11](https://oyc.yale.edu/philosophy/phil-176/lecture-11) (Kagan; 50-minute sessions) on the soul and body theories and the personality theory.

**Checkpoints (book closed).**
1. *Distinguish sameness of substance, of man and of person, and say which identity of person consists in.* The same immaterial or material stuff; the same living organism; the same thinking being that can consider itself as itself at different times, whose identity is consciousness (E II.xxvii.9). Why "relative to a kind" opens the chapter. [04](04-arguments.md), Argument 7; guide checkpoint 12.
2. *Work the prince and cobbler, and the Socrates case.* The prince's consciousness in the cobbler's body is the prince; the same soul without the consciousness would not make the same person, and Socrates awake and Socrates asleep are not the same person if the waking one cannot reach the sleeping one's thoughts. What do they show about body and soul as criteria? [04](04-arguments.md), Argument 7; guide checkpoint 13.
3. *Why is "person" a forensic term, and how do Butler and Reid object?* It appropriates actions and merit (E II.xxvii.26). Butler: consciousness presupposes identity, so the account is circular; Reid: the brave officer remembers being flogged as a boy, and the general remembers taking the standard as an officer but not the flogging, so on a memory criterion the general is the officer, the officer is the boy, and the general is not the boy, which breaks the transitivity of identity. Say how the continuity repair works and who developed it. [04](04-arguments.md), Argument 7; guide checkpoint 14; [05](05-conversations.md), "Butler, Reid and personal identity."
4. *Words and abstraction.* Words stand for ideas in the speaker's mind (E III.ii.1–2); general words stand for ideas made general by leaving out circumstances. Berkeley's reply from the triangle passage; Ayers on whether Locke was an imagist. Why does a private-idea theory of meaning make the *abuse of words* a danger? [04](04-arguments.md), Argument 8, "Where scholars disagree."
5. *Real and nominal essence, and the dispute over what Locke denies.* The real essence is the unknown constitution on which the observable properties depend; the nominal essence is the abstract idea that fixes a sortal; kinds are the work of the understanding. Ayers and Uzgalis read this as denying natural kinds in the Aristotelian sense; Lovejoy and Wiggins disagree; Kripke and Putnam answer the nominal-essence theory of reference. [04](04-arguments.md), Argument 9; [05](05-conversations.md), "Natural kinds."

## Week 5 — Knowledge and its limits; faith and enthusiasm: E IV.i–iv, IV.xi, IV.xv–xix (~5.9 hrs: 5 desk + 0 media + 0.9 cards)

**Primary.** About three hours, the heaviest *Essay* reading, and beyond the path's assignment (the guide's §1.9 only outlines Book IV). IV.i.1–7 whole: knowledge as the perception of the agreement or disagreement of ideas (E IV.i.2) and its four sorts (E IV.i.3). IV.ii on degrees, closely: intuition, demonstration, and sensitive knowledge (E IV.ii.14). IV.iii on its extent (E IV.iii.6, whether matter could think; E IV.iii.18, morality as demonstrable), at a steady pace. IV.xi on the existence of particular things, closely, with the argument from the involuntariness and the pleasure and pain of sensation, and the dream objection. Then IV.xv–xvi (probability and degrees of assent) at a steady pace, IV.xvii (reason) lightly, IV.xviii (faith and reason) closely, and IV.xix (enthusiasm) closely, from Nidditch, the 1823 *Works* or the Wikisource 1853 printing, as the Gutenberg text leaves it empty; the passage on the telescope, which Locke uses to say that to put out the light of reason to make way for revelation is to put out both (E IV.xix.4), is the one to find.

**The guide.** Its §1.9 (Book IV in outline); misreading 7.

**Alongside.** [02](02-works-and-texts.md), "The four books" and the *Reasonableness of Christianity* and Stillingfleet entries; [03](03-system-and-concepts.md), "Knowledge, its degrees and extent; sensitive knowledge; probability and assent" and "Faith, reason and enthusiasm," with the key terms "Knowledge" to "Enthusiasm"; [04](04-arguments.md), Arguments 10–12; [05](05-conversations.md), "Hooker," "Damaris Masham," "Hume," "Kant" and "Shaftesbury, the third Earl, and the moral sense"; [06](06-reception-and-debates.md), "Stillingfleet, Edwards and the deists" and "Britain in the eighteenth century." In Session B, the IEP's ["Locke: Knowledge of the External World"](https://iep.utm.edu/locke-kn/) for Argument 11, the SEP's ["Locke's Philosophy of Science"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/locke-philosophy-science/) and ["Locke's Moral Philosophy"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/locke-moral/). *Optional, not counted:* *The Reasonableness of Christianity* in Higgins-Biddle.

**Media.** None assigned.

**Checkpoints (the dossier's own).**
1. *Why is knowledge narrower than our ideas?* Knowledge is the perception of agreement or disagreement of ideas (E IV.i.2); we have ideas of things whose connections we cannot perceive (the real essence of substances), so knowledge falls short of ideas, and ideas of things. Name the four sorts of agreement. Where does the stage's later problem of induction land on this? [04](04-arguments.md), Argument 10; [03](03-system-and-concepts.md), "Extent."
2. *Intuition, demonstration, sensitive knowledge: say what each is and how certain.* Immediate perception of agreement; agreement perceived by a chain of intuitions; and assurance of particular existing things affecting the senses (E IV.ii.14), which Locke concedes is less than the others. Why does he still call it knowledge? [04](04-arguments.md), Arguments 10–11.
3. *Give Locke's case that we know external things, the dream objection, and the retort.* Sensation is involuntary, and pain and pleasure are felt in earnest; a man dreaming of a fire is not burned as one in the fire is (E IV.xi). The skeptic replies that the argument rests on what is in question; the dispute is whether Locke offers knowledge or high probability. Berkeley's reply (row 8). [04](04-arguments.md), Argument 11; IEP, "Locke: Knowledge of the External World."
4. *State the proportion of assent to evidence, and the rule on revelation.* Assent should match the grounds of probability (E IV.xv–xvi); faith is assent to God's revelation, but reason must judge whether it is revelation, and nothing contrary to clear reason can be (E IV.xviii). Why is that not hostility to religion? Name the *Reasonableness*. [04](04-arguments.md), Argument 12; [05](05-conversations.md), "Damaris Masham."
5. *What is enthusiasm, and what is wrong with it?* A counterfeit ground of assent: a persuasion of inspiration offered in place of reason (E IV.xix), tested by asking what evidence connects the persuasion to God, since the strength of a persuasion is no evidence of its source. Who is the target, and how does it prepare toleration in Week 7? [04](04-arguments.md), Argument 12; [06](06-reception-and-debates.md), live debate (6), read ahead.

**Reconstructions.** Arguments 10 and 11 as one chain in full; Argument 12 in skeleton, with its objection and reply filled in at the +2-day touch. Their +2-day touches in Session C; Argument 7 and Arguments 8–9 their +1-week touches; Arguments 2–4 their +3-week touch, done as one account of how ideas are acquired and what they reach, which this week's Book IV is the end of.

**Cards.** +6: Arguments 10–12; the three degrees of knowledge, probability and the regulation of assent, enthusiasm. Deck at 27.

## Week 6 — State of nature and property: *Second Treatise* chs. 1–5; the *First Treatise* in selection (~6.3 hrs: 4 desk + 1.4 media + 0.9 cards)

**Primary.** About two and a half hours, in Laslett, whose introduction (the Exclusion Crisis dating) goes first. ST ch. 1 and the Preface quickly. Ch. 2, the week's center, twice: the state of nature as a state of perfect freedom and equality within the law of nature (ST §4), the law of reason that binds all and forbids harm to another's life, health, liberty or possessions, resting on our being God's workmanship (ST §6), and each person's executive power to punish its breach (ST §7). **Checkpoints (the dossier's own).**
1. *State Locke's state of nature, and say how it differs from Hobbes's.* Men live together under the law of nature with no common judge (ST §§4–7), and the state of war is a separate condition, force without right (ST §19), where Hobbes makes the two one. Give the reason Locke thinks the state of nature has inconveniences that government cures, and the weakness in saying so. [04](04-arguments.md), Argument 13; Hobbes's Argument 6.
2. *What does the* First Treatise *do?* It attacks Filmer's patriarchalism, the claim that Adam's fatherhood gave him absolute sovereignty which kings inherit; it denies that Scripture gives that title, and that any heir could be identified. The *Second Treatise* names Filmer on almost every page; it was not written to answer Hobbes. [04](04-arguments.md), Argument 13; [06](06-reception-and-debates.md), "Misconceptions," last entry.
3. *How is the law of nature known, and what does it rest on?* By reason, and by our being God's workmanship (ST §6). Strauss makes self-preservation the primary right and Locke a Hobbesian in disguise; Dunn, Tully and Ashcraft make natural-law duties primary; Simmons holds both basic; Waldron argues that the theology gives equality its basis. Name tensions (1) and (2). [04](04-arguments.md), Argument 13; [06](06-reception-and-debates.md), live debate (2).
4. *Reconstruct property by labor and its two provisos, with the money step.* Each owns his person; labor joins to a thing what the laborer owns, so the thing is his (ST §27), provided enough, and as good, is left in common for others (ST §27) and nothing spoils (ST §31); money, a lasting store of value agreed by consent, removes the second limit (ST §§46–50). Then the three readings: Macpherson's capitalist charter, Tully's constrained right, Waldron's reading of the first proviso as descriptive. [04](04-arguments.md), Argument 14; [06](06-reception-and-debates.md), live debate (3).
5. *What is the question of colonialism, and what does the text allow?* Tully, Arneil and Armitage read ch. 5 as shaped by, and serving, English claims on the American land; Locke served the Lords Proprietors of Carolina. Where do the evidence and the arguments diverge? [06](06-reception-and-debates.md), "The colonial and slavery questions"; [01](01-life-and-times.md), "Carolina."

**Checkpoints (book closed).**
1. *Distinguish express and tacit consent, and give Simmons's objection.* Consent by declaration against consent by residence or use (ST §119); if tacit consent is that thin it cannot distinguish free submission from subjection. Simmons concludes that Locke's argument leads toward philosophical anarchism; Pitkin reads it differently. That is tension (3). [04](04-arguments.md), Argument 15; [05](05-conversations.md), "Hume."
2. *Why do people leave the state of nature, and what limits the legislative?* To preserve their lives, liberties and estates, by a settled law, a known judge and power to execute it (ST §§123–126); the legislative is supreme but a fiduciary power, bound by the law of nature, unable to take property without consent or to transfer its power (ST §§134–142, 149). Contrast Hobbes's undivided sovereign, and Locke's remark on the polecats and the lion (ST §93). [04](04-arguments.md), Argument 15; [05](05-conversations.md), "Hobbes."
3. *When is government dissolved, and who judges?* When the legislative or the executive breaks its trust and seeks "the lives, liberties, and estates of the people" (ST §222); the people are then released and may act, there being no judge on earth, with an appeal to heaven. Why is the right of resistance not a license for rebellion, and what did the 1680s have to do with its form? Ashcraft's reading against Dunn's. [04](04-arguments.md), Argument 16; [01](01-life-and-times.md), "The Exclusion Crisis."
4. *Reconstruct the argument for toleration, and the limits Locke sets.* The magistrate's care is the civil interests; the care of souls was not committed to him; belief cannot be compelled by force; a church is a voluntary society. Limits: no toleration for those who deny the bonds of human society, nor for those who owe allegiance to a foreign power. [04](04-arguments.md), Argument 16; [06](06-reception-and-debates.md), "Misconceptions" on toleration.
5. *Give Proast's objection and the reply.* Force cannot change belief directly but may bring a man to attend to reasons; Locke's replies in the later *Letters*, and Waldron's modern restatement, that the argument proves too little. Where does the *Essay*'s account of belief (Week 5) enter? [04](04-arguments.md), Argument 16, "Where scholars disagree"; [05](05-conversations.md), "Stillingfleet and Proast."

## Capstone essay (800–1,200 words)

Write in Week 7, after the checkpoints, book closed for the first draft. **The gate relationship, stated exactly:** Stage 3's gate essay is Hume's problem of induction. One of the gate's exit checkpoints is Locke's, "State Locke on primary/secondary qualities and on personal identity, and Berkeley's 'master argument' reply to material substance," and Weeks 2 and 4 prepare it directly (Arguments 3 and 7, with Berkeley's objections to 3 in Week 2). This essay is the dossier's own consolidation, not the gate and not a substitute for it. Each prompt draws on at least three weeks.

1. **Is Locke's empiricism consistent with his politics?** Reconstruct Arguments 1, 2 and 13. If every idea comes from experience and the *Essay* denies innate practical principles (Week 1), how can the law of nature be known (Week 6), and does the answer lean on God's workmanship? Set the Strauss reading against Dunn and Tully, and say what tension (1) costs.
2. **Locke against Hobbes: is the state of nature the difference that matters?** Reconstruct Arguments 13, 15 and 16 with Hobbes's Arguments 6 and 11–12 from the [Hobbes dossier](../thomas-hobbes/04-arguments.md). Use the polecats and the lion (ST §93) and Hobbes's reply from civil war, and weigh the trust against authorization, with Argument 1 for the law of nature's source (Weeks 1, 6, 7).
3. **Does Locke's account of ideas survive Berkeley?** Reconstruct Arguments 2, 3, 6 and 8, then 11. Test the primary/secondary line, the supposed support and the abstract idea against Berkeley's three objections, and say which of Locke's replies is his and which the literature's (Weeks 2–5).
4. **Self-ownership and the person: how does the forensic person ground property?** Reconstruct Arguments 7, 14 and 15. Locke's property in one's own person (ST §27) and the person as the unit of accountability (E II.xxvii.26): is the link a debt or a coincidence? Use the provisos, Waldron's reading, and tension (4) (Weeks 4, 6, 7).
5. **Reason, revelation and toleration: does the argument for toleration need the* Essay*?** Reconstruct Arguments 12 and 16 with Argument 1's treatment of assent. Set Proast's objection against the reply, and Waldron's restatement, and say whether toleration for those who deny the bonds of society and for those who owe allegiance to a foreign power follows from the same logic (Weeks 1, 5, 7).
