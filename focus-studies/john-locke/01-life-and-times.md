# Life and Times: Wrington, Oxford, Exeter House, Montpellier, Rotterdam, Oates
**English** | [简体中文](../../zh/focus-studies/john-locke/01-life-and-times.md)


John Locke lived from 1632 to 1704, and his life runs alongside the English century of revolution. He was ten when the Civil War began and a schoolboy a short walk from Whitehall when Charles I was executed in 1649; he was a young Oxford don at the Restoration of 1660; he spent the 1670s and early 1680s at the elbow of the most dangerous politician in England, the first Earl of Shaftesbury; he fled to Holland in 1683 and came home with the Revolution in February 1689; and in the fifteen years left to him he became the leading philosopher in Europe and a working official of the new regime. He came to print late. Apart from occasional verses and short pieces, nothing of his appeared until he was fifty-seven, and then, within a few months of 1689–90, came the *Essay concerning Human Understanding*, the *Two Treatises of Government* and *A Letter concerning Toleration*. Two of the three were anonymous.

That reticence shapes the record. Unlike [Hobbes](../thomas-hobbes/01-life-and-times.md), Locke wrote no autobiography; what he said about himself is scattered through letters, journals and prefaces, above all the Epistle to the Reader of the *Essay*. The first life was the *Éloge* by his friend Jean Le Clerc (1705), built largely on letters from Lady Masham, in whose house he died, and from his former pupil the third Earl of Shaftesbury. Leslie Stephen's article in the *Dictionary of National Biography* (1893), public domain and cited here as the DNB, draws on Le Clerc, on Lord King's *Life* (1829) and on H. R. Fox Bourne's *Life of John Locke* (1876), the first to work through the Shaftesbury papers. The standard modern lives, both cited by the SEP, are Maurice Cranston's *John Locke: A Biography* (1957) and Roger Woolhouse's *Locke: A Biography* (2007). This file checks its dates against the DNB, the SEP articles "John Locke" and "Locke's Political Philosophy", and the IEP article "John Locke: Political Philosophy", and says so where they disagree.

Quotations from Locke keep the spelling of the saved public-domain texts: the *Essay* from Project Gutenberg #10615, a modern-spelling transcription that includes the prefatory notes down to the sixth edition and does not name the printing it follows; the Preface to the *Two Treatises* from Project Gutenberg #7370; and the *Letter* in William Popple's English translation of 1689, from Wikisource. The DNB is quoted from its Wikisource transcription.

## Wrington and Pensford, 1632–1647

Locke was born on 29 August 1632 at Wrington in north Somerset, about ten miles from Bristol, in the house of his mother's brother (the DNB). The family home was Beluton, near Pensford in the parish of Publow, left to his father by the grandfather, Nicholas Locke, a clothier. The father, also John (1606–1661), was an attorney, clerk to the county justices, and agent to one of them, Alexander Popham. Of the mother, Agnes Keene, little is known; her son called her, in the DNB's quotation, "a very pious woman and affectionate mother". There was one brother, Thomas, born in 1637. The SEP describes the parents as Puritans "of modest means". The father was stern while the boy was small and relaxed as he grew; the DNB, from Lady Masham, says that he came to live "perfectly with him as a friend", and once solemnly asked his son's pardon for having struck him as a child.

When the Civil War broke out in 1642, Popham raised a regiment of horse for Parliament and the elder Locke became one of his captains. The regiment served around Bristol and Exeter and was apparently broken up after Waller's defeat at Roundway Down on 13 July 1643 (the DNB). The father went back to the law poorer than before. What the war gave the son was a patron: Popham sat for Bath in the Long Parliament from 1645, and his interest opened the way to the best school in England.

## Westminster School, 1647–1652

Locke went to Westminster, probably in 1646, and was placed on the foundation as a King's Scholar in 1647 (the DNB; the SEP gives 1647). The school was governed by a parliamentary committee, but its headmaster was Richard Busby, and its boys included John Dryden and Robert South, the future court poet and the future high-church preacher (the DNB). Westminster is a short walk from Whitehall, where Charles I was beheaded on 30 January 1649. The school tradition that the boys were kept indoors that day is a tradition only; nothing Locke wrote mentions the scene. But these were the years of the regicide, the abolition of monarchy and the Lords, and the Commonwealth, and his first political writings, ten years later, would be marked by a horror of the disorder that religious zeal had brought.

## Christ Church, Oxford, and the Restoration, 1652–1662

At Whitsuntide 1652 Locke was elected to a studentship at Christ Church, Oxford, and he matriculated on 27 November (the DNB; the SEP puts his arrival in the autumn of 1652, at twenty). The SEP calls Christ Church the most important Oxford college, as Westminster was the most important school; its dean, and the university's vice-chancellor, was John Owen. Locke took his BA on 14 February 1656 and his MA on 29 June 1658, and became a senior student, the Christ Church equivalent of a fellow, though not a permanent one (the DNB and SEP agree).

Like [Hobbes](../thomas-hobbes/01-life-and-times.md) at Magdalen Hall half a century earlier, he found the scholastic Aristotle of the schools of little use. The DNB reports, from Spence's *Anecdotes*, that his aversion to disputation led him to spend much of his first years reading romances, and Lady Masham heard that he was not a "very hard student" and preferred the company of pleasant and witty men; she also said that his first relish for philosophy came from Descartes. The SEP adds that he read Boyle before Descartes, and saw Descartes as an alternative to the sterile Aristotelianism of Oxford while remaining critical of his rationalism.

Oxford in the 1650s had more than Aristotle. Around John Wilkins, Warden of Wadham and Cromwell's brother-in-law, met the experimental philosophers who would form the Royal Society after the Restoration. Locke attended Wallis on geometry and Seth Ward on astronomy, the two mathematicians then demolishing Hobbes's geometry (the DNB), and a Westminster friend, Richard Lower, introduced him to medicine and to the work at Wadham (the SEP). Robert Boyle settled in Oxford in 1654, and Locke formed with him a lifelong friendship. The SEP calls Boyle Locke's scientific mentor and says that Locke took the language of primary and secondary qualities from Boyle's *Origin of Forms and Qualities*; [the arguments file](04-arguments.md) reconstructs the distinction as Argument 3.

His politics in these years were cautious. He wrote verses for the university's collection on Cromwell's peace with the Dutch in 1654 and verses welcoming Charles II in 1660; the IEP reads the pair as prudence. The DNB judges that he had become alienated from Puritan dogmatism and "heartily welcomed" the Restoration, hoping that a return to constitutional government would favor political and religious freedom. His father died on 13 February 1661 and his brother soon after, leaving him the Somerset property and a small independent income.

## The *Two Tracts* and the *Essays on the Law of Nature*, 1660–1664

The Restoration brought Locke college office: lecturer in Greek from Christmas 1660, lecturer in rhetoric from Christmas 1662, censor of moral philosophy from Christmas 1663, each for the following year (the DNB; the SEP gives 1663 for rhetoric, counting the year of service). It also brought his first political writing. The *Two Tracts on Government*, the first in English (1660) and the second in Latin (about 1662), answer Edward Bagshaw, a fellow student of Christ Church, who had argued against the imposition of religious uniformity (the IEP). The young Locke took the side of authority: the magistrate may lawfully impose the *indifferent things* of worship, because the alternative is the religious rage that had just torn the country apart. The position is close to Hobbes's, though Locke does not cite him (compare the [Hobbes system file](../thomas-hobbes/03-system-and-concepts.md) on the sovereign and public worship), and the IEP notes how conservative it looks to a reader who comes from the *Two Treatises*: its author supports Charles II's Act of Uniformity of 1662. Neither tract was printed in his lifetime; Philip Abrams edited them in 1967 (the SEP's bibliography).

The *Essays on the Law of Nature* are Locke's Latin lectures as censor of moral philosophy, 1663–64 (the IEP). They argue that there is a law of nature, the decree of a superior will, binding on all and knowable by sense and reason, not by inscription on the mind or by tradition. The argument against inscription is an early form of the attack on innate principles that opens the *Essay*. The *Essays* were unpublished in his lifetime (the SEP, "Locke's Political Philosophy"), and scholars disagree about how much of them survives in the mature Locke. Leo Strauss and Peter Laslett, the SEP reports, both found the later natural-law theory full of contradictions, Strauss inferring that Locke did not really believe in natural law and Laslett advising that the philosopher and the political writer be kept apart; Yolton, Simmons, Grant and others read it as a consistent foundation. [The reception file](06-reception-and-debates.md) takes up the debate.

## Cleves, medicine, Boyle and Sydenham, 1665–1667

By the mid-1660s Locke had to choose a career. Fifty-five of Christ Church's sixty senior studentships were reserved for men in orders or preparing for them; only five could be held by laymen, two in medicine, two in law and one in moral philosophy (the SEP). A letter of 1666 shows him refusing advantageous offers of church preferment, doubting his fitness and unwilling to take an irrevocable step (the DNB, from King). Public service tempted him first: at the end of 1665 he went as secretary to Sir Walter Vane's mission to the Elector of Brandenburg at Cleves, and wrote home amused letters about the drinking and the scholastic disputations of the natives (the DNB). Offered a post on an embassy to Spain, he declined, and went back to Oxford, to medicine and to Boyle. In November 1666 a dispensation allowed him to keep his studentship without taking orders, and on 23 November 1668 he was elected a fellow of the Royal Society (the DNB).

He practiced medicine with an old college friend, David Thomas; their Oxford laboratory was, the SEP says, very likely a pharmacy in effect. He never took the doctorate. A request from Clarendon as Chancellor in 1666 that he be allowed the MB and MD was not obeyed, a second attempt in 1670 was withdrawn, and he took the MB only in 1674, moving in January 1675 to one of the two medical studentships (the DNB, which suspects the prejudice of Oxford's high churchmen).

The great medical friendship was with Thomas Sydenham, the London physician who, in the SEP's words, championed careful observation of disease and rejected appeal to underlying causes. Locke went with him on visits to patients, wrote a Latin poem for the second edition of his book on fevers (1668), and began a medical treatise of his own, a fragment of which (1669) attacks the futility of scholastic disputes in medicine (the DNB). Twenty years later, in the Epistle to the Reader, he set Sydenham beside Boyle:

> The commonwealth of learning is not at this time without master-builders, whose mighty designs, in advancing the sciences, will leave lasting monuments to the admiration of posterity: but every one must not hope to be a Boyle or a Sydenham; and in an age that produces such masters as the great Huygenius and the incomparable Mr. Newton, with some others of that strain, it is ambition enough to be employed as an under-labourer in clearing the ground a little, and removing some of the rubbish that lies in the way to knowledge

— *Essay*, Epistle to the Reader

The SEP notes that the under-labourer's clearing is also a demolition, of the scholastic and in part the Cartesian philosophy. The "historical, plain method" announced in the Introduction (E I.i.2) is close to a physician's: describe how ideas actually arise before theorizing about what lies behind them.

## Lord Ashley and Exeter House, 1666–1672

In July 1666 Anthony Ashley Cooper, Lord Ashley, one of the richest men in England (the SEP), came to Oxford to visit his son and drink medicinal waters, which he had asked Dr. Thomas to supply. Thomas was away, and Locke saw to the delivery (the SEP and DNB). The two men took to each other at once, and by the summer of 1667 Locke was living at Exeter House, Ashley's mansion in the Strand (the DNB). Ashley had supported Cromwell until the Protectorate turned military, had sat on the commission that invited Charles II home, and served the king from 1660 to 1673 (the IEP); he favored toleration for Protestant dissenters and trade and colonies as the source of national wealth (the SEP). In the SEP's summary, Locke became his physician, secretary, researcher, political operative and friend, at the heart of English politics.

The change in Locke's politics was quick. His unpublished *Essay concerning Toleration* (1667) already argues that the magistrate's business is the peace of the commonwealth, and that opinions and worship that do not threaten it should be left alone; the DNB's summary is that all religions are to be tolerated except atheism, which struck at morality, and Catholicism, which claimed powers inconsistent with the state's supremacy. Locke held that view for the rest of his life.

His first service was medical. In 1668 Ashley's internal abscess became dangerous; Locke organized an operation and kept the wound open afterwards with a silver tube, which the satirists of the day often mentioned (the DNB). The SEP calls it perhaps the most carefully documented operation of the century and says that by saving his patron's life Locke changed English history. He negotiated the marriage of Ashley's son in 1669 and attended the birth of the grandson, the future third earl, in February 1671 (the DNB), whose education he later supervised.

## The meeting of 1671 that began the *Essay*

It was at Exeter House, probably in 1671, that the *Essay* began. Locke tells the story in the Epistle to the Reader:

> Were it fit to trouble thee with the history of this Essay, I should tell thee, that five or six friends meeting at my chamber, and discoursing on a subject very remote from this, found themselves quickly at a stand, by the difficulties that rose on every side. After we had awhile puzzled ourselves, without coming any nearer a resolution of those doubts which perplexed us, it came into my thoughts that we took a wrong course; and that before we set ourselves upon inquiries of that nature, it was necessary to examine our own abilities, and see what OBJECTS our understandings were, or were not, fitted to deal with.

— *Essay*, Epistle to the Reader (the capitals are the Gutenberg transcription's)

Locke gives no date. The date of about 1671 comes from Lady Masham (the DNB) and fits the manuscripts: as the [study guide](../../study/locke-essay.md) notes, a first draft dates from 1671. Nor does Locke name the "subject very remote from this". James Tyrrell, one of the friends present, recalled that it was the principles of morality and revealed religion (the SEP, citing Cranston 1957: 140–1). If Tyrrell is right, the *Essay* began as a preliminary to ethics and theology: before arguing about what God requires of us, find out what we can know. That is why Week 1 of this dossier begins with the Epistle, and why the chapters on faith, reason and enthusiasm that close Book IV (Week 5) bring the book back to where the friends started. The Epistle adds that the first "hasty and undigested thoughts" were continued "by intreaty", written "by incoherent parcels", and at last, "in a retirement where an attendance on my health gave me leisure, it was brought into that order thou now seest it". The retirement was Holland, and the book took eighteen years.

## Carolina and the Council of Trade, 1669–1675

Ashley was one of the Lords Proprietors of Carolina, and Locke served as their secretary from 1669; the DNB has him much occupied by the business until the autumn of 1672. The *Fundamental Constitutions of Carolina* survive in a draft dated 21 June 1669 in Locke's hand (the DNB). The scheme is aristocratic and permits slavery, giving every freeman "absolute power and authority over his negro slaves" (as the SEP quotes it); it also allows any seven persons who profess belief in God and public worship to form a church, a clause the DNB reads as Locke's own. How much else is his is disputed. David Armitage (2004) argued that Locke was revising the *Constitutions* while writing the *Second Treatise* and left the slavery clause unchanged; Thomas Wilson, L. H. Roper and James Farr give other accounts of the colony and of whom Locke's theory of slavery was aimed at (all in the SEP). Woolhouse's verdict, as the SEP quotes it, is that there "certainly seems to be 'a glaring contradiction between his theories and Afro-American slavery'". [The reception file](06-reception-and-debates.md) carries the debate.

In 1672 Ashley became Earl of Shaftesbury (April) and Lord Chancellor (November), and Locke his secretary of presentations, handling church livings. On 15 October 1673 he became secretary to the Council of Trade and Plantations, at a salary that was never paid (the DNB). Shaftesbury was dismissed at the end of 1673 and went into opposition, and the council was dissolved on 12 March 1675. Shaftesbury settled an annuity of £100 on Locke at the end of 1674 (the DNB).

## France, 1675–1679

Locke's health was poor, his lungs especially (in later life he was asthmatic and could bear London's smoke only in the warm months, the SEP says). In November 1675 he set out for the south of France, then a resort of invalids. He left London on 15 November and reached Montpellier on Christmas Day, by Paris, Lyons and Avignon; he stayed until March 1677, then went on to Paris, arriving on 23 May (the DNB). There he took charge of the son of one of Shaftesbury's City friends (the DNB transcription names the father as Sir John Hanks; modern lives spell it Banks). An attempt to reach Rome in 1678 turned back at Montpellier, and he stayed in Paris until April 1679, when Shaftesbury called him home.

His journals record prices and wages, the condition of laborers, the Estates of Languedoc, vines and olives, and the Protestant churches (the DNB); the SEP stresses his study of French Protestantism, still protected by the Edict of Nantes, which Louis XIV would revoke in 1685. In Paris he formed a lasting friendship with the scholar Nicolas Thoynard, and at Montpellier he met Thomas Herbert, later Earl of Pembroke, to whom he would dedicate the *Essay* (the DNB). He kept writing: in 1679 he told Thoynard the *Essay* was complete, which the DNB thinks premature.

## The Exclusion Crisis and the *Two Treatises*, 1679–1683

While Locke was abroad, Shaftesbury spent a year in the Tower (from February 1677 by the DNB; 1676 by the SEP). In 1678 Titus Oates's tale of a Catholic plot to murder the king and crown his brother James set off a national panic. Shaftesbury did not invent the plot but exploited it, building a party network and a Commons majority for a bill to exclude the Catholic James from the succession (the SEP). The bill passed the Commons and failed in the Lords; the parties formed in the struggle, Whigs and Tories, were in the IEP's words the first two political parties of modern times. Charles dissolved the Parliament that met at Oxford in March 1681 and ruled without one thereafter. Shaftesbury was arrested in July 1681 (on the 2nd, by the DNB; the 21st, by the SEP) and charged with treason, but a London grand jury threw out the bill in November.

Locke spent much of 1679–80 at Thanet House, Shaftesbury's London residence, took lodgings for him at Oxford for the Parliament of 1681, and afterwards lived mostly at Christ Church (the DNB). He was also writing the *Two Treatises*. The occasion was Sir Robert Filmer's *Patriarcha*, a defense of the divine right and absolute power of kings as heirs of Adam, written decades earlier and published in 1680 at the height of the crisis (the IEP). The *First Treatise* refutes it; the *Second* gives Locke's own account of the state of nature, property, consent, trust and resistance.

For two and a half centuries the book was read as what its Preface of 1689 said it was:

> Reader, thou hast here the beginning and end of a discourse concerning government; what fate has otherwise disposed of the papers that should have filled up the middle, and were more than all the rest, it is not worth while to tell thee. These, which remain, I hope are sufficient to establish the throne of our great restorer, our present King William; to make good his title, in the consent of the people

— *Two Treatises of Government*, Preface

The modern dating begins with Peter Laslett's critical edition, the edition the [learning path](../../learning-path.md) assigns. Laslett argued that the book was written in substance during the Exclusion Crisis and revised for publication in 1689; the IEP summarizes him as placing its origins in 1679, and on the usual account of his introduction he also held that the *Second Treatise* was begun before the *First*. Richard Ashcraft's *Revolutionary Politics and Locke's Two Treatises of Government* (1986) kept the Exclusion setting but placed the writing in 1680–82 (the IEP), and connected Locke with the Whigs who moved from parliamentary agitation toward armed resistance. The SEP states the result as settled: "We now know" that the book was written during the Exclusion Crisis, in 1681, and may have been meant in part to justify the rising some Whig leaders were planning. Either way the book was written for a cause that was losing, and the Preface's King William is a later frame.

Whether Locke himself was a conspirator is another question. The DNB, writing before Laslett, judged that he was not privy to Shaftesbury's plots, noted that he solemnly denied writing the Whig pamphlets attributed to him, and remarked that Christ Church was the worst possible base for a plotter against church and king. Ashcraft's Locke was deep in radical Whig politics, in England and later among the exiles; Woolhouse's biography is more cautious (the SEP). The question is open.

## Flight to Holland and the Christ Church expulsion, 1683–1684

Shaftesbury fled to Holland in November 1682 and died there in January 1683 (the SEP). In June 1683 the Rye House Plot, a scheme to shoot the king and his brother on their way back from the Newmarket races, was revealed; Lord William Russell and Algernon Sidney were executed and the Earl of Essex committed suicide (the IEP), and Sidney's manuscript *Discourses concerning Government*, which argued for a right to revolt, was used against him at his trial. On 21 July Oxford's Convocation condemned propositions from Hobbes, Milton, Baxter and others and ordered their books burned (see [the Hobbes life](../thomas-hobbes/01-life-and-times.md)). At Christ Church, the DNB says, Locke was closely watched and accused of association with one of the Rye House agents. He left for the West Country in the very week the plot was revealed, by the SEP's account, and by September 1683 he was in Holland.

The government struck at what it could reach. On 6 November 1684 the Earl of Sunderland told John Fell, Dean of Christ Church, to expel him. Fell replied that Locke had been watched "for divers years" and that no one had heard him speak a word for or against the government: there was not in the world "such a master of taciturnity and passion" (the DNB). He proposed a summons and expulsion only for refusal; the king's order of 11 November required expulsion at once, and on 16 November Fell reported it done (the DNB). After the Revolution Locke petitioned for his studentship and then let the petition drop rather than displace his successor.

## Exile, 1683–1689

By January 1684 Locke was in Amsterdam (the DNB). Charles II died on 6 February 1685 and his Catholic brother became James II; in June the Duke of Monmouth landed in the West Country with a force raised among the exiles and was defeated and executed (the SEP). On 17 May 1685 the English envoy had demanded the extradition of eighty-four suspects, Locke among them. He lived in hiding in Amsterdam, in the house of the physician Pieter Guenellon's father-in-law, as "Dr. Van der Linden" (the DNB). When friends at court obtained the offer of a pardon from James, he declined it, Le Clerc reports, because he had committed no crime (the DNB). How far he was involved with the exiles' plans is disputed: Ashcraft argued for close association with the English revolutionaries in Holland, and the SEP points readers to Woolhouse's more cautious review.

The exile was also the most productive time of his life. His closest friend was Philippus van Limborch, Remonstrant professor of theology at Amsterdam, with whom he corresponded for the rest of his life; through Limborch he met Jean Le Clerc in the winter of 1685–86 (the DNB). From February 1687 he lived in Rotterdam with the Quaker merchant Benjamin Furly, presided over a philosophical club called "The Lantern", and came to know the circle of William of Orange (the DNB).

In the autumn of 1685, the year of the Catholic king's accession and of the revocation of the Edict of Nantes, Locke wrote for Limborch a Latin letter on toleration, an expansion, the DNB says, of the essay of 1667. Its thesis is that the commonwealth exists for civil interests only, that the care of souls is not committed to the magistrate because faith is inward persuasion and cannot be forced, and that a church is a voluntary society. Popple's English opens:

> I must needs answer you freely that I esteem that toleration to be the chief characteristic mark of the true Church.

— *A Letter concerning Toleration*, tr. William Popple (1689), opening paragraph

It was printed only in 1689, after Locke had gone home: in Latin in Holland in the spring, probably seen through the press by Limborch, and in Popple's English in the autumn, both anonymous (the DNB). Locke's only angry letter to Limborch was provoked by hearing that his friend had told two intimates who the author was. Argument 16 in [the arguments file](04-arguments.md) reconstructs the *Letter*, and Week 7 reads it.

In Holland, too, the *Essay* was brought into order. In January 1688 Le Clerc's *Bibliothèque universelle* printed a French abridgment, communicated by Locke (the DNB), which the SEP says gave the continent most of its knowledge of the book until Pierre Coste's French translation (1700 by the DNB; the SEP says 1704). Locke also read Newton's *Principia* and asked Huygens whether its mathematics could be trusted (the SEP).

## Return and publication, 1689

William of Orange landed in England in November 1688 and James II fled. Locke crossed with Princess Mary, landing at Greenwich on 12 February 1689 (the DNB). He declined an embassy to Brandenburg and other posts on grounds of health and accepted the near-sinecure of Commissioner of Appeals at £200 a year, which he held until he died (the DNB). Then came the three books. The *Essay* bears a dedication to Pembroke signed "Dorset Court, 24th of May, 1689", and appeared in December 1689 with 1690 on the title page (the [study guide](../../study/locke-essay.md); the DNB says early in 1690), under Locke's name; he was paid £30 for the copyright. The *Two Treatises*, published in 1689 (the SEP) with a 1690 title page, and the *Letter* were anonymous (the SEP). The book about ideas carried his name; the books arguing that a ruler who breaks his trust may be resisted, and that the magistrate has no business with souls, did not.

## Oates, Damaris Masham and the last friendships

London's smoke drove Locke out. From the spring of 1691 his home was Oates, in the parish of High Laver in Essex, the house of Sir Francis Masham and his second wife Damaris, the daughter of the Cambridge Platonist Ralph Cudworth (the DNB). Locke had met Damaris Cudworth in 1682 and, the SEP says, became involved with her intellectually and romantically; she married Masham after Locke went into exile. She was a philosopher in her own right (the SEP), and her account of Locke, sent to Le Clerc, is the main source for his character. He paid a pound a week for board for himself and his servant and lived, the DNB says, among an attached domestic circle for the rest of his life.

William Molyneux of Dublin, who praised the *Essay* in his *Dioptrica Nova* (1692), became his warmest correspondent. Molyneux had put to him, in letters of 7 July 1688 and 2 March 1693, the problem of a man born blind and made to see, which Locke printed in the second edition (E II.ix.8, Argument 4; the SEP's "Molyneux's Problem"), and he urged the chapter on identity added in 1694 (the study guide). They met only once, in 1698; Molyneux died on 11 October that year (the DNB). Newton, whom Locke came to know after his return (the SEP), was consulted with him on the coinage. Peter King, grandson of Locke's uncle and later Lord Chancellor, became almost an adopted son, and Anthony Collins a devoted friend from 1703 (the DNB).

## Money, the press and the Board of Trade, 1691–1700

Locke's public work rested on his friendship with the Whig ministers John Somers and Charles Montagu. At the end of 1691 he wrote for a member of Parliament, no doubt Somers (the DNB), the letter published in 1692 as *Some Considerations of the Consequences of the Lowering of Interest*. In the coinage crisis of the 1690s he and Newton were both consulted, and his *Further Considerations concerning Raising the Value of Money* (1695), against the Treasury secretary William Lowndes, argued against devaluation; the recoinage bill of April 1696 followed his principles (the DNB). In 1695 notes attributed to him helped persuade Parliament not to renew the Licensing Act (the DNB, which reports Macaulay's doubt about the authorship).

In May 1696 he was appointed a commissioner of the new Board of Trade at £1,000 a year (the DNB). The SEP calls him its most influential member until 1700 and quotes Laslett's description of the board as "the body which administered the United States before the American Revolution". Its business ran from the Irish wool trade and piracy to colonial government and the English poor; Locke drafted a scheme for Irish linen and, in 1697, a harsh plan for the poor law, with workhouses and working schools (the DNB). He resigned in June 1700, refusing to keep a salary he could no longer earn.

## Stillingfleet, the later *Essay*, education and religion

The *Essay* sold fast. A second edition appeared in 1694, with the new chapter on identity and a rewritten chapter on power (II.xxi); a third in 1695; and a fourth, dated 1700, with new chapters on the association of ideas and on enthusiasm (the DNB). Coste's French version followed in 1700 and Burridge's Latin in 1701. In 1703 the heads of the Oxford colleges agreed that tutors should not read it with their pupils, a ban the DNB thought served only as advertisement.

The weightiest attack came from Edward Stillingfleet, Bishop of Worcester. John Toland's *Christianity not Mysterious* (1696) claimed to apply Locke's philosophy to theology, and Stillingfleet's *Vindication of the Doctrine of the Trinity* attacked Toland and Locke together (the DNB). The charge that mattered for the *Essay*, in the SEP's summary, was that Locke put substance out of the reasonable part of the world, and with it the support of the Trinity. Locke, annoyed at Toland's claim of kinship, answered in a *Letter* to the bishop (1697) and two replies (1697 and 1699) (the DNB's narrative; its list of works dates both replies 1697). The exchange is Locke's own best commentary on the *Essay*, above all on substance (Argument 6) and on whether God could give matter the power to think (E IV.iii.6); Weeks 3 and 5 send readers to it.

*Some Thoughts concerning Education* (1693) grew out of letters written from Holland from 1684 to his friend Edward Clarke about Clarke's son, and was published at Molyneux's urging (the DNB): habit, example and esteem over rote and the rod, as the library entry puts it. *The Reasonableness of Christianity* (1695), anonymous again, argued from the Gospels that the faith required for salvation is belief that Jesus is the Messiah, with repentance and a good life. John Edwards attacked it vehemently, and Locke replied in two *Vindications* (1695, 1697). The DNB judged that it struck the keynote of the most popular theology of the next century, deist and latitudinarian alike.

## Death at Oates, 1704

In his last years Locke was confined to Oates, though he still gave medical advice to poor neighbors in 1701 (the DNB). He began a fourth letter on toleration against Jonas Proast, whose attacks had drawn his second and third letters (1690, 1692), and left it unfinished. On 27 October 1704 he could not rise; on the 28th he asked to be dressed, and while Lady Masham read the Psalms to him he became restless, raised his hands to his eyes and died quietly (the DNB). The SEP calls the day Sunday 28 October; by the Old Style calendar then used in England, 28 October 1704 was a Saturday (see the corrections log). He was buried with as little show as possible in the churchyard at High Laver, under a Latin epitaph of his own. He left legacies to the poor of High Laver and of Publow and Pensford, where he had grown up, and most of his property to Francis Masham and Peter King (the DNB). His papers passed to King's descendants, the Lovelace family (the DNB), and the twentieth century's work on them recovered the *Two Tracts* and the *Essays on the Law of Nature* and dated the *Essay*'s drafts and the *Two Treatises*. The *Paraphrase and Notes on the Epistles of St. Paul* (1705–07) and the *Posthumous Works* (1706), with *Of the Conduct of the Understanding*, came after him.

## Weighing the sources

**What is solid.** The dates of schooling, degrees and college offices, the entry into Shaftesbury's household, the French journey, the flight of 1683, the expulsion of 1684, the extradition list of 1685, the return of 1689, the publications and their editions, the Board of Trade and the death at Oates rest on college and state records, Locke's journals and accounts, and the books themselves. The DNB, SEP and IEP agree on the outline; where they differ by months or a day (Shaftesbury's imprisonment and arrest, Coste's translation, the weekday of Locke's death), this file gives both.

**What rests on friends.** Almost everything about Locke's character comes from people who loved him: Lady Masham, the third Earl of Shaftesbury, and Le Clerc, who built on both. The undergraduate who read romances, the father who became a friend, the date of the Exeter House meeting and the death during the Psalms come from this circle. Tyrrell's report that the meeting of 1671 was about morality and revealed religion is one witness's memory.

**What is disputed.** When and in what order the *Two Treatises* were written (Laslett against Ashcraft; the SEP's 1681); how far Locke was involved in Whig conspiracy in 1681–83 and among the exiles (Ashcraft for, the DNB and more cautiously Woolhouse against); how much of the Carolina constitutions is his (Armitage against Farr and others); and whether his natural-law theory is coherent (Strauss and Laslett doubting, Yolton, Simmons and Grant defending).

**What Locke concealed.** The record is thin partly because Locke kept it so: anonymous books, denied pamphlets, a false name in hiding, and the silence Fell complained of. Biographers who find him evasive and those who find him prudent are reading the same silences.

**Where this file differs from the library entry.** This file agrees with [the library entry](../../eras/03-early-modern/john-locke.md) and the [study guide](../../study/locke-essay.md) on the outline and the dates of the works. It hedges one phrase: the entry has Locke returning after the Revolution brought William and Mary to the throne, while the DNB has him landing on 12 February 1689, as the settlement was being made (see the corrections log).
