# Focus Study: John Locke (1632 – 1704)

## The figure in brief

John Locke was born in August 1632 at Wrington in Somerset and grew up at Pensford, the son of a country attorney who served on Parliament's side in the Civil War. He was educated at Westminster School and Christ Church, Oxford, where he stayed for fifteen years as a student and lecturer, wrote two early tracts in defence of the magistrate's power over religious worship, and turned to medicine and experimental science with Robert Boyle and Thomas Sydenham. In 1667 he joined the household of Anthony Ashley Cooper, later the first Earl of Shaftesbury, as physician, secretary and adviser, and his life became bound to Shaftesbury's politics. A discussion among friends around 1671 began the work that became the *Essay Concerning Human Understanding*. During the Exclusion Crisis he wrote the *Two Treatises of Government*; when Shaftesbury fell, Locke went into exile in the Netherlands (1683–89), where he finished the *Essay* and wrote the *Letter Concerning Toleration*. He came back in February 1689, and within a year all three books were in print, the *Two Treatises* and the *Letter* anonymously. He spent his last years at Oates in Essex, in the household of Damaris Masham, serving on the Board of Trade, defending the *Essay* against Bishop Stillingfleet, and writing on education, money and the reasonableness of Christianity. He died there in October 1704.

He matters to this library because he set the terms of two modern conversations at once. The *Essay* asks what the human mind can know and answers from experience: no innate principles, ideas from sensation and reflection, primary and secondary qualities, substance as a supposed support we cannot see, personal identity as consciousness, real and nominal essences, and a knowledge that runs out quickly into probability. Berkeley, Hume, Leibniz, Reid and Kant all start from it. The *Second Treatise* gives the liberal account of government: a state of nature governed by a law of nature, property begun in labour, political power held by consent and in trust, and a people that may resist when the trust is broken. The *Letter* separates the business of the magistrate from the care of souls. Each of these is still argued over, and each is often simplified.

## Why he rewards weeks of study

First, *the method*. Locke writes as an "under-labourer", clearing ground with a plain "historical" method, and the *Essay* repays a reader who follows its qualifications, which often arrive several sections after the claim they qualify. Read in order, it is a single inquiry into the extent of human understanding.

Second, *the arguments*. The case against innate principles, the primary and secondary quality distinction, the memory theory of personal identity, the labour theory of property and the account of tacit consent are among the most cited and most contested arguments in philosophy. Each can be put in numbered premises, and each has a live scholarly dispute about what it is.

Third, *the reply to Hobbes*. Locke is built straight after the [Hobbes dossier](../thomas-hobbes/README.md) because he keeps the state of nature, consent and covenant and changes what each one does. Reading them together shows the contract tradition dividing into its absolutist and liberal branches.

## On the quotations

The dossier quotes only public-domain texts, so that every quotation can be checked word for word, and it keeps their wording exactly. The *Essay* is quoted from Project Gutenberg's transcription (eBooks #10615–10616) of a posthumous printing in largely modern spelling; IV.xix, "Of Enthusiasm", which that transcription leaves empty, is quoted from an 1853 printing on Wikisource. The *Second Treatise* is quoted from Project Gutenberg (#7370), the *First Treatise* from a 1764 printing on Wikisource, and the *Letter Concerning Toleration* from William Popple's English translation of 1689. For reading, the dossier recommends Nidditch's Clarendon text or Winkler's Hackett abridgement for the *Essay*, Laslett's Cambridge edition for the *Two Treatises*, and Tully or Goldie for the *Letter*.

The *Essay* is cited by book, chapter and section in Nidditch's numbering (E II.xxvii.9), which modern editions share. The Gutenberg text numbers a few chapters differently (II.xxi, II.xxvii and the end of I.iv); where it does, a draft may add the Gutenberg number in brackets. The *Second Treatise* is cited by its numbered paragraphs (ST §27), the *First Treatise* likewise (FT §14). The *Letter* has no standard divisions, so it is cited with a short locating phrase.
