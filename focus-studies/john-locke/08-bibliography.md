# Bibliography: John Locke
**English** | [简体中文](../../zh/focus-studies/john-locke/08-bibliography.md)

*Focus study: John Locke · [Dossier home](README.md) · Library entry: [John Locke](../../eras/03-early-modern/john-locke.md) · Study guide: [*Essay Concerning Human Understanding*](../../study/locke-essay.md) · Edition rulings: [02-works-and-texts.md](02-works-and-texts.md)*

Annotated and tiered: read downward only as far as your questions require. No prices are given; none was read on a publisher page for this list. Years and publishers come from a publisher page, a library catalogue record (Open Library edition records, searched 13 November 2026) or the bibliographies of the SEP and IEP entries in §4, and an entry says which where they disagree; page counts appear only where the catalogue record states them. One fact governs the list: the *Essay* is cited by **book, chapter and section** (E II.xxvii.9), the *Two Treatises* by **section** (ST §27, FT §86), and the *Letter* by a short locating phrase, since it has no standard numbering. Every reading edition below keeps Locke's section numbers, so it finds every reference in this dossier. The edition rulings are made and explained in [02-works-and-texts.md](02-works-and-texts.md); this list follows them. This file quotes Locke only in three short phrases ("white paper", "substance in general", "enough, and as good"), taken from the saved free texts; the rest of the dossier quotes him from the public-domain texts listed at the end of §1, in their own (largely modernized) spelling.

## 1. Primary texts: the editions the plan uses

**Peter H. Nidditch (ed.), *An Essay Concerning Human Understanding* (Clarendon Edition of the Works of John Locke; Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1975).** The dossier's first ruling for the *Essay*, Weeks 1–5, and the edition the scholarship cites. It prints the fourth edition (1700) and records the other editions' variants, which is how you see what Locke gave up when he rewrote II.xxi for the second edition (Week 3). OUP's series page says the Clarendon Edition began with this volume in 1975; a review record in *Philosophy* gives liv + 867 pages. The paperback (Open Library: Clarendon Press, 1979, 748 pp.) omits the Register of Formal Variants and the Glossary and adds a new foreword by Nidditch (OUP's page for that ISBN). Whether it keeps the notes that record the first-edition text of II.xxi was not checked here, so look before you rely on it for Week 3.

**Kenneth P. Winkler (ed.), *An Essay Concerning Human Understanding*, abridged (Indianapolis: Hackett, 1996; Open Library: 416 pp.).** The learning path's fallback if Nidditch stalls you. The study guide says it keeps everything the guide covers, including II.xxi and II.xxvii; the PhilPapers record adds that it prints passages from the replies to Stillingfleet arranged by topic, useful for Argument 6. It was not checked here that it contains every chapter set for Weeks 4–5 (III.vi, IV.iii, IV.xi, IV.xv–xix): look at its contents before you buy it for those weeks, and use Nidditch or the free texts for any it omits.

**Roger Woolhouse (ed.), *An Essay Concerning Human Understanding* (Penguin Classics; Penguin Books, 1997; Open Library: 784 pp.).** The whole *Essay* in one modern paperback. It is not one of the dossier's rulings: use it if you want the complete book in one volume and do not need Nidditch's apparatus (whose text it follows was not checked here). A related option is Pauline Phemister's abridgement for Oxford World's Classics (Oxford University Press, 2008; 520 pp.), which OUP says uses Nidditch's text.

**Peter Laslett (ed.), *Two Treatises of Government*, student edition (Cambridge Texts in the History of Political Thought; Cambridge University Press, 1988; Open Library: 464 pp.).** The ruling for Weeks 6–7. Cambridge's page says this edition revises Laslett's second edition of 1970, with a reset and revised introduction. [02](02-works-and-texts.md) credits that introduction with the modern dating of the *Two Treatises* to the Exclusion Crisis (an attribution it logs as unchecked), so read it before Week 6 and set it against Ashcraft (§3). It prints both treatises, so it serves the *First Treatise* selections as well.

**Ian Shapiro (ed.), *Two Treatises of Government and A Letter Concerning Toleration* (Rethinking the Western Tradition; Yale University Press, 2003; Open Library: 358 pp.).** An alternative for Weeks 6–7 that puts both political texts in one volume. Yale's page lists interpretive essays by John Dunn (on context and the controversies), Ruth Grant (on women and the family) and Shapiro (on the democratic elements of Locke's theory). Use Laslett for the text and the dating; use Shapiro if you want the essays.

**The *Letter*.** Mark Goldie (ed.), *A Letter concerning Toleration and Other Writings* (Indianapolis: Liberty Fund, 2010; Online Library of Liberty record), Week 7. The OLL collection page says the volume adds excerpts from the *Third Letter*, *An Essay on Toleration* and fragments, which is where you meet Locke's answer to Proast (Argument 16). The OLL makes the edition readable online. Or James H. Tully (ed.), *A Letter Concerning Toleration* (Hackett, 1983; Open Library: 62 pp.), the edition the SEP's political entry cites. Both print the English, not Locke's Latin; for an argument that turns on a word, Richard Vernon (ed.), *Locke on Toleration* (Cambridge Texts in the History of Philosophy; Cambridge University Press, 2010), translates the *Letter* afresh (translation by Michael Silverthorne, per the catalogue record), and Raymond Klibansky's Latin with J. W. Gough's English (Oxford, 1968; SEP, via [02](02-works-and-texts.md)) is the scholarly bilingual text.

**Mark Goldie (ed.), *Locke: Political Essays* (Cambridge Texts in the History of Political Thought; Cambridge University Press, 1997).** The early and minor political writings: the *Two Tracts*, the *Essays on the Law of Nature* and seventy shorter pieces, per Cambridge's description. Optional background for Weeks 1 and 6–7, where [02](02-works-and-texts.md) places them.

**The Clarendon Edition volumes already out.** OUP's series page says thirty-seven volumes are planned and eighteen were out by 2012; [02](02-works-and-texts.md) lists the published ones the SEP names. The ones you may open in this course: Peter H. Nidditch & G. A. J. Rogers (eds.), *Drafts for the Essay concerning Human Understanding*, vol. 1 (Clarendon Press, 1990), with Drafts A and B of 1671, and the 1685 Draft C, described on OUP's page for its Clarendon volume (catalogued as vol. 2, eds. J. R. Milton & G. A. J. Rogers, Oxford University Press, 2021; that these records are one book is inferred from their titles); J. R. Milton & Philip Milton (eds.), *An Essay concerning Toleration and Other Writings on Law and Politics, 1667–1683* (2006), Week 7; John C. Higgins-Biddle (ed.), *The Reasonableness of Christianity* (the IEP gives 1999, the catalogue 2000), Week 5; and E. S. de Beer (ed.), *The Correspondence of John Locke* (8 vols., 1976–89, per the IEP), for the letters with Molyneux and Limborch. Use them to check, not to read.

**Free texts: the ones the dossier quotes,** so you can check any quotation word for word (all links opened 13 November 2026):

- ***Essay*:** Project Gutenberg [#10615](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/10615) (Books I–II) and [#10616](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/10616) (Books III–IV). A modern-spelling transcription of a posthumous printing, with the 1690 title page; it leaves II.viii.10, one section of II.xxi and the whole of IV.xix empty, and it numbers II.xxi and II.xxvii differently from Nidditch. [02](02-works-and-texts.md) gives the details and the dossier's section concordance.
- ***Essay* IV.xix, "Of Enthusiasm":** Wikisource's transcription of the [Troutman & Hayes 1853 printing](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/An_Essay_Concerning_Human_Understanding_(Troutman_%26_Hayes_1853)/Book_4/Chapter_XIX), saved as `essay-IV-xix-wikisource-1853.wiki`; the 1823 *Works* ([vol. III](https://archive.org/details/worksjohnlocke07lockgoog) on the Internet Archive, uncorrected OCR) are a further copy.
- ***Two Treatises*:** Project Gutenberg [#7370](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/7370), the Preface and *Second Treatise* only, with the front matter of the London edition of 1764; for the *First Treatise*, Wikisource's [*Two Treatises of Government (1764)*](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Two_Treatises_of_Government_(1764)) or the 1823 *Works*, [vol. V](https://archive.org/details/worksjohnlocke01lockgoog).
- ***Letter*:** William Popple's English of 1689 on [Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/A_Letter_Concerning_Toleration), in a modernized transcription without Popple's preface.

**Avoid** any free *Essay* built from the Gutenberg text that does not say IV.xix is missing; *Second Treatise* editions that drop the Preface without saying so; page citations of the *Essay* without an edition; and treating Popple's English as Locke's own words where it departs from the Latin. Modernized paraphrases such as the Early Modern Texts versions (earlymoderntexts.com) can help you through a hard chapter, but they rewrite Locke's sentences, so never quote or reconstruct from them.

## 2. First secondary books, in order

1. **E. J. Lowe, *Locke* (Routledge Philosophers; Routledge, 2005).** One or two evenings before Week 1. The NDPR review describes the series as introductions for undergraduates and general readers, and the IEP calls the book an introductory overview of the philosophy and the politics; it is the one first book here that covers both halves of the course. Lowe was a metaphysician, and the book assesses Locke in a contemporary setting (Routledge's description), so read its verdicts as one philosopher's, to be tested in your own reconstructions. If you want something shorter, the IEP calls Andrew Pyle, *Locke* (Polity, 2013), a very good place for beginners to start (not looked up in the catalogue).
2. **Roger Woolhouse, *Locke: A Biography* (Cambridge University Press, 2007; xviii + 528 pp., per a review record).** The life, for [01-life-and-times.md](01-life-and-times.md) and Week 1. The SEP cites it, and the IEP calls it the best and most recent biography (the IEP dates it 2009; the catalogue and both reviews checked give 2007). Cambridge's description says it explains the ideas as well as the life; the older standard is Maurice Cranston, *John Locke: A Biography* (1957, per the SEP).
3. **For the *Essay*, one guide beside the text, Weeks 1–5.** Either William Uzgalis, *Locke's Essay Concerning Human Understanding* (Continuum, 2007; Open Library: 141 pp.), by the author of the SEP's main Locke entry, or Lowe's longer *Locke on Human Understanding* (Routledge Philosophy Guidebooks; Routledge, 1995; 203 pp.), reissued as *The Routledge Guidebook to Locke's Essay Concerning Human Understanding* (2013, per the catalogue). Both follow the order of the *Essay*, so read the relevant chapter after you have reconstructed Locke's argument, never before.
4. **Vere Chappell (ed.), *The Cambridge Companion to Locke* (Cambridge University Press, 1994; 329 pp.).** Dip in by topic from Week 2 on; the IEP describes it as essays on all aspects of Locke's thought. For the *Essay* alone, Lex Newman (ed.), *The Cambridge Companion to Locke's "Essay Concerning Human Understanding"* (Cambridge University Press, 2007; 502 pp.), a chapter per main topic, Weeks 2–5.
5. **John Dunn, *Locke: A Very Short Introduction* (Oxford University Press, 2003; 136 pp.).** Before Week 6, by the scholar behind the theological reading of the politics (§3); the IEP's political entry lists it. It is a reissue of Dunn's *Locke* in the Past Masters series (Oxford University Press, 1984, per the catalogue; the series name and the reissue are from memory).

## 3. Advanced and specialist, by area

Open these when a week's checkpoint question has narrowed to a debate. Most are academic books, best borrowed. A scholar's position is reported from the SEP or IEP entry named; anything else is marked as from memory.

- **The whole philosophy.** Michael Ayers, *Locke: Epistemology and Ontology* (2 vols., Routledge, 1991, per the SEP; the IEP gives 1993), the classic large study, one volume on each half of its title (IEP). Nicholas Jolley, *Locke: His Philosophical Thought* (Oxford University Press, 1999), a shorter analytic survey that the SEP cites on both substance and freedom. Matthew Stuart (ed.), *A Companion to Locke* (Blackwell Companions to Philosophy; Wiley-Blackwell; the SEP gives 2016, the catalogue records 2014 and 2015), and Jessica Gordon-Roth & Shelley Weinberg (eds.), *The Lockean Mind* (Routledge, 2022, per the SEP), the two big recent collections and the place to start a literature search on one argument. Patrick J. Connolly (ed.), *The Oxford Handbook of John Locke* (Oxford University Press) is listed as forthcoming in the SEP's freedom entry (revised May 2026) and as in press since July 2025 in a contributor's university record; check whether it is out before you look for it. John W. Yolton, *A Locke Dictionary* (Blackwell, 1993), for terms. J. L. Mackie, *Problems from Locke* (Clarendon Press, 1976), which the IEP says uses Locke to raise philosophical puzzles: good for Arguments 3, 6 and 7. The older general study is Richard I. Aaron, *John Locke* (Oxford University Press, 1937; later editions 1955 and 1971, per the catalogue).
- **Innate ideas and the way of ideas (Weeks 1–2; Arguments 1–4).** John W. Yolton, *John Locke and the Way of Ideas* (Oxford University Press, 1956; reprinted by Thoemmes, 1996). The SEP says Yolton argued persuasively that the innatism Locke attacks was widely held in seventeenth-century England, which answers the charge that Book I attacks a straw man. Yolton's *Locke and the Compass of Human Understanding* (Cambridge University Press, 1970) follows the *Essay* further. For the rationalist reply, Nicholas Jolley, *Leibniz and Locke: A Study of the "New Essays on Human Understanding"* (Clarendon Press, 1984; the subtitle from memory). For the science Locke was under-labouring for, Peter R. Anstey, *John Locke and Natural Philosophy* (Oxford University Press, 2011), which the IEP calls a thorough examination of Locke's scientific and medical thinking.
- **Substance and essences (Weeks 3–4; Arguments 6, 9).** Here there is a live dispute. Ayers proposes, per the SEP, that "substance in general" picks out the same thing as the unknown real essence, so Locke need not posit a bare substratum without properties; the SEP reports that this reading has been criticized for lack of textual support and for conflict with what Locke says, citing Jolley (1999: 71–3). Ayers and Uzgalis ("The Anti-Essential Locke and Natural Kinds", *Philosophical Quarterly* 38, 1988) read Locke as denying Aristotelian natural kinds, against Lovejoy and David Wiggins (SEP). Matthew Stuart, *Locke's Metaphysics* (Oxford University Press, 2013), which the IEP calls an in-depth treatment, is the fullest recent book on Weeks 3–4.
- **Power and freedom (Week 3; Argument 5).** Gideon Yaffe, *Liberty Worth the Name: Locke on Free Agency* (Princeton University Press, 2000), which the SEP's freedom entry cites for the view that Locke wants a liberty worth having rather than mere absence of constraint; read the entry's survey of replies (Lowe, Hoffman, Chappell, Garrett) alongside it. Vere Chappell's articles on suspension, listed there, are the specialist's starting point.
- **Personal identity (Week 4; Argument 7).** The SEP entry by Jessica Gordon-Roth sets out the main readings: substance readings; mode readings, popular today (Antonia LoLordo, *Locke's Moral Man*, Oxford University Press, 2012; Ruth Mattern; Uzgalis), going back to Edmund Law; and "appropriation" readings, on which a person extends back only to the acts it takes as its own, in Kenneth Winkler's "Locke on Personal Identity" (*Journal of the History of Philosophy* 29, 1991), Udo Thiel's *The Early Modern Subject* (Oxford University Press, 2011) and LoLordo. Shelley Weinberg, *Consciousness in Locke* (Oxford University Press, 2016), is in the SEP's words the first large-scale treatment of Locke on consciousness. Read Winkler's article first: it is short and sets up the Butler and Reid objections that the library entry poses.
- **Natural law, rights and property (Week 6; Arguments 13–14).** The SEP's political entry by Alex Tuckness lays out two disputes. On rights and duties: Leo Strauss (1953; the book is *Natural Right and History*, title from memory) makes the right of self-preservation primary; John Dunn, *The Political Thought of John Locke* (Cambridge University Press, 1969), James Tully and Richard Ashcraft make natural-law duties primary; A. John Simmons, *The Lockean Theory of Rights* (Princeton University Press, 1992), holds rights and duties equally basic. On property: C. B. Macpherson, *The Political Theory of Possessive Individualism: Hobbes to Locke* (Clarendon Press, 1962), reads Locke as defending unlimited capitalist accumulation; James Tully, *A Discourse on Property: John Locke and His Adversaries* (Cambridge University Press, 1980), reads him as a critic of self-interest who saw the coming of money as a fall (SEP, "John Locke", §4); Jeremy Waldron, *The Right to Private Property* (Clarendon Press, 1988), argues that the "enough, and as good" condition (ST §27) is descriptive rather than a normative proviso (SEP political entry). Read Macpherson and Tully against each other before you write up Argument 14. On the theology: Dunn argues that Locke's arguments depend on religious premises many no longer share, while Waldron, *God, Locke, and Equality: Christian Foundations in Locke's Political Thought* (Cambridge University Press, 2002), argues that the theology gives equality a firmer basis than secular theories do (SEP political entry). The Waldron book is the best single book for Argument 13.
- **Consent and resistance (Week 7; Arguments 15–16).** A. John Simmons, *On the Edge of Anarchy: Locke, Consent, and the Limits of Society* (Princeton University Press, 1993): per the SEP, Simmons agrees that only real consent obliges, doubts that most people have given it, and concludes that Locke's argument pushes toward philosophical anarchism; the SEP sets Hannah Pitkin's reading against him. For the history: Richard Ashcraft, *Revolutionary Politics and Locke's Two Treatises of Government* (Princeton University Press, 1986; 626 pp.), which ties the *Second Treatise* to the conspiratorial politics of the early 1680s and reads it as a call to resistance (as [02](02-works-and-texts.md) summarizes it), and John Marshall, *John Locke: Resistance, Religion and Responsibility* (Cambridge University Press, 1994; 509 pp.), cited in the SEP's political entry.
- **Toleration (Week 7; Argument 16).** John Marshall, *John Locke, Toleration and Early Enlightenment Culture* (Cambridge University Press, 2006; 776 pp.), which the SEP calls massive and cites for the 1680s as the climactic decade of persecution. Jeremy Waldron, "Locke, Toleration, and the Rationality of Persecution", in his *Liberal Rights* (Cambridge University Press, 1993), pp. 88–114, restates Proast's objection for a modern audience: force cannot change belief directly, but it may do so indirectly (SEP political entry). It is the essential reading for the objection to Argument 16. Nicholas Jolley, *Toleration and Understanding in Locke* (Oxford University Press, 2017), links the *Letter* to the *Essay* (description from memory; unchecked).

## 4. Reference

Authors and dates are from each entry's own page, as saved on 13 November 2026.

- **[SEP, "John Locke"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/locke/)** by William Uzgalis (first published 2 September 2001; substantive revision 7 July 2022). Life, the *Essay* book by book, the politics, toleration, and a long bibliography with the Clarendon volumes. Read §§1–2 before Week 1 and the rest as the weeks reach each topic.
- **[SEP, "Locke's Political Philosophy"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/locke-political/)** by Alex Tuckness (9 November 2005; revised 6 October 2020). Natural law and rights, the state of nature, property, consent, the separation of powers, resistance, toleration, each with the scholars on every side (the source of most positions reported in §3). Before Week 6.
- **Satellite SEP entries, by week:** ["Molyneux's Problem"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/molyneux-problem/) (Brian Glenney; revised 8 January 2026), Week 2; ["Locke On Freedom"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/locke-freedom/) (Samuel Rickless; revised 12 May 2026), Week 3; ["Locke on Personal Identity"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/locke-personal-identity/) (Jessica Gordon-Roth; revised 29 September 2025) and the general ["Personal Identity"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/identity-personal/) (Eric T. Olson; revised 30 June 2023), Week 4; ["Locke's Philosophy of Science"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/locke-philosophy-science/) (Hylarie Kochiras; revised 8 November 2023), Weeks 4–5; ["Locke's Moral Philosophy"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/locke-moral/) (Patricia Sheridan; revised 20 October 2026), Weeks 5–6. Free; budget an hour or two per entry (an estimate).
- **[IEP, "Locke, John"](https://iep.utm.edu/locke/)** by Patrick J. Connolly: the plainer first pass on the whole philosophy, with a short annotated reading list (the source of the IEP verdicts in §§1–3). ["Locke, John: Political Philosophy"](https://iep.utm.edu/locke-po/) (Alexander Moseley), Weeks 6–7. ["Locke: Knowledge of the External World"](https://iep.utm.edu/locke-kn/) (Matthew Priselac), Week 5 and Argument 11. The IEP pages show no publication date.
- **[PhilPapers, "John Locke"](https://philpapers.org/browse/john-locke)**, for finding articles on one argument, with subcategories such as [Locke: Relations](https://philpapers.org/browse/locke-relations) for identity.
- **Journals.** *Locke Studies* and the older *Locke Newsletter*, both cited throughout the SEP bibliographies, are the specialist venues.

- **BBC Radio 4, *In Our Time*** (free on BBC Sounds). No programme on Locke alone was found, either in the show's podcast feed or by search. Dates, guests and lengths below are from each programme page.
  - **Assigned:** ["Empiricism"](https://www.bbc.co.uk/programmes/p004y28g) (10 June 2004; 45 minutes; Melvyn Bragg with Judith Hawley, Murray Pittock and Jonathan Rée). The page opens with Locke's "white paper" passage from II.i.2, and the programme follows the idea of experience through the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. After Week 2. The podcast file in the feed is shorter (about 28 minutes by its stated length).
  - **Optional:** ["Toleration"](https://www.bbc.co.uk/programmes/p004y288) (20 May 2004; 45 minutes; with Justin Champion, David Wootton and Sarah Barber), Week 7; ["The Glorious Revolution"](https://www.bbc.co.uk/programmes/p00547fk) (19 April 2001; 45 minutes; with John Spurr, Rosemary Sweet and Scott Mandelbrote), for [01-life-and-times.md](01-life-and-times.md) and the dating question; ["The Divine Right of Kings"](https://www.bbc.co.uk/programmes/b0080xph) (11 October 2007; 45 minutes; with Justin Champion, Tom Healy and Clare Jackson), whose page names Locke's political works, for the *First Treatise* in Week 6; ["The Social Contract"](https://www.bbc.co.uk/programmes/b008w3xm) (7 February 2008; 45 minutes; with Melissa Lane, Susan James and Karen O'Brien), after Week 7; ["Civility"](https://www.bbc.co.uk/programmes/m002f9f4) (2025; 51 minutes; with Teresa Bejan, Phil Withington and John Gallagher), whose page names Locke with Hobbes, Roger Williams and Rawls, Week 7; and ["Bishop Berkeley"](https://www.bbc.co.uk/programmes/b03y36vr) (20 March 2014; 47 minutes; with Peter Millican, Tom Stoneham and Michela Massimi), for the reply to Argument 3 after Week 2. The Civility page gives a first broadcast of 3 July 2025 and the feed 31 July 2025, so only the year is given.
- **Yale, PLSC 114, *Introduction to Political Philosophy*** (Steven B. Smith; [Open Yale Courses](https://oyc.yale.edu/political-science/plsc-114), free, with transcripts and audio). The course page says it was videotaped in Fall 2006 and taught in 50-minute sessions; the lecture pages state no running time. Three lectures, all titled "Constitutional Government: Locke, *Second Treatise*":
  - [Lecture 15](https://oyc.yale.edu/political-science/plsc-114/lecture-15) (chs. 1–5): who Locke was, natural law, and property and labor. After Week 6.
  - [Lecture 16](https://oyc.yale.edu/political-science/plsc-114/lecture-16) (chs. 7–12): "Locke and the Spirit of Capitalism", government by consent and limited government. After the first half of Week 7.

- **Yale, PHIL 176, *Death*** (Shelly Kagan; [Open Yale Courses](https://oyc.yale.edu/philosophy/phil-176), free; recorded Spring 2007 in 50-minute sessions, per the course page). Not a Locke course, but its personal-identity lectures use him. In [Lecture 10](https://oyc.yale.edu/philosophy/phil-176/lecture-10) ("Identity Across Space and Time and the Soul Theory") Kagan presents Locke's worry that, on the soul theory, we could never tell whether we had survived from one minute to the next; in [Lecture 11](https://oyc.yale.edu/philosophy/phil-176/lecture-11) ("The Body Theory and the Personality Theory") he calls these "Lockean worries" and notes that Locke believed in souls but did not make them the key to identity. Lectures 12–13 do not mention Locke by name but take the personality theory through duplication and fission, which is where Argument 7 is pressed today. After Week 4.
- **Wi-Phi (Wireless Philosophy)**: [*Locke on Personal Identity*](https://www.wi-phi.com/series/locke-on-personal-identity/), two short animated episodes by Michael Della Rocca (Yale), on what makes you the same person as the child you were and whether body, soul or something else carries identity. Before Week 4. No running times are stated.
