# Works and Texts: The Corpus in Reading Order

*Focus study: John Locke · [Dossier home](README.md) · Library entry: [John Locke](../../eras/03-early-modern/john-locke.md) · Study guide: [*Essay Concerning Human Understanding*](../../study/locke-essay.md) · Arguments in full: [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md)*

Locke came to print late. Within a few months of 1689–90, when he was in his late fifties, he published three works that had been in his papers for years: *A Letter Concerning Toleration*, the *Two Treatises of Government* and *An Essay Concerning Human Understanding*. Only the *Essay* carried his name. Behind them lie thirty years of unpublished writing: the Restoration tracts on the magistrate's power over religion, the Latin essays on the law of nature, a manuscript essay on toleration, and the two 1671 drafts of the *Essay*. Ahead of them lie fifteen years of revision and controversy: four more editions of the *Essay*, two more letters on toleration, the education book, the *Reasonableness of Christianity*, the exchange with Bishop Stillingfleet, and the posthumous *Conduct of the Understanding*. Much of the early material survives because Locke's papers passed to his nephew Peter King and, as the Lovelace collection, eventually to Oxford, bought and given by Paul Mellon (SEP). That is why twentieth-century scholars can see Locke's development in a way his first readers could not.

Two facts shape how the corpus is read. First, the title-page dates are not the dates of thought. The *Essay* had been growing since 1671; the *Two Treatises* were written during the Exclusion Crisis, c. 1679–83, not after the Revolution of 1688 as their Preface suggests; the *Letter* was written in Latin in Holland in 1685. Second, the *Essay* is a moving text. Locke revised it four times, and two chapters the dossier reads most closely are second-edition work: II.xxi on power was rewritten and II.xxvii on identity added in 1694. Unless this file names another edition, a statement about what the *Essay* says refers to the fourth edition (1700) as modern editions print it.

Locke is built straight after [Thomas Hobbes](../thomas-hobbes/README.md), and the two works files share a plan, so [the Hobbes works file](../thomas-hobbes/02-works-and-texts.md) can be read alongside this one; where Locke answers Hobbes, this file points to the Hobbes argument concerned.

Quotations keep the spelling of the saved public-domain texts, and nothing inside quotation marks is modernized. The texts are: the *Essay* from Project Gutenberg #10615–10616, a modern-spelling transcription of a posthumous printing (see "The free text" below), with the 1853 printing (Troutman & Hayes, on Wikisource) for the one chapter Gutenberg omits; the *Two Treatises* from Project Gutenberg #7370 and from Wikisource, both transcribing the London edition of 1764; and William Popple's English *Letter* from Wikisource, in a modernized transcription.

## The forms Locke wrote in

The *Essay* runs in books, chapters and short numbered sections, each section with a marginal summary in Locke's words; reading a chapter's section headings first is the fastest way into it. The title means what it says. In the Epistle to the Reader Locke presents himself as "an under-labourer in clearing the ground a little" (E, Epistle) for the scientists of the age, and complains of critics who "will not suffer it to be an essay" (E, Epistle). The *Two Treatises* also run in numbered sections, but the numbering is continuous through each treatise, which is why the *Second Treatise* is cited by section alone (ST §27). The toleration writings are public letters: the first addressed to Philipp van Limborch (DNB), the later ones answering a named critic, Jonas Proast. The Stillingfleet controversy too was conducted by printed letters. *Some Thoughts concerning Education* grew from letters of advice to a friend, and *Of the Conduct of the Understanding* from an unfinished chapter for the *Essay*.

## The Oxford years: the unpublished early works (1660–1667)

### The *Two Tracts on Government* (c. 1660–1662)

Locke's first sustained writings answer one question raised by the Restoration church settlement: may the magistrate impose "indifferent things", matters of worship that Scripture neither commands nor forbids? The young Locke says yes. The English tract answers Edward Bagshaw's plea for toleration point by point. The Latin tract makes the same case as an academic oration; Cambridge's headnote in Mark Goldie's *Locke: Political Essays* (1997) dates it between autumn 1660 and early 1663, most likely late 1662, and notes that its title was given by Philip Abrams. A *Historical Journal* abstract places both tracts in 1660–62. Neither was published by Locke; Abrams edited them as *Two Tracts on Government* (Cambridge, 1967; SEP bibliography).

The tracts show Locke starting from the other side of the toleration question, with Hobbes's premise that without one authority over public worship religious dispute becomes civil war ([Hobbes's Argument 15](../thomas-hobbes/04-arguments.md)). The SEP says that in the early 1660s Locke was very likely an orthodox Anglican and that his views evolved. The *Cambridge History of Political Thought 1450–1700* traces a movement from absolutism and unconditional obedience in the tracts to popular sovereignty and a right of revolution in the *Two Treatises*. Whether this was a conversion or the same concern for order applied to new circumstances is a debate; [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md) gives it. **Difficulty:** low. **Place:** optional background for Week 7.

### The *Essays on the Law of Nature* (c. 1662–1664)

While holding college office at Christ Church, Locke wrote Latin disputations on the law of nature; the IEP says they grew out of his teaching. A Cambridge Core chapter dates them 1662–64 and gives their alternative title, *Questions Concerning Natural Law*. They stayed in manuscript until Wolfgang von Leyden edited them with a translation (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1954; IEP and SEP bibliographies). Two theses run into the mature work: the law of nature is known by reason working on sense experience, not by inscription on the mind, which is Book I of the *Essay* in embryo; and it binds because it is the will of a superior, the voluntarist strand that the SEP's entry on the political philosophy traces into the *Essay* (it cites E I.iii.6 and IV.x.7). The SEP's entry on the moral philosophy reports that Laslett, Aaron and von Leyden read Locke's natural law as a relic of these years and a "rogue element" in the empiricist *Essay*; the opposing readings are in [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md). **Difficulty:** moderate. **Place:** optional, with Weeks 1 and 6 (Arguments 1 and 13).

### *An Essay concerning Toleration* (c. 1667)

A manuscript essay on toleration, usually dated 1667, the year Locke joined Lord Ashley's household, already argues the mature position in outline: the magistrate's business is civil peace, and opinions and modes of worship fall outside it unless they threaten that peace (date and summary from memory; logged unchecked). J. R. Milton and Philip Milton edited it in the Clarendon Edition volume *An Essay concerning Toleration and Other Writings on Law and Politics, 1667–1683* (2006; SEP). **Place:** optional, with Week 7, as the bridge between the tracts and the *Letter*.

## *An Essay Concerning Human Understanding* (1690; five editions to 1706)

### Origins and the drafts (1671–1688)

The Epistle tells how a group of "five or six friends" (E, Epistle) meeting in Locke's chamber got stuck, and Locke proposed that they first examine what their understandings were fitted to deal with. The SEP adds that James Tyrrell, who was present, remembered the subject as the principles of morality and revealed religion. What followed was "written by incoherent parcels" (E, Epistle) over nearly twenty years.

Two drafts survive from 1671 (IEP). Draft A, the shorter, was first printed by Richard Aaron and Jocelyn Gibb as *An Early Draft of Locke's Essay* (Oxford, 1936); both A and B are in the Clarendon *Drafts for the Essay concerning Human Understanding*, vol. 1, ed. Peter Nidditch and G. A. J. Rogers (1990; SEP bibliography). A partial Draft C of 1685 also survives (from memory; logged unchecked). The drafts are for specialists, but Tyrrell's memory carries one lesson for any reader: the theory of ideas began as a way into questions of morality and religion, so Book IV's account of knowledge, probability and faith is where the project was always heading, as the study guide also says.

In exile in Holland Locke finished the book and printed a French summary in 1688. The SEP calls it a fifty-page advance notice that for years gave Continental readers most of what they knew of the *Essay*; Locke mentions "a short Epitome of this Treatise, which was printed in 1688" (E, Epistle), condemned by some unread because it denied innate ideas.

### The five editions

**First edition, 1690.** Printed by "Eliz. Holt, for Thomas Basset", with "MDCXC" on the title page, and dedicated to Thomas, Earl of Pembroke and Montgomery (title page and dedication as reproduced in the saved Gutenberg text). The library entry and the study guide say it came out in December 1689; the DNB says it "appeared early in 1690" and that the dedication is dated May 1689 (see the corrections log, Doubts). In this edition II.xxi holds that the will is determined by the greater good as the understanding judges it, and there is no chapter on identity.

**Second edition, 1694.** Called for in August 1692 and delayed by Locke's alterations and a slow press, it appeared in 1694, with the additions also printed separately for owners of the first (DNB). The new Epistle reports that the bookseller "desires too, that it should be known that it has one whole new chapter concerning Identity" (E, Epistle): II.xxvii, "Of Identity and Diversity", written, the study guide says, at William Molyneux's urging. Most additions, Locke says, only confirm or explain what he had said, with one exception: "I must only except the alterations I have made in Book II. chap. xxi." (E, Epistle). Closer inspection of the mind had given him reason "somewhat to alter the thoughts I formerly had concerning that which gives the last determination to the Will in all voluntary actions" (E, Epistle). The revised chapter makes "uneasiness", not the judged greater good, what determines the will, and adds the power to suspend a desire while it is examined, which Locke calls "the source of all liberty" (E II.xxi.47); the IEP dates the doctrine of suspension from this edition. The second edition also adds Molyneux's problem, which Molyneux had sent Locke in a letter "some months since" (E II.ix.8); the SEP's entry on Molyneux's problem dates that letter 2 March 1693 and says Locke quoted it almost verbatim in the 1694 edition.

So Weeks 3 and 4 read 1694 Locke. Nidditch records the first-edition text of II.xxi among his variants, and anyone writing on Argument 5 should know that the dispute over whether suspension gives Locke a robust freedom or only one more desire at work (IEP) is a dispute about the revised text.

**Third edition, 1695.** Almost a reprint of the second (DNB).

**Fourth edition, 1700.** Out in autumn 1699, dated 1700, and "again carefully revised" (DNB). Its notice in the Epistle announces "an addition of two chapters wholly new; the one of the Association of Ideas, the other of Enthusiasm" (E, Epistle), II.xxxiii and IV.xix, and a change of vocabulary through the whole book: "I have therefore in most places chose to put DETERMINATE or DETERMINED, instead of CLEAR and DISTINCT" (E, Epistle). The fourth edition is the copy-text of Nidditch's Clarendon edition (study guide).

**Fifth edition, 1706.** Posthumous, "with a few corrections by Locke" (DNB). The free text's Epistle closes with a further note, for what it calls "the Sixth Edition", saying the greatest part of what is new is in II.xxi. Which edition first carried each late addition is a matter for the Clarendon apparatus; the dossier does not rule on it.

**Translations.** Pierre Coste's French appeared at Amsterdam in 1700 and Richard Burridge's Latin in 1701; John Wynne's student abridgment, made with Locke's approval, in 1696 (all DNB; the SEP dates Coste to 1704, see Doubts). Leibniz's chapter-by-chapter reply, the *Nouveaux essais*, was written in 1704 and published only in 1765 (DNB).

### The four books

Book I, "Neither Principles nor Ideas are Innate", clears the ground (Week 1, Argument 1). Book II, "Of Ideas", builds the inventory: simple ideas and the qualities that cause them (II.i–viii), the operations of the mind (II.ix–xi), complex ideas, modes, substances and relations (II.xii–xxviii), and the late chapter on association (II.xxxiii) (Weeks 2–4, Arguments 2–7). Book III, "Of Words", treats words as signs of ideas and diagnoses their abuse (Week 4, Arguments 8–9). Book IV, on knowledge and probability, defines knowledge as the perception of the agreement of ideas, sets its degrees and extent, and ends with faith, reason and enthusiasm (Week 5, Arguments 10–12). The study guide covers Book II in depth and maps the rest; the dossier reads III and IV properly. **Difficulty:** moderate, high in II.xxi and IV.iii; the prose is plain but the qualifications arrive late. **Place:** Weeks 1–5.

### The free text: what Project Gutenberg #10615–10616 is

The dossier quotes the *Essay* from Gutenberg's two-volume transcription, saved as `essay-gutenberg-10615.txt` (Books I–II) and `essay-gutenberg-10616.txt` (Books III–IV). Its header names no copy-text. Four facts, checked in the saved files:

1. **Title page and text do not match.** It reproduces the 1690 title page ("Humane Understanding", Holt for Basset, MDCXC), but includes the 1694 chapter on identity, the 1700 chapter on association and the Epistle's notes for later editions. It is a posthumous printing in modern spelling.
2. **One chapter and two sections are missing.** Three slots carry only the note "[not in early editions]": II.viii.10, II.xxi.57 in its own numbering, and the whole of IV.xix, "Of Enthusiasm". The dossier quotes IV.xix from the 1853 printing (Troutman & Hayes) transcribed on Wikisource, saved as `essay-IV-xix-wikisource-1853.wiki`. It is a transcription of a nineteenth-century printing, not of Locke's text, so check every quotation from it word by word.
3. **Its numbering departs from Nidditch's in II.xxi and II.xxvii.** In II.xxvii it runs two ahead from the definition of a person and has no §24: Nidditch's II.xxvii.9 ("a thinking intelligent being, that has reason and reflection") is its §11, and Nidditch's §26 ("Person a forensic Term") its §28. In II.xxi it runs one ahead by the sections on suspension (Nidditch's §47 is its §48) and ends at §75, where Nidditch has 73 (study guide).
4. **Modern spelling, old italics.** Words printed in italics in the original appear in capitals ("from EXPERIENCE"). The dossier keeps them as the saved text has them.

## *Two Treatises of Government* (1690; corrected editions to 1764)

### Composition

The *Two Treatises* came out anonymously in 1689 (SEP; IEP; library entry), probably with 1690 on the title page (from memory; logged unchecked), which is the date this dossier's plan uses; the DNB says early 1690. The Preface presents the book as a defense of a revolution just completed: "thou hast here the beginning and end of a discourse concerning government" (TT, Preface), the middle having been lost, and what remains should suffice to "establish the throne of our great restorer, our present King William" (TT, Preface). For two centuries the book was read on those terms.

Twentieth-century scholarship reversed that reading. The SEP says the book was written during the Exclusion Crisis, about 1681, and may have been meant in part to justify the armed rising Country Party leaders were planning; the IEP says it was composed much earlier than its publication. Peter Laslett's introduction to his Cambridge edition (1960) is usually credited with the reversal. The scholars still differ on date and purpose. Laslett put the main composition in 1679–80; Richard Ashcraft's *Revolutionary Politics and Locke's Two Treatises of Government* (1986), cited in the SEP, ties the *Second Treatise* to the conspiratorial politics of the early 1680s and reads it as a call to resistance (the attribution of the reversal and Laslett's dates are from memory; logged unchecked). A book written to justify a planned rising reads differently on resistance from one written to vindicate a settled revolution, which is why the dating matters for Week 7; [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md) takes it up.

### The *First Treatise*: against Filmer

The *First Treatise* answers Sir Robert Filmer's *Patriarcha*, published posthumously in 1680 (DNB; SEP), which derived royal power from Adam's God-given dominion. In eleven chapters Locke takes apart Adam's supposed title by creation, by donation, by Eve's subjection and by fatherhood, and then the impossibility of identifying Adam's heir. The Preface sets the tone: "there was never so much glib nonsense put together in well-sounding English" (TT, Preface). Most readers skip the book, but it secures the premise the *Second Treatise* opens with (ST §1 sums up its conclusions in four points): no one has natural authority over another by fatherhood or by divine donation. **Difficulty:** low in argument, high in patience. **Place:** Week 6, in selection: chs. 1–4 (including §42 on charity, which the SEP cites) and §§86–92, where Locke founds property in the creatures on God's having planted in man a desire for self-preservation (FT §86); the selection is the dossier's. Hobbes's view that dominion arises from consent or conquest, not generation, is the other side of the quarrel ([Hobbes's Argument 13](../thomas-hobbes/04-arguments.md)).

### The *Second Treatise*

The *Second Treatise*, *An Essay concerning the True Original, Extent, and End of Civil Government*, has nineteen chapters and 243 sections, in the order of the dossier's political arguments: political power (ch. 1), the state of nature and of war (chs. 2–3), slavery (ch. 4), property (ch. 5), paternal power (ch. 6), political society and consent (chs. 7–8), the ends of government (ch. 9), forms of commonwealth and the legislative, executive and federative powers (chs. 10–13), prerogative (ch. 14), the kinds of power compared (ch. 15), conquest, usurpation and tyranny (chs. 16–18), and dissolution (ch. 19). The learning path sets chs. 2, 5 and 7–9; the dossier reads chs. 1–5 in Week 6 and chs. 7–19 in Week 7. **Difficulty:** low to moderate; the difficulty is in the arguments (the provisos, tacit consent), not the prose. **Place:** Weeks 6–7, Arguments 13–16. Chapter 2 is the passage most directly answering Hobbes: compare [Hobbes's Argument 6](../thomas-hobbes/04-arguments.md) and the section on Locke in the [Hobbes conversations file](../thomas-hobbes/05-conversations.md).

### The editions and the free texts

The second and third editions are dated 1694 and 1698, and in his last years Locke corrected a copy for a future edition (from memory; logged unchecked). The saved Gutenberg transcription, `second-treatise-gutenberg-7370.txt`, reproduces the front matter of the London edition of 1764, "REPRINTED, THE SIXTH TIME" (TT, title page as transcribed), whose editor says the text was collated with the three lifetime editions and has Locke's last corrections "from a Copy delivered by him to Mr. Peter Coste" (TT, 1764 editor's note), then at Christ's College, Cambridge. A *Historical Journal* article, "The Christ's Copy of John Locke's *Two Treatises of Government*" (Cambridge Core record), shows the copy is still studied. Cambridge describes Laslett's 1988 student edition as a revision of his second edition of 1970 with a reset introduction.

Two cautions. First, Gutenberg #7370 contains only the Preface and the *Second Treatise*, not the *First*. For the *First Treatise* use the Wikisource transcription of the 1764 edition, saved as `two-treatises-1764-wikisource.txt` (its *First Treatise* chapters 1–4 are scan-backed, the rest not), or the 1823 *Works*, vol. V, saved as `works-1823-vol5-archive.txt` (uncorrected OCR). Second, the Gutenberg header says the text was recovered from C. B. Macpherson's Hackett paperback (1980) and that "only the original words contained in the 1690 Locke text" are included; but the front matter it transcribes is the 1764 edition's, so the dossier treats its words as the 1764 text. Gutenberg's section heads read "Sect. 27."; in the Wikisource file the *First Treatise* has "§. 27." and the *Second* bare numbers. All are cited by section (ST §27, FT §27).

## The letters on toleration (1689–1692)

### *Epistola de Tolerantia* and Popple's *Letter* (1689)

Locke wrote the *Letter* in Latin in Holland in 1685 (SEP), addressed to Limborch (DNB); the SEP links it with the Protestant refugees crossing from France after Louis XIV revoked the Edict of Nantes that year. It was published anonymously in Latin in Holland in spring 1689, "probably by Limborch" (DNB), and William Popple's English translation followed that autumn (DNB). Popple added a preface to the reader that is his, not Locke's; it is not in the saved transcription.

The *Letter* argues from the nature of two societies. The commonwealth is "a society of men constituted only for the procuring, preserving, and advancing their own civil interests" (*Letter*, on the definition of the commonwealth); a church is a voluntary society for public worship; belief cannot be compelled, so the magistrate's force cannot save souls. It opens by calling toleration "the chief characteristic mark of the true Church" (*Letter*, opening sentence) and ends by excluding, among others, those who deny God: "Promises, covenants, and oaths, which are the bonds of human society, can have no hold upon an atheist" (*Letter*, on the limits of toleration). The SEP notes that Locke also excluded Catholics, as agents of a foreign power. **Difficulty:** low. **Place:** Week 7, Argument 16, against [Hobbes's Argument 15](../thomas-hobbes/04-arguments.md).

**Latin and English differ.** Popple's English is a contemporary translation, freer in places than the Latin (from memory; logged unchecked). J. W. Gough's translation, printed with Raymond Klibansky's Latin text (Oxford, 1968; SEP), is the scholarly English from the Latin. The dossier quotes Popple because it is the English Locke's contemporaries read and it is public domain; an argument that turns on a word should be checked against Gough or the Latin.

### The *Second* and *Third Letters* and the fragment of a *Fourth*

Jonas Proast attacked the *Letter* (DNB), arguing, in the standard summary, that moderate penalties could bring dissenters to consider truths they would otherwise ignore. Locke replied in *A Second Letter concerning Toleration* (1690) and *A Third Letter for Toleration* (1692), both signed "Philanthropus" (DNB), and began a fourth in 1704 that his death interrupted; the fragment appeared in the *Posthumous Works* of 1706 (DNB). (The DNB's works list dates the third letter 1690, its narrative 1692; the dossier follows 1692. See Doubts.) Proast's point, that force might work indirectly by making people attend to reasons, is the strongest objection to the *Letter*'s central argument (the summary of Proast is from memory; logged unchecked). **Place:** optional, with Week 7, for the objection to Argument 16. Not in the dossier's saved sources; paraphrase only.

## The works of the 1690s

### *Some Thoughts concerning Education* (1693)

The book collects advice Locke had been sending Edward Clarke since 1684 about the upbringing of his son, and the SEP adds his daughters. The DNB says Molyneux pressed Locke to publish it and that it reached a fourteenth edition by 1772. It prefers habit, example and esteem to rote and the rod. The SEP, following Ruth Grant and Nathan Tarcov, reads it with the *Conduct* as a bridge between the *Essay* and the politics: education trains the power to suspend desire described in II.xxi. **Place:** not set reading; optional with Week 3. Clarendon text: John and Jean Yolton (1989; SEP).

### *The Reasonableness of Christianity* (1695) and the *Vindications*

Published anonymously in 1695, the *Reasonableness* reads the Gospels and Acts to find what a Christian must believe and concludes, in the IEP's summary, that many beliefs traditionally held mandatory are unnecessary. The DNB says it struck the keynote of the most popular theology of the eighteenth century, deist and latitudinarian alike. John Edwards attacked it, and Locke answered in a *Vindication* (1695) and a *Second Vindication* (1697) (DNB). Whether Locke was by then anti-Trinitarian is disputed: the SEP points to his part in arranging the anonymous publication of a tract of Newton's against the Trinity, and to Ashcraft's distinction between Anglican and dissenting "rational theology". **Place:** optional, with Week 5 (Argument 12). Clarendon texts: Higgins-Biddle's *Reasonableness* (2000 in the SEP, 1999 in the IEP) and Nuovo's *Vindications* (2012; SEP).

### The Stillingfleet letters (1697)

Edward Stillingfleet, Bishop of Worcester, attacked passages of the *Essay* in a *Discourse in Vindication of the Doctrine of the Trinity*. Locke answered in *A Letter to the Right Reverend Edward, Lord Bishop of Worcester*, a *Reply* to the bishop's answer, and a *Reply* to his answer to the second letter, all dated 1697 by the DNB's list of works (its narrative dates the last reply 1699). The IEP calls it Locke's most famous and philosophically important controversy. The weight falls on substance (the SEP reports Stillingfleet's charge that Locke had put substance out of the reasonable part of the world; Argument 6), on certainty and whether knowledge by ideas leaves room for faith (Arguments 10 and 12), and on Locke's suggestion that God could give matter the power to think (E IV.iii.6) (the last two topics are from memory; logged unchecked). **Place:** optional, with Weeks 3–5, in extracts. Not in the saved sources; paraphrase only.

### Money and the Board of Trade

Locke's writings on interest (1692) and on the recoinage (1695) shaped the recoinage of 1696, and from 1696 to 1700 he was the most energetic member of the new Board of Trade (DNB). Kelly's *Locke on Money* (Clarendon, 2 vols., 1991; SEP) edits the economic writings. **Place:** not set reading. The property chapter already gives the account the dossier needs: money came in by "the tacit agreement of men to put a value on it" (ST §36), with its consequences in ST §§45–50.

## The posthumous works

***Of the Conduct of the Understanding* (1706).** Begun as a chapter for the fourth edition of the *Essay*, it grew too long, was never finished, and was published by Peter King in the *Posthumous Works* of 1706 (SEP; the DNB dates its writing about 1697). It is a manual of intellectual self-discipline, against prejudice and in favor of assent proportioned to evidence, and the SEP notes that it repeats the *Essay*'s rejection of enthusiasm. Grant and Tarcov edit it with the *Thoughts* (Hackett, 1996; SEP). **Place:** optional, with Week 5. Not in the saved sources.

**Other posthumous pieces.** The *Posthumous Works* also printed the examination of Malebranche's "seeing all things in God", *A Discourse of Miracles*, the fragment of a fourth letter on toleration and memoirs of Shaftesbury; the *Paraphrase and Notes on the Epistles of St Paul* came out in parts in 1705–7 (DNB), and Wainwright edits it for the Clarendon Edition (2 vols., 1987; SEP). The 1720 collection published under Anthony Collins's direction includes *The Fundamental Constitutions of Carolina* (DNB), in which, the SEP says, Locke and Shaftesbury instituted religious toleration in 1669. The *Carolina* constitutions matter for the debate over Locke and slavery in [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md). E. S. de Beer's *Correspondence* (8 vols., Clarendon, 1976–89, with vol. 1 in 2010; SEP) prints the letters in 3,648 numbers; those with Molyneux, Limborch and Damaris Masham matter most here, and Masham has her own place in the library's [early modern women philosophers entry](../../eras/03-early-modern/early-modern-women-philosophers.md).

## The Clarendon Edition of the Works of John Locke

Oxford's Clarendon Edition began with Nidditch's *Essay* in 1975. The SEP expects it to be definitive and to supersede the old collected *Works*, of which it calls the 1823 edition (10 vols., London: Thomas Tegg and others; IEP) probably the most standard. Published so far, as the SEP lists them: the *Essay* (Nidditch, 1975); *Drafts A and B* (Nidditch and Rogers, 1990); *Some Thoughts concerning Education* (Yolton and Yolton, 1989); *The Reasonableness of Christianity* (Higgins-Biddle); *An Essay concerning Toleration and Other Writings on Law and Politics, 1667–1683* (Milton and Milton, 2006); the *Vindications* (Nuovo, 2012); the *Paraphrase* (Wainwright, 1987); *Locke on Money* (Kelly, 1991); and the *Correspondence* (de Beer). Neither the *Two Treatises* nor the *Letter* is on the SEP's list, which is why Laslett, Goldie and Gough remain the scholarly texts for them.

## A note on citation and editions

**The *Essay*** is cited by book, chapter and section, as E II.xxvii.9, in Nidditch's numbering, with E, Epistle for the Epistle to the Reader. Where the Gutenberg text numbers a section differently (II.xxi from the sections on suspension, II.xxvii from Nidditch's §9), the dossier still cites Nidditch's number and the quote log gives both. Do not cite Gutenberg's or Fraser's numbers in those chapters without saying so.

**The *Two Treatises*** are cited by section: ST §27, FT §86, TT, Preface. The numbering is the same in the 1764 text, in Laslett and in other reliable editions; the dossier never cites by page.

**The *Letter*** has no standard section numbers. Cite it as *Letter* with a short locating phrase, never with an invented number; a page reference must name its edition.

**Spelling.** Quotations keep the saved text's spelling, never modernized and never adjusted towards Nidditch. In practice the dossier shows three spellings: the Gutenberg *Essay* in modern spelling with italics as capitals; the 1853 printing for IV.xix ("much-what"); and the 1764 *Two Treatises*, with eighteenth-century forms such as "shewn" and "inclose". Nidditch prints Locke's own spelling ("perswade", in the SEP's quotation of IV.xix.4), so a quotation here will often look more modern than the same words in the reading edition. The difference comes from the editors of the free texts, not from Locke.

**Reading editions (the dossier's rulings).** The learning path names Nidditch or Winkler for the *Essay* and Laslett for the *Two Treatises*; the brief adds Tully or Goldie for the *Letter*. The dossier keeps all of these and rules as follows.

- ***Essay*:** Peter H. Nidditch (ed.), *An Essay Concerning Human Understanding* (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1975), the fourth-edition text with the other editions' variants recorded (study guide; SEP), and the edition the scholarship cites. Or Kenneth P. Winkler (ed.), abridged (Hackett, 1996), which keeps everything the study guide covers, including II.xxi and II.xxvii (study guide). Because it is abridged, check that Weeks 4–5's chapters (III.vi, IV.iii, IV.xi, IV.xv–xix) are in it before relying on it, and use Nidditch or the free texts for any that are not (Winkler's contents beyond the study guide's selections are not checked here; logged).
- ***Two Treatises*:** Peter Laslett (ed.), student edition (Cambridge Texts in the History of Political Thought, 1988), which Cambridge describes as a revision of the 1970 second edition with a reset introduction. Read the *First Treatise* selections in it for Week 6.
- ***Letter*:** Mark Goldie (ed.), *A Letter concerning Toleration and Other Writings* (Indianapolis: Liberty Fund, 2010; Online Library of Liberty record), which Liberty Fund describes as the first fully annotated modern edition and which adds other writings on toleration. Or James H. Tully (ed.), *A Letter Concerning Toleration* (Hackett), short and inexpensive (its date, 1983, and its use of Popple's text are from memory; logged unchecked). For the Latin, Klibansky and Gough (Oxford, 1968; SEP).
- **Early works:** Mark Goldie (ed.), *Locke: Political Essays* (Cambridge Texts, 1997), which the Cambridge catalogue describes as five major essays and seventy shorter ones, including the Second Tract in Abrams's translation (Cambridge headnote).
- ***Thoughts* and *Conduct*:** Ruth W. Grant and Nathan Tarcov (eds.) (Hackett, 1996; SEP).

**Avoid** any free *Essay* built from the Gutenberg text without saying that IV.xix is missing; editions of the *Second Treatise* that drop the Preface without saying so (the dating question starts there); citations of the *Essay* by page without an edition; and treating Popple's English as Locke's own words where it departs from the Latin.

## The works in the dossier's reading order

The [learning path](../../learning-path.md) sets *Essay* Book II (II.i–viii, II.xxi, II.xxvii) with Book I as a summary pass, and *Second Treatise* chs. 2, 5 and 7–9, in Stage 3, row 7. The dossier keeps that core and widens it to seven weeks, about 40 hours all-in, adding Books III and IV, the rest of the *Second Treatise*, the *First Treatise* in selection and the *Letter*; [07-study-plan.md](07-study-plan.md) gives the split.

1. **Week 1, life; against innate ideas:** the Epistle to the Reader; *Essay* I.i–iv, a summary pass with I.ii read closely. Argument 1. The Epistle also records the edition history: the 1688 epitome, the 1694 notice of II.xxi and II.xxvii, the 1700 notice of II.xxxiii and IV.xix.
2. **Week 2, ideas and qualities:** II.i–viii, II.xii; Molyneux's problem, II.ix.8. Arguments 2–4, with the study guide's §§1.2–1.5.
3. **Week 3, power, freedom, substance:** II.xxi, II.xxiii. Arguments 5–6. Read II.xxi as the 1694 rewrite, and look at Nidditch's first-edition variants to see what Locke gave up. Compare [Hobbes's Argument 16](../thomas-hobbes/04-arguments.md) against Bramhall.
4. **Week 4, personal identity; words and essences:** II.xxvii; III.ii–iii, III.vi. Arguments 7–9. In the free text, II.xxvii runs two sections ahead of Nidditch from §9.
5. **Week 5, knowledge and its limits; faith and enthusiasm:** IV.i–iv, IV.xi, IV.xv–xix. Arguments 10–12. IV.xix is missing from the Gutenberg text: read it in Nidditch or in the 1853 printing on Wikisource, where the telescope image begins "he that takes away reason, to make way for revelation, puts out the light of both" (E IV.xix.4). The *Conduct* and the *Reasonableness* are optional companions.
6. **Week 6, state of nature and property:** *Second Treatise* chs. 1–5; *First Treatise* in selection (chs. 1–4, §§86–92). Arguments 13–14. Read ST ch. 2 against [Hobbes's Argument 6](../thomas-hobbes/04-arguments.md).
7. **Week 7, consent, resistance, toleration; capstone:** *Second Treatise* chs. 7–19 (7–9 closely, the rest in selection); the *Letter*. Arguments 15–16, then the capstone essay. Keep the Exclusion Crisis dating and the Proast exchange in mind.
