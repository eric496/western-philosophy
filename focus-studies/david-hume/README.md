# Focus Study: David Hume (1711 – 1776)

*Portrait of David Hume by Allan Ramsay, 1766. Image via Wikimedia Commons.*

## The figure in brief

Hume was born in Edinburgh in 1711, the younger son of a minor laird, and grew up at Ninewells in the Borders. He went to Edinburgh University at about ten, left without a degree, and turned from the law to philosophy. At eighteen, by his own account, "a new Scene of Thought" opened to him, and the strain of pursuing it brought on years of illness. He wrote *A Treatise of Human Nature* in France, at La Flèche, and published it in 1739–40; it "fell dead-born from the press", as he put it in *My Own Life*. He then recast it in shorter, more readable works: the *Philosophical Essays concerning Human Understanding* (1748, retitled *An Enquiry* in 1758), the *Enquiry concerning the Principles of Morals* (1751), and the *Essays*. His reputation for irreligion cost him chairs at Edinburgh and Glasgow. He made his living as a tutor, secretary, librarian, diplomat and Under-Secretary of State, and his *History of England* (1754–62) made him famous. In Paris he was celebrated as "le bon David"; his quarrel with Rousseau in 1766 became a public scandal. He spent his last years in Edinburgh's New Town, revising the *Dialogues concerning Natural Religion*, which appeared in 1779, three years after his death on 25 August 1776.

The ideas this library keeps meeting begin with him or take their modern form in his hands: the copy principle, the division of knowledge into relations of ideas and matters of fact, the problem of induction, causation as constant conjunction and the mind's expectation, the self as a bundle of perceptions, the argument against miracles, the case against the design argument, reason as "the slave of the passions", the gap between *is* and *ought*, and justice as an artificial virtue.

## Why he rewards weeks of study

First, *the questions*: Hume's problems about induction, causation and the self are still open, and most of the replies to them still begin from his statement of them. Reading him closely shows how much of the debate he anticipated. Second, *the two versions*: Hume wrote his philosophy twice, in the *Treatise* and in the two *Enquiries*, and comparing them shows what he kept, what he cut, and what he changed his mind about, most famously in the *Treatise*'s Appendix on personal identity. Third, *the reach*: he is the philosopher Kant said woke him from his "dogmatic slumber", the ancestor of utilitarianism, logical positivism and modern naturalism, and the critic every later defence of natural theology has had to answer. Reading him is reading the end point of Stage 3's empiricist line and the starting point of Kant.

## The study plan at a glance

The library's [learning path](../../learning-path.md) meets Hume in **Stage 3** (Early Modern to Kant) and fixes the order: the first *Enquiry* whole, then the *Dialogues*, then the second *Enquiry* in selection, and the *Treatise* only after the *Enquiry*. This dossier keeps the path's editions and its rule, pairing each *Enquiry* section with the *Treatise* passage it recasts only after the *Enquiry* has been read. [07-study-plan.md](07-study-plan.md) lays out an **8-week plan** at the path's weekly rhythm:

| Week | Reading | Theme |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | *My Own Life*; EHU 1–3; T Introduction, T 1.1 | Ideas and their origin |
| 2 | EHU 4–5; T 1.3.6–8; the *Abstract* | The fork, induction, custom, belief |
| 3 | EHU 6–8; T 1.3.14; T 2.3.1–2 | Probability, necessary connexion, liberty |
| 4 | EHU 12; T 1.4.1–2, 1.4.6–7; the Appendix | Scepticism, body and the self |
| 5 | EHU 9–11; DNR 1–5 | Animals, miracles, the *Dialogues* opened |
| 6 | DNR 6–12; *Natural History of Religion* selections | Design, evil, Philo's reversal |
| 7 | T 2.3.3; T 3.1–3.3 selections | Reason and the passions, is and ought, justice |
| 8 | Second *Enquiry* selections; "Of the Standard of Taste"; "Of the Original Contract"; capstone | Utility, taste, government, synthesis |

Weeks 1–5 also use the library's [study guide to the first *Enquiry*](../../study/hume-enquiry.md), section by section.

The honest total is about **47 hours: 36 at the desk, about 4 of assigned media, and about 7.5 of daily card review**, with weekly loads of about 4.9 to 7.2 hours. These hours sit on top of Stage 3's own budget rather than inside it; the plan file states the net addition to the stage and the combined cost if you also run the [Descartes](../rene-descartes/README.md) or [Kant](../immanuel-kant/README.md) dossiers.

## On the quotations

Hume wrote in English, so every quotation of Hume is his own wording, in his own eighteenth-century spelling, taken from Peter Millican and Amyas Merivale's [Hume Texts Online](https://davidhume.org/). Citations use the Clarendon paragraph references printed there and in the modern Oxford editions: *Treatise* by book, part, section and paragraph (T 1.3.6.11), the *Enquiries* by section and paragraph (EHU 4.18, EPM 9.22), the *Dialogues* by part and paragraph (DNR 12.1). Any edition with these numbers will let you find a passage; the older Selby-Bigge/Nidditch editions lack them.
