# Hume in Conversation — Backward, Sideways, Forward
**English** | [简体中文](../../zh/focus-studies/david-hume/05-conversations.md)

*Focus study: David Hume · [Dossier home](README.md) · [Library entry](../../eras/03-early-modern/david-hume.md) · [Study guide](../../study/hume-enquiry.md)*

Hume called the *Treatise* a failure that "fell dead-born from the press" (MOL 6). The record shows a book that was argued with from the start. Hume wrote it after reading Bayle, Malebranche, Locke and Berkeley, and he named his British models in its Introduction. Its reception cost him two university chairs. Its recastings drew replies from the ministers and professors of the Scottish Enlightenment, and Kant built his critical philosophy on one of its problems. This file traces those exchanges. The scholarly controversies and the reception by era are in [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md).

Hume is quoted from *Hume Texts Online* (Millican and Merivale), in his original spelling, and cited by the Clarendon paragraph numbers printed in the recommended editions: *Treatise* (T), first *Enquiry* (EHU), second *Enquiry* (EPM), *Dialogues* (DNR), *Abstract*, *My Own Life* (MOL). His letters (Greig's edition) are paraphrased.

## Backward: what he inherited and what he refused

### The ancient sceptics and Cicero

Hume's scepticism has two ancient sources, and he kept them apart. The [Pyrrhonists](../../eras/01-ancient/pyrrho-of-elis-and-the-skeptics.md), known to him through Sextus Empiricus and Bayle, suspended judgment on everything. Hume's chief objection is practical: "Nature is always too strong for principle" (EHU 12.23). The Academic sceptics of Plato's later Academy, known to him mainly through [Cicero](../../eras/01-ancient/cicero-and-lucretius.md), weighed probabilities instead. Hume adopted their name for his own position: "a more mitigated scepticism or academical philosophy, which may be both durable and useful" (EHU 12.24).

Cicero was a model in ethics and in form. Hume told Francis Hutcheson that he took his catalogue of virtues from Cicero's *De officiis*, not from the devotional manual *The Whole Duty of Man* (letter of 16 March 1740, Greig). The *Dialogues* follow Cicero's *De natura deorum*, a dialogue on the gods with a Stoic, an Epicurean and an Academic sceptic. In both works the sceptic has the sharpest lines, and in both the narrator ends by preferring someone else (DNR 12.34; compare *De natura deorum* III.95). This is week 6 ([04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 11).

### Bayle

Pierre Bayle's *Historical and Critical Dictionary* (1697) was the arsenal of eighteenth-century scepticism ([Bayle and Voltaire](../../eras/03-early-modern/bayle-and-voltaire.md)). In August 1737 Hume told his friend Michael Ramsay to prepare for the metaphysics of the *Treatise* by reading Malebranche, Berkeley, Bayle's articles on Zeno and Spinoza, and Descartes' *Meditations*. The *Treatise* sends readers to "Bayle's dictionary, article of Spinoza" (T 1.4.5.22n47). In the first *Enquiry* Bayle is the standard of comparison for a sceptic: Berkeley's writings "form the best lessons of scepticism, which are to be found either among the ancient or modern philosophers, Bayle not excepted" (EHU 12.15n32).

### Malebranche and the Cartesians on causation

[Nicolas Malebranche](../../eras/03-early-modern/nicolas-malebranche.md) gave Hume the negative half of his account of causation. Malebranche argued that we perceive no necessary connection between a created cause and its effect, so God is the only true cause. The *Treatise* footnotes the relevant chapter, "Father Malbranche, Book vi. Part 2. chap. 3." (T 1.3.14.7n29). Hume accepted the premise and rejected the conclusion. We have no impression of power in God either, and occasionalism makes God weaker: "It argues surely more power in the Deity to delegate a certain degree of power to inferior creatures" (EHU 7.22). His note on the history of the doctrine reads: "Des Cartes insinuated that doctrine of the universal and sole efficacy of the Deity, without insisting on it. Malebranche and other Cartesians made it the foundation of all their philosophy" (EHU 7.25n16). Hume's argument is week 3 ([04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 5). The [Descartes dossier](../rene-descartes/05-conversations.md) tells the story from the other end, including Hume's verdict that Cartesian doubt "would be entirely incurable" (EHU 12.3).

When the Edinburgh accusers of 1745 read the *Treatise* as denying that God is the first cause, Hume replied that the passage concerned "this Cartesian Doctrine, of secondary Causes" (*Letter from a Gentleman* 33).

### Locke

[John Locke](../../eras/03-early-modern/john-locke.md) heads the list of predecessors in the *Treatise* Introduction (T 0.7n1). Locke used *idea* for any content of the mind. Hume split it into impressions and ideas and made the copy principle the test of meaning (week 1, [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 1). He read Locke's attack on innate ideas as a confused version of that principle, and blamed the confusion on scholastic habits: "Locke was betrayed into this question by the schoolmen" (EHU 2.9n1.3). Locke's chapter on power is where Hume's inquiry into necessary connexion begins (EHU 7.8n12). In politics Hume turned against Locke. "Of the Original Contract" (1748) denies that any actual government rests on the consent of the governed (paragraphs 7–9; week 8).

### Berkeley on abstract ideas

[George Berkeley](../../eras/03-early-modern/george-berkeley.md) is the predecessor Hume praised most warmly. Berkeley had argued in 1710 that there are no abstract general ideas, only particular ideas made general by their use with a word. Hume called this "one of the greatest and most valuable discoveries that has been made of late years in the republic of letters" (T 1.1.7.1). He added a mechanism, custom, by which a particular idea stands for others like it. On the external world Hume read Berkeley against Berkeley's intentions. Berkeley meant to refute the sceptics and the atheists, but his arguments "admit of no answer and produce no conviction" (EHU 12.15n32). Hume borrowed from him the case against the primary and secondary quality distinction (EHU 12.15–16; week 4, [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 7).

### Newton's method

The *Treatise* is subtitled "Being an Attempt to introduce the experimental Method of Reasoning into Moral Subjects." The model was Newton, who explained many effects by a few principles and declined to speculate about hidden causes; association plays the part of attraction in the mind. In the second *Enquiry* Hume justifies generalizing from justice to the other social virtues by appeal to "Newton's chief rule of philosophizing" (EPM 3.48), the second of the *Principia*'s Rules of Reasoning: assign the same causes to the same natural effects. He also defended Newton against Newtonians who denied all power to matter: "It was never the meaning of Sir Isaac Newton to rob second causes of all force or energy" (EHU 7.25n16).

### Shaftesbury, Hutcheson, Butler, Mandeville

The rest of Hume's list in the Introduction is British moral philosophy: "Mr. Locke, my Lord Shaftsbury, Dr. Mandeville, Mr. Hutchinson, Dr. Butler, &c." (T 0.7n1). The *Abstract* says they differ among themselves but "seem all to agree in founding their accurate disquisitions of human nature entirely upon experience" (*Abstract* 2).

The [moral sense school](../../eras/03-early-modern/moral-sense-school.md) of Shaftesbury, Hutcheson and Butler shaped Book 3 most. Shaftesbury had said that we approve of characters by an inner sense, and Hume credited him with first noticing the conflict between reason and sentiment in ethics (EPM 1.4). Hutcheson made the moral sense a faculty and argued against both Hobbes's egoism and the rationalists. Hume's section title, "Moral distinctions deriv'd from a moral sense" (T 3.1.2), is Hutcheson's thesis. His explanation of it, through sympathy and a general point of view, is his own (week 7, [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 14).

Joseph Butler's *Fifteen Sermons* (1726) had argued that self-love and benevolence are distinct principles. Hume used this against Hobbes and Mandeville (EPM App. 2). In 1737 he told Henry Home (later Lord Kames) that he was cutting the "nobler parts" of the *Treatise*, the reasoning on miracles among them, to give Butler as little offence as possible (letter of 2 December 1737, Greig). The miracles argument waited for the first *Enquiry*.

Bernard Mandeville's *Fable of the Bees* (1714, expanded 1723) argued that the virtues were invented by "skilful politicians" to tame selfish animals. Hume agreed that justice is artificial (week 7, [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 15). He denied that moral feeling is artificial. Politicians can assist the sentiments, but "nothing can be more evident, than that the matter has been carry'd too far by certain writers on morals, who seem to have employ'd their utmost efforts to extirpate all sense of virtue from among mankind" (T 3.2.2.25).

### Hobbes

[Thomas Hobbes](../../eras/03-early-modern/thomas-hobbes.md) appears in Hume mostly in footnotes and mostly as an opponent, for instance as one who tried to prove that every beginning of existence needs a cause (T 1.3.3.4n17). Hume classed him with Locke as a holder of "the selfish system of morals," and added that Hobbes lived well without "any restraint of religion, which might supply the defects of his philosophy" (EPM App2.3). On liberty the two agreed: freedom is acting according to one's will, and it is compatible with necessity (week 3, [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 6).

### Clarke and Wollaston: moral rationalism

Samuel Clarke's Boyle Lectures (1704–05) held that reason perceives eternal fitnesses of things that bind God as well as men. William Wollaston's *Religion of Nature Delineated* (1722) held that a wrong act is a practical lie. *Treatise* 3.1.1 is aimed at both. It opens by paraphrasing Clarke, on those who affirm "that there are eternal fitnesses and unfitnesses of things, which are the same to every rational being that considers them" (T 3.1.1.4). Wollaston is "a late author, who has had the good fortune to obtain some reputation" (T 3.1.1.15n68), and Hume answers him with the example (T 3.1.1.15) of a man seen through a window with his neighbour's wife: the act may cause a false belief, but its wickedness lies elsewhere. The is/ought paragraph closes the same section (T 3.1.1.27; [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 14).

## Sideways: friends, critics and clergy

### Hutcheson's verdict on Book 3

In 1739 Hume sent Book 3 in manuscript to Francis Hutcheson, professor of moral philosophy at Glasgow. Hutcheson's complaint survives through Hume's reply: the book lacked a certain warmth in the cause of virtue (letter of 17 September 1739, Greig). Hume answered that one can study the mind as an anatomist or as a painter, and that he had chosen the anatomist's part. The image reached print: "The anatomist ought never to emulate the painter" (T 3.3.6.6). The first *Enquiry* opens with the same contrast (EHU 1.1–8). In 1745 Hutcheson did not support Hume's candidacy for the chair of moral philosophy at Edinburgh, and Hume complained of this in his letters (Mossner, *Life*).

### The Edinburgh clergy, Warburton and Hurd

In 1745 the Principal of Edinburgh University, William Wishart, circulated extracts from the *Treatise* charging scepticism and atheism. Hume's answer was printed by Kames as *A Letter from a Gentleman to his Friend in Edinburgh* (1745). It answers six charges, among them that the *Treatise* denied "That whatever begins to exist must have a Cause of Existence" (*Letter* 26). Hume replied that he had denied only that the maxim is intuitively or demonstratively certain. In 1751–52 Hume failed again, at Glasgow, for the chair of logic. In 1755 and 1756 members of the General Assembly of the Church of Scotland moved to censure him for infidel writings. Moderate clergy, Hugh Blair and William Robertson among them, blocked it in committee (Mossner, *Life*). His kinsman Kames came under attack at the same time for his *Essays on the Principles of Morality and Natural Religion* (1751), although that book defended a moral sense and natural justice against Hume while accepting his doctrine of necessity.

The English attack came from the circle of William Warburton, Bishop of Gloucester. In 1757 Richard Hurd, with Warburton, attacked *The Natural History of Religion* in a pamphlet that Hume said was written "with all the illiberal petulance, arrogance, and scurrility, which distinguish the Warburtonian school" (MOL 13).

### Adam Smith

[Adam Smith](../../eras/03-early-modern/adam-smith.md) met Hume around 1750 and became his closest friend. *The Theory of Moral Sentiments* (1759) builds on Hume's sympathy and changes it. For Hume sympathy communicates feelings by association. For Smith it is an imaginative change of places, and conscience is an imagined impartial spectator. Smith also argued against Hume that utility is seldom the first ground of our approval of virtue, which is usually a sense of its propriety (*TMS* IV.2).

At the end Smith stood beside him. Hume named him in 1776 to see the *Dialogues* into print, and Smith, fearing the outcry, declined. The *Dialogues* appeared in 1779 through Hume's nephew. Smith's letter to William Strahan of 9 November 1776, printed with *My Own Life*, judged the dying Hume as close to perfect wisdom and virtue as human frailty allows.

### Reid and the Common Sense school

[Thomas Reid](../../eras/03-early-modern/thomas-reid.md) said the *Treatise* woke him from Berkeley's idealism. In 1763 Hugh Blair sent Hume the manuscript of Reid's *Inquiry into the Human Mind* (1764). Hume replied courteously, and Reid called himself Hume's disciple in metaphysics. Reid's thesis was that Hume's conclusions follow validly from the "Way of Ideas," the view shared from Descartes to Hume that the mind's immediate objects are its own ideas. The conclusions being absurd, Reid rejected the premise for direct realism and first principles of common sense. In the *Essays on the Active Powers* (1788) he derived the idea of power from our own agency and defended justice as a natural virtue ([04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 15). Norman Kemp Smith's reading (1941), which makes Hume himself a naturalist about common belief, is traced in [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md).

James Beattie's *Essay on the Nature and Immutability of Truth* (1770) made the same case loudly. Hume answered indirectly. In 1775 he sent his publisher an "Advertisement" for his *Essays and Treatises* that disowned the *Treatise* as a juvenile work. In the covering letter to Strahan he called it a complete answer to Reid and to Beattie, whom he called bigoted and silly (letter of 26 October 1775, Greig).

### Price

Richard Price, a Welsh Dissenting minister in London, wrote the most careful rationalist reply. His *Review of the Principal Questions in Morals* (1758) argued that right and wrong are simple ideas of the understanding, like necessity and equality, which the copy principle cannot explain either. In *Four Dissertations* (1767) he argued against EHU 10 that an event's prior improbability lowers the credibility of good testimony less than Hume supposed, a point taken up in the Bayesian discussions of [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md).

### Campbell on miracles

George Campbell, later Principal of Marischal College, Aberdeen, answered EHU 10 in *A Dissertation on Miracles* (1762). Trust in testimony, he argued, is an original disposition, not learned from experience of its reliability, so experience of nature cannot simply be set against it. Hume saw the manuscript through Hugh Blair and declined to reply in print (letters of 1761–62, Greig). Campbell's view of testimony as evidence in its own right anticipates modern epistemology of testimony (week 5, [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 10).

### Rousseau

Hume brought [Jean-Jacques Rousseau](../../eras/03-early-modern/jean-jacques-rousseau.md), then a fugitive from Switzerland, to England in January 1766. Within months Rousseau decided that Hume was plotting against him, and Hume published his side as the *Exposé succinct* (Paris, 1766). The two also disagreed in principle. Rousseau made natural pity the root of morality and the social contract the measure of legitimate government. Hume grounded morality in sympathy refined by society and denied that any actual government rests on a contract ("Of the Original Contract" 7–9).

## Forward: the heirs and the critics

### Kant

[Kant](../../eras/03-early-modern/immanuel-kant.md) supplied the most quoted tribute. The Preface to the *Prolegomena* (1783) says: "the suggestion of David Hume was the very thing, which many years ago first interrupted my dogmatic slumber" (Carus translation). The same preface names "Reid, Oswald, Beattie, and lastly Priestley" as critics who missed the point. Kant generalized Hume's question. If the necessity in the principle that every event has a cause cannot come from experience, the same holds for every concept by which the understanding connects things a priori. The *Critique of Pure Reason* makes such concepts conditions of any possible experience (week 2, [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 3).

What Kant read, and when, is disputed. The first *Enquiry* had a German translation in 1755. The *Treatise* had none until 1790–92. In 1771 Hamann ([Hamann and Herder](../../eras/03-early-modern/hamann-and-herder.md)) published a German version of the conclusion of *Treatise* Book 1 (T 1.4.7) in a Königsberg newspaper, and Manfred Kuehn has argued that this, rather than the *Enquiry*, was the spark. The [Kant dossier](../immanuel-kant/05-conversations.md) treats the dispute from Kant's side.

### Bentham and Mill

[Jeremy Bentham](../../eras/04-19th-century/jeremy-bentham.md) took utility from Hume's moral writings. In a note to chapter 1 of *A Fragment on Government* (1776) he wrote that reading Book 3 of the *Treatise* made him feel as if scales had fallen from his eyes, and that he learned there to call the cause of the people the cause of virtue. Utilitarianism kept Hume's test of usefulness and dropped his psychology of approval.

[John Stuart Mill](../../eras/04-19th-century/john-stuart-mill.md) took up Hume's problem in *A System of Logic* (1843). He admits that every induction assumes the uniformity of nature, and treats that assumption as itself the widest induction, which critics call Hume's circle. In the posthumous essay "Theism" (1874) he judged the design argument the only one with real scientific weight, a conclusion Cleanthes would have welcomed.

### Darwin and Huxley

Philo had proposed that order might arise without design, since matter given eternity must try every arrangement (DNR 8.2). He describes nature as "a blind Nature, impregnated by a great vivifying principle, and pouring forth from her lap, without discernment or parental care, her maimed and abortive children" (DNR 11.13). He had no mechanism. Charles Darwin's *On the Origin of Species* (1859) supplied one, natural selection, and Darwin wrote in his *Autobiography* that Paley's design argument failed once that law was found (week 6, [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Arguments 11 and 12).

Thomas Henry Huxley, who coined the word "agnostic" in 1869, wrote *Hume* (1879) for the English Men of Letters series. He made Hume the ancestor of scientific naturalism, and faulted Hume's definition of a miracle as a violation of a law of nature, since laws are only summaries of what we have so far observed.

### Logical positivism and Ayer

The [Vienna Circle](../../eras/05-20th-century/vienna-circle-logical-positivism.md) took the fork of EHU 4.1 and the closing paragraph of the first *Enquiry*, which commits to the flames any book with neither "abstract reasoning concerning quantity or number" nor "experimental reasoning concerning matter of fact and existence" (EHU 12.34). A. J. Ayer's *Language, Truth and Logic* (1936) traced its views to Berkeley and Hume in its preface and made the fork a criterion of meaning: a sentence means something only if it is analytic or empirically verifiable. Ayer's emotivism, which says moral judgments express feeling and state no facts, is a sharpened form of Hume's sentimentalism (week 2, [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 2). Many historians doubt that Hume meant the fork as a theory of meaning.

### Popper

[Karl Popper](../../eras/05-20th-century/karl-popper.md) accepted Hume's logical result and rejected his psychology. In *Logik der Forschung* (1934; English 1959) and *Conjectures and Refutations* (1963) he agreed that no number of observations proves a universal law, and denied that we learn by repetition. Science proceeds by conjecture and attempted refutation, and needs no inductive inference. Critics such as Wesley Salmon replied that relying on the best-tested theory for the next prediction is induction under another name.

### Goodman and Quine

Nelson Goodman's *Fact, Fiction, and Forecast* (1955) set Hume's problem aside as dissolved and posed a "new riddle of induction." An emerald is "grue" if examined before some future date and green, or not so examined and blue. Every emerald observed so far is both green and grue, yet only one prediction seems warranted. Goodman credited Hume with the right kind of answer, a description of which regularities the mind projects, and supplied his own: the entrenchment of predicates in past usage.

[W. V. O. Quine](../../eras/05-20th-century/w-v-o-quine.md)'s "Epistemology Naturalized" (1969) accepted Hume's sceptical result about justifying belief in bodies and in the future. He replaced the search for foundations with a scientific study of how the mind gets from stimulation to theory, close to Hume's science of man (T Intro 4–6). His "Natural Kinds" (1969) met Goodman's riddle with an innate sense of similarity refined by evolution and science.

### Mackie and Blackburn

J. L. Mackie wrote on both of Hume's main topics, in *The Cement of the Universe* (1974) and *Hume's Moral Theory* (1980). His *Ethics: Inventing Right and Wrong* (1977) argued that moral judgments claim an objective authority that nothing in the world has, so that they are systematically false. He traced this error theory to Hume's projectivism.

Simon Blackburn accepted the projection and rejected the error. His *Spreading the Mind* (1984) takes its title from the *Treatise*: "the mind has a great propensity to spread itself on external objects" (T 1.3.14.25). Quasi-realism holds that moral judgments express attitudes, and that we may still call them true and argue about them. Blackburn is also a leading opponent of the New Hume reading of causation ("Hume and Thick Connexions," 1990; see [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md)).

### Parfit

[Derek Parfit](../../eras/05-20th-century/derek-parfit.md)'s *Reasons and Persons* (1984), Part III, revived the bundle view of T 1.4.6 under the name "reductionism." Identity consists in physical and psychological continuity and nothing further, and Parfit drew a conclusion Hume did not: identity is not what matters in survival. Hume himself had complained in the Appendix that he could not render two principles consistent, "that all our distinct perceptions are distinct existences, and that the mind never perceives any real connexion among distinct existences" (T App. 21). The Appendix problem is week 4 ([04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 8).

## The themes trace

The library's [problem index](../../themes.md) places Hume in seven of its nine threads.

- [§1 Knowledge & Skepticism](../../themes.md#1-knowledge--skepticism--what-can-we-know-and-how-do-we-know-it) — Induction without rational warrant, belief by custom, mitigated scepticism. Weeks 2 and 4; Bayle behind, Reid, Kant and Popper ahead.
- [§2 Mind & Body](../../themes.md#2-mind--body--what-is-mind-and-how-does-it-relate-to-the-body) — No impression of mental substance; the immateriality question has no distinct meaning. Week 4; Locke and Berkeley behind, Reid ahead.
- [§3 God & Religion](../../themes.md#3-god--religion--does-god-exist-and-what-follows-for-faith-and-reason) — Miracles, design and evil. Weeks 5 and 6; Cicero behind, Campbell and Darwin ahead.
- [§4 Ethics & the Good Life](../../themes.md#4-ethics--the-good-life--how-should-i-live-and-what-is-right) — Morals from sentiment, reason the slave of the passions, is/ought. Weeks 7 and 8; Hutcheson and Clarke behind, Smith, Price and Blackburn ahead.
- [§5 Politics & Justice](../../themes.md#5-politics--justice--how-should-we-live-together-and-who-may-rule) — Justice as convention, government on opinion, against the original contract. Weeks 7 and 8; Mandeville and Locke behind, Rousseau and Bentham ahead.
- [§6 Self & Identity](../../themes.md#6-self--identity--what-am-i-and-what-makes-me-the-same-person-over-time) — The bundle and the Appendix second thoughts. Week 4; Locke behind, Reid and Parfit ahead.
- [§8 Science & Method](../../themes.md#8-science--method--how-should-inquiry-proceed-and-what-does-it-prove) — The experimental method in moral subjects; induction as the problem of scientific inference. Weeks 1–3; Newton behind, Goodman and Quine ahead.

He is absent from §7 (Language & Meaning) and §9 (History & Progress). Both absences are filing decisions. The theory of abstract ideas (T 1.1.7) is a theory of general words, and the fork became a criterion of meaning in Ayer's hands. Hume wrote a six-volume *History of England* and an essay on the rise and progress of the arts and sciences.
