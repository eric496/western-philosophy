# Hume in Conversation — Backward, Sideways, Forward
**English** | [简体中文](../../zh/focus-studies/david-hume/05-conversations.md)

*Focus study: David Hume · [Dossier home](README.md) · [Library entry](../../eras/03-early-modern/david-hume.md)*

Hume wrote as if in a seminar that had been running for a century before he entered it. He took his vocabulary from Locke, his sharpest single lesson from Berkeley, his opponents' confidence from Descartes, and his moral starting point from the moral-sense school — then submitted the whole inheritance to one instrument, the copy principle, and reported what survived. His contemporaries answered with friendship (Smith), fascination (Paris), paranoia (Rousseau), and organized outrage (the Scottish clergy and their pamphleteers). Posterity answered by building on the ruins: Reid founded a school on refuting him, Kant founded a system on answering him, and the empiricist tradition has been arguing about whether to thank or blame him ever since.

## Backward: the inheritance, audited

### Locke: the way of ideas, taken over and turned against itself

Hume's machinery is [Locke](../../eras/03-early-modern/john-locke.md)'s. The *Essay Concerning Human Understanding* (1689) supplied the program — no innate ideas; all materials of thought from sensation and reflection — and the vocabulary: where Locke used "idea" for everything perceived, Hume split the class into impressions and ideas and armed the split with the copy principle. Locke's materials, audited by Locke's own empiricist test, then fail in places Locke never audited: no impression answers to substance, to necessary connection, or to a simple identical self. The personal-identity case shows the method at its most loyal and most destructive. Locke had made identity a matter of consciousness and memory rather than substance; Hume accepts the framework, looks for the impression of the self that the framework requires, finds only the bundle, and confesses in the Appendix that he cannot make the result consistent. Hume is not Locke's opponent from outside. He is what the way of ideas produces when it is followed without flinching — which is exactly how Reid, below, would later frame the indictment.

### Berkeley: the lesson learned, the conclusion refused

From [Berkeley](../../eras/03-early-modern/george-berkeley.md) Hume took two things. The first is technical and acknowledged: the attack on abstract general ideas in the *Treatise* (1.1.7) follows Berkeley's, and Hume calls it "one of the greatest and most valuable discoveries" of recent philosophy. The second is methodological and rueful. In a famous footnote in the *Enquiry* (§XII) Hume remarks that Berkeley's arguments, whatever their author intended, "admit of no answer and produce no conviction" — they are the best lessons in skepticism available, because they show how far the way of ideas can push a rigorous mind. Where Berkeley stopped, Hume declined to stop. Berkeley dissolved material substance but kept spirits — active minds, and God as the perceiver who holds the world in being. Hume applied Berkeley's own test to spirit: from what impression is *it* derived? Finding none, he let minds go the way of matter, into the bundle. Immaterialism's lesson, as Hume learned it, is that the audit cannot be halted at the point where its author found the results comfortable.

### Descartes and Spinoza: the rationalist foil, and the hideous hypothesis

[Descartes](../../eras/03-early-modern/rene-descartes.md) is the standing foil — unsurprising in a book drafted largely at La Flèche, the Jesuit college where Descartes himself was educated. Descartes had doubted everything dubitable in order to stop doubting: the *cogito*, clear and distinct perception, and a guaranteeing God rebuilt certainty on the far side of the exercise. Hume's skepticism is the same instrument with the guarantees removed. No impression underwrites clear and distinct ideas as a mark of truth; no God is available to certify the faculties, since the arguments for God are themselves audited and found to rest on the causal reasoning under examination. Where Descartes treated doubt as a solvent to be used once, Hume treats it as a permanent finding about reason's reach, to be lived with rather than cured. [Spinoza](../../eras/03-early-modern/baruch-spinoza.md) figures briefly and vividly as the limit case of system-building: in *Treatise* 1.4.5 Hume calls Spinoza's monism a "hideous hypothesis" — and then, characteristically, argues that the orthodox doctrine of an immaterial soul is exposed to the very objections that make Spinoza hideous. The rationalists supplied Hume with his targets; they also supplied, in their demand for first principles clearly grasped, the standard of intelligibility his audit enforces.

### Shaftesbury and Hutcheson: the moral sense, naturalized

Hume's ethics descends from the moral-sense school founded by Shaftesbury and systematized by Francis Hutcheson, Glasgow's professor of moral philosophy, whose *Inquiry into the Original of Our Ideas of Beauty and Virtue* (1725) made moral approval a kind of perception — immediate, disinterested, unreasoned. Hume keeps the immediacy and drops the theology: where Hutcheson's moral sense was a faculty implanted by a benevolent God, Hume's sentiment is a natural fact about creatures built as we are, and sympathy does the social work Hutcheson left to design. The contact is documented in Hume's own hand. In September 1739, with Book III of the *Treatise* in preparation, he wrote to Hutcheson defending his procedure — he was, he said, taking his "Catalogue of Virtues from Cicero's Offices, not from the Whole Duty of Man" — a naturalist's manifesto in one sentence: the virtues are to be collected from human life as observed, not from devotional manuals. The is–ought paragraph (*Treatise* 3.1.1) belongs to this quarrel: its immediate targets are the rationalists, but it also serves notice on the whole school that a *feeling* of approbation, however described, will not by itself generate conclusions of reason.

### The Newtonian model: experimental reasoning in moral subjects

The *Treatise*'s subtitle is a declaration of method: *An Attempt to Introduce the Experimental Method of Reasoning into Moral Subjects*. Newton is the model throughout — not the conclusions but the discipline. Do not feign hypotheses; derive a few general principles from phenomena; let those principles explain as much as they can and confess the residue. Hume's association of ideas is offered in Newton's spirit, as "a kind of attraction, which in the mental world will be found to have as extraordinary effects as in the natural" — one gentle force doing for perceptions what gravity does for bodies. His "rules by which to judge of causes and effects" (*Treatise* 1.3.15) consciously parallel Newton's rules of reasoning in the *Principia*. And the Introduction's ambition — a "science of man" as the foundation on which the other sciences depend — is the Newtonian program turned inward: human nature mapped by the same experimental patience that had mapped the heavens. (There is no library entry for Newton in this repo; he stands behind several, this one most explicitly.)

## Sideways: friends, salons, and a rupture

### Edinburgh: Smith, the Select Society, and the chairs he never got

The Scottish Enlightenment was a small world, and Hume was at its center without ever holding the academic post that should have been his. He was blocked from the Edinburgh chair of ethics and pneumatic philosophy in 1745 and from a Glasgow chair in 1752, both times by organized clerical opposition to his religious views; a pamphlet of extracts from the *Treatise* circulated against him in the Edinburgh contest to brand him a heretic. He belonged instead to the republic of letters the Scots built outside the universities: the Select Society, founded in Edinburgh in 1754, where Hume, [Adam Smith](../../eras/03-early-modern/adam-smith.md), and their circle debated questions of taste, trade, and morals weekly.

The Smith friendship is the era's great philosophical partnership. Smith's *Theory of Moral Sentiments* (1759) takes up Hume's sympathy and rebuilds it — sympathy becomes fellow-feeling with any passion whatever, and Hume's general point of view is interiorized as the impartial spectator — a development, not a copy, and Smith knew the difference as well as Hume did. Their last exchange of roles is the famous one. Hume, dying in 1776, asked Smith among others to see the *Dialogues* into print; Smith declined the exposure, and Hume made other arrangements. What Smith did write was the letter to the publisher William Strahan (9 November 1776) describing Hume's cheerful, unrepentant death — "I have always considered him, both in his lifetime and since his death, as approaching as nearly to the idea of a perfectly wise and virtuous man, as perhaps the nature of human frailty will permit." Published, the letter scandalized pious readers precisely as Hume would have wished: the deathbed became evidence in the argument.

### Paris: le bon David

From 1763 to 1766 Hume served at the British embassy in Paris and was lionized — "le bon David," fêted in the salons of Madame Geoffrin and the circle of d'Holbach, praised by Voltaire, who admired the *History of England*. The philosophes took him for one of their own, a misreading he seems to have enjoyed without correcting too firmly: his mitigated skepticism, and his policy of not publishing his most dangerous pieces, were more cautious than the Parisian manner. But the Paris years matter to the philosophy for one consequential act of generosity: it was Hume who arranged refuge in England for the persecuted Rousseau.

### The Rousseau rupture (1766)

The facts, as the paper trail gives them. [Rousseau](../../eras/03-early-modern/jean-jacques-rousseau.md), expelled from France and Switzerland, accepted Hume's protection and arrived in England in January 1766, settling at Wootton in Staffordshire. Within months he had become convinced that Hume was the center of a conspiracy to dishonor him — a conviction fed by a satirical forged letter (Horace Walpole's mock letter from the King of Prussia, circulated in Paris) and by Rousseau's reading of slights in the arrangements made for him. In July 1766 Rousseau sent Hume a long letter of accusation; Hume, stung and fearing for his reputation, published the correspondence as *A Concise and Genuine Account of the Dispute Between Mr. Hume and Mr. Rousseau* (1766), with supporting letters from d'Alembert and others.

Both sides kept the receipts, and the dossier reports them without a verdict. Rousseau's letters are the record of a man in a persecutory state, and scholars have overwhelmingly found the conspiracy imaginary; the imagined plot's details dissolve on inspection. But Hume's remedy — printing a friend's private letters to vindicate himself — struck some contemporaries, and still strikes some readers, as a breach of the very politeness "le bon David" was famous for, and Hume's own later regrets about the publication are part of the record too. Rousseau left England in 1767. The episode is the darkest entry in Hume's biography and a standing exhibit in the difference between winning an argument and conducting one.

### The public antagonists: Beattie and the clergy

Hume's most successful contemporary enemy was James Beattie, Aberdeen professor, whose *Essay on the Nature and Immutability of Truth* (1770) attacked Hume's philosophy — with a personal contempt unusual even in that polemical age — and became a bestseller. Hume never answered in print and expressed his contempt privately; posterity split the difference oddly, since Beattie's book is now read only as a document of the reaction, while the reaction itself (below) built a school. The clerical opposition was the institutional form of the same hostility: it kept Hume from chairs in 1745 and 1752, and it is the background fact of his publishing strategy — the suppressed essays on suicide and immortality, the *Dialogues* withheld until after his death. Hume's caution and his enemies' vigilance explain each other.

## Forward: the respondents

**Reid: the school founded on the refutation.** Thomas Reid read the *Treatise* and concluded that its conclusions were the honest terminus of a shared mistake: the "way of ideas" running from Descartes through Locke and Berkeley. His *Inquiry into the Human Mind on the Principles of Common Sense* (1764) — sent to Hume in draft through Hugh Blair, and answered by Hume with notable courtesy; the two never met — proposed that perception is not inference from ideas but an original operation governed by principles of common sense that philosophy has no standing to overrule. Reid's is the first systematic answer Hume received, and it frames the question [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md) still tracks: whether Hume's skepticism is a discovery about reason or a reductio of the premises that produced it. (Reid has no library entry in this repo; he is answered-space, the founding figure of the Scottish Common Sense school.)

**Kant: the awakening.** [Kant](../../eras/03-early-modern/immanuel-kant.md)'s testimony, in the Preface to the *Prolegomena* (1783), is precise about the debt: "the remembrance of David Hume was the very thing that many years ago first interrupted my dogmatic slumber," and the problem Kant names is Hume's — the concept of cause claims a necessary connection that experience never delivers. Kant's answer relocates the necessity into the mind's own conditions of experience, at the price of the thing in itself. The whole exchange, including what Kant could and could not have read, is traced in the Kant dossier's [conversations file](../immanuel-kant/05-conversations.md); it is the single most consequential reply chain in this library.

**Bentham, and through him Mill.** [Jeremy Bentham](../../eras/04-19th-century/jeremy-bentham.md) reported in the Preface to *A Fragment on Government* (1776) that Hume's demolition of the original contract — in the essay "Of the Original Contract," where allegiance is shown to rest on utility rather than promise — opened his eyes: the scales, as he put it, fell. The utility principle that organizes Bentham's system is Hume's discovery re-founded as a program. [John Stuart Mill](../../eras/04-19th-century/john-stuart-mill.md) inherited Hume twice over: through Bentham's utility, and through the associationist psychology of his father's school, which runs from Hume (and Hartley) into the Mills' analysis of mind. His *Examination of Sir William Hamilton* (1865) defends a phenomenalism about matter — a "permanent possibility of sensation" — that is Berkeley's and Hume's lineage self-consciously continued.

**Darwin: a notebook debt, extent debated.** Darwin read Hume while the theory of natural selection was forming, and his notebooks of 1838–39 engage recognizably Humean questions — instinct and habit as explanations that bypass reason, the standing of the design argument after the *Dialogues*. How much of the mature theory's framing that debt explains is debated among historians of science; the dossier records the reading, not a settled influence claim.

**Russell, Ayer, and the positivists' Hume.** [Bertrand Russell](../../eras/05-20th-century/bertrand-russell.md) gave Hume the most quoted modern verdict, in his *History of Western Philosophy* (1945): Hume developed the empiricism of Locke and Berkeley "by making it self-consistent made it incredible" — a dead end, Russell concluded, in whose direction it is impossible to go further. His "On the Notion of Cause" (1912) had already argued, in a Humean spirit, that advanced physics has no use for the notion. A. J. Ayer's *Language, Truth and Logic* (1936) recruited Hume rather than mourning him: the fork — relations of ideas, matters of fact — became the ancestry of the verification principle, and the [Vienna Circle](../../eras/05-20th-century/vienna-circle-logical-positivism.md) read Hume as a patron saint. Ayer's own later *Hume* (1980) is a slimmer, more faithful tribute, and it opens this dossier's bibliography.

**Popper: the problem accepted, the office abolished.** [Karl Popper](../../eras/05-20th-century/karl-popper.md) states his debt without hedging: he approached the problem of induction through Hume and concluded Hume was "perfectly right" that induction can neither be justified nor observed as a method of science. His move is to accept the negative result and deny the premise that science needs induction at all — knowledge grows by conjecture and refutation, and the problem of justification dissolves when justification is no longer the goal. Whether that is a solution or a change of subject is the standing dispute about Popper, and it begins, by his own account, at Hume's desk.

**Mackie and Strawson: the working heirs.** J. L. Mackie built on Hume in both his fields: *The Cement of the Universe* (1974) defends a sophisticated regularity theory of causation in full awareness of its Humean descent, and *Ethics: Inventing Right and Wrong* (1977) opens its error theory — moral judgments purport objectivity and are systematically false — under Hume's patronage, is–ought passage included. P. F. Strawson's "Freedom and Resentment" (1962) naturalized responsibility in the reactive attitudes in a manner directly continuous with Hume's sentimentalism, and his descriptive metaphysics kept Hume's question — what must our conceptual scheme be, given the minds we have? — at the center of analytic philosophy. (Neither Mackie nor Strawson has a library entry here; his son Galen Strawson's causal realism is a live debate treated in [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md).)

## The themes trace

The library's [problem index](../../themes.md) places Hume in seven of its nine threads — a fair measure of a philosopher whose system was designed as a science of the whole human being:

- [§1 Knowledge & Skepticism](../../themes.md#1-knowledge--skepticism--what-can-we-know-and-how-do-we-know-it) — ideas copy impressions; induction has no rational warrant; skepticism is cured not by argument but by nature and custom.
- [§2 Mind & Body](../../themes.md#2-mind--body--what-is-mind-and-how-does-it-relate-to-the-body) — no impression of a mental substance exists: the mind is a bundle of perceptions in perpetual flux.
- [§3 God & Religion](../../themes.md#3-god--religion--does-god-exist-and-what-follows-for-faith-and-reason) — the *Dialogues* leave the design argument in ruins, testimony never outweighs experience for miracles, and religion's roots are fear and hope.
- [§4 Ethics & the Good Life](../../themes.md#4-ethics--the-good-life--how-should-i-live-and-what-is-right) — morals are founded on sentiment, not reason; sympathy, corrected by a general point of view, yields the catalogue of virtues.
- [§5 Politics & Justice](../../themes.md#5-politics--justice--how-should-we-live-together-and-who-may-rule) — justice is an artificial virtue born of scarcity and convention; and all government rests finally on opinion, not force.
- [§6 Self & Identity](../../themes.md#6-self--identity--what-am-i-and-what-makes-me-the-same-person-over-time) — introspection finds only particular perceptions, never a self: the mind is a bundle, and personal identity is imagination's construction.
- [§8 Science & Method](../../themes.md#8-science--method--how-should-inquiry-proceed-and-what-does-it-prove) — induction, the engine of all experimental science, has no rational foundation: it is custom — nature's cure for its own skepticism.

The index does not place him in §7 (Language & Meaning) or §9 (History & Progress). The first absence is just: Hume's remarks on language are real but instrumental — words, like ideas, are audited by their impressions — and he built no philosophy of language for the index to record. The second is the index's most interesting omission: the man who wrote the century's best-selling *History of England* theorizes progress hardly at all, treating history as a storehouse of experiments in human nature rather than a story with a direction. The trace, absences included, is the system in miniature: wherever a question can be brought under the experimental method, Hume is there.
