# David Hume: The Arguments
**English** | [简体中文](../../zh/focus-studies/david-hume/04-arguments.md)

*Focus study · [Dossier home](README.md) · Library entry: [David Hume](../../eras/03-early-modern/david-hume.md) · The architecture these arguments build: [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md) · Study guide: [*Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding*](../../study/hume-enquiry.md)*

Each argument below is reconstructed in numbered premises in the house style: what the argument proves, the reconstruction, its textual basis, the strongest standard objection, and the best reply — Hume's own where he gave one, the literature's where he did not, labeled as such. Citations follow the conventions of Hume scholarship: **T** = *A Treatise of Human Nature*, by book.part.section; **E** = *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding*, by section (paragraph numbers are added only for the famous passages the repo's guide already pins); **EPM** = *An Enquiry Concerning the Principles of Morals*, by section; the *Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion* are cited by Part.

One framing caution governs the whole file. Hume argues the same conclusions twice over — once in the *Treatise* (1739–40) and again, recast, in the *Enquiries* (1748, 1751) — and the two versions are not always identical in emphasis; where the difference matters, the reconstruction says which text it follows. Some arguments exist in only one place: personal identity, the passions, and the foundations of morality are *Treatise*-only (the *Enquiry* drops them), miracles is *Enquiry*-only, and the natural-religion arguments belong to the posthumous *Dialogues* (1779), where who speaks for Hume is itself disputed. Over all of it hangs the file's governing interpretive dispute. On the **skeptical reading** (the traditional one, and the one Hume's first readers took home), the arguments are demolitions: reason cannot found causation, induction, selfhood, or morality, and what remains is an exposed void that custom merely papers over. On the **naturalistic reading** (Norman Kemp Smith's, now widely held), the demolitions are ground-clearing for a positive science of man: the point was never that we lack beliefs but that beliefs have natural, not rational, foundations — custom, sympathy, sentiment — which Hume then maps in detail. The **New Hume** debate (Argument 3) is the sharpest local form of the same quarrel. Each reconstruction below is stated so that the dispute can be seen, not settled.

**Argument 1. The copy principle as a test of meaning**

*What it proves:* that every simple idea must copy a preceding simple impression — and, wielded as an instrument, that philosophical terms which cannot be traced to an impression ("substance," "necessary connection," "the soul") are suspect as empty noise. It is the detector the rest of the system runs on.

1. All perceptions of the mind divide into **impressions** — the lively perceptions of sensing, feeling, and emotion — and **ideas**, their fainter copies in memory and imagination (T 1.1.1; E 2).
2. Complex ideas can be built without a complex impression — we compound, transpose, augment, and diminish materials experience supplies (a golden mountain joins gold and mountain) — but a **simple** idea cannot be invented: it copies a corresponding simple impression. Hence a blind man has no idea of color.
3. Therefore, for any term whose meaning is in dispute, the test is: *from what impression is the supposed idea derived?* Hume's own formulation (E 2): when we suspect a philosophical term is used "without any meaning or idea (as is but too frequent), we need but inquire, *from what impression is that supposed idea derived?*"
4. **Hume's own counterexample.** A man who has seen every shade of blue except one may, Hume concedes, supply the missing shade from the gradation of the others — forming a simple idea with no corresponding impression (E 2; T 1.1.1).
5. **Hume's dismissal of it.** The case is, in his words, "so particular and singular, that it is scarce worth our observing, and does not merit, that for it alone we should alter our general maxim" (E 2).
Therefore, the copy principle stands as the system's working test of meaning: a term that survives no impression-hunt is presumed empty until its defender produces the impression.

**Textual basis.** T 1.1.1; E 2 (the test at E 2.9; the missing shade at E 2.8). The guide's [Section II discussion](../../study/hume-enquiry.md) gives the passage map.

**Objection.** The concession is more damaging than Hume allows. If the mind can generate one simple idea without an impression — by running along a gradient of resembling impressions — then the barrier between experiential content and invention is porous in principle, and the defender of "substance" or "self" can plead the same porosity: perhaps those ideas too are constructed by legitimate mental operations from materials that are not simple copies. A second objection presses self-application (the same problem the fork faces): is the copy principle itself a relation of ideas or a matter of fact? If a matter of fact, it is a contingent generalization that a single counter-instance — say, the missing shade — should dent more than Hume admits.

**Reply.** Hume's own reply is premise 5's wager, and it is candidly pragmatic: the principle is offered as a well-confirmed empirical maxim, not an a priori definition, and one singular exception does not overturn a maxim confirmed everywhere else. The literature's defense adds what Hume's associationism implies: the missing shade is generated entirely from *neighboring impressions* by resemblance — the mind supplies a gap inside an experienced series, which is a different operation from minting a simple content (a color for the blind, a tie between objects) with no experienced series to interpolate into. Whether that distinction is principled or convenient remains genuinely disputed; the dossier's judgment is that the copy principle's real force in Hume's hands is procedural — it shifts the burden of proof — and that burden-shifting survives the missing shade even if the principle as a universal law does not.

**Argument 2. The problem of induction**

*What it proves:* that reasoning from the observed to the unobserved has no rational foundation — it cannot be justified by demonstration or by experience — and is instead the work of custom or habit. The entry's [signature argument](../../eras/03-early-modern/david-hume.md); the dossier follows the *Enquiry* version and adds the *Treatise* original.

1. All reasoning about unobserved matters of fact rests on the relation of cause and effect (E 4).
2. Knowledge of cause and effect comes only from experience, never from reasoning alone: nothing in a cause, inspected a priori, dictates its effect.
3. Inferring the unobserved from the observed therefore presupposes that the future will resemble the past — that nature's course will continue uniformly.
4. **The demonstrative horn:** that presupposition cannot be proved demonstratively, because its denial — a changed course of nature — is conceivable without contradiction, and demonstrative certainty extends only where the contrary implies contradiction.
5. **The probable horn:** it cannot be proved from experience either, because reasoning from past uniformity to future uniformity itself presupposes the very resemblance in question. The argument is circular.
Therefore, inductive inference has no rational justification; it is performed not by reason but by **custom or habit**, the mind's determination, after repeated conjunction, to expect the usual attendant.

**Textual basis.** E 4 (the fork at E 4.1–2; the dilemma at E 4.10–16 in the guide's numbering); T 1.3.6 ("Of the inference from the impression to the idea"), where the *Treatise* adds that the inference cannot be reason's work because children and animals make it without any reasoning, and reason operating on ideas alone would require a medium — a connecting proposition — that experience never supplies. The skeptical *solution* is E 5. Full reconstruction in the [study guide](../../study/hume-enquiry.md).

**Objection.** The bar is set unfairly high. A practice need not be demonstrable, or non-circularly provable, to be *justified*: reliabilists argue induction is justified if it in fact reliably yields true beliefs, whether or not we can prove it does; pragmatic vindications argue it is the best strategy available — if any method works, induction does — and that no more can sensibly be asked.

**Reply.** Hume's own reply is the "sceptical solution" of E 5, and it concedes the practical point in advance: he is not counseling anyone to stop expecting bread to nourish. Nature, through custom, determines us to infer, and we neither can nor should resist. The argument's target is narrower and survives the objection: no *reason* — no argument that does not already assume uniformity — can be given for the inference, so its foundation is natural, not rational. Whether the reliabilist and pragmatic replies answer that challenge or change the subject from justification to usefulness is, as the entry notes, itself part of the continuing debate — the debate is the problem's afterlife, not its solution.

**Argument 3. Causation: no impression of necessary connection, and the two definitions of cause**

*What it proves:* that our idea of necessary connection is not copied from anything perceived in objects — watching causes operate shows contiguity, priority, and constant conjunction, never a power or tie — but from an internal impression: the mind's felt determination, after repetition, to pass from one object to its usual attendant. Necessity, on the famous provocation, exists in the mind, not in objects.

1. Apply the copy principle's test (Argument 1): if we have an idea of necessary connection, some impression must correspond to it.
2. In any single instance, none is found. One billiard ball strikes another: we perceive contiguity in space, priority in time, and the second ball's motion — but no further item, no power, force, or necessary tie between them (T 1.3.2; E 7).
3. The mind's own operations do not supply it either. Locke's candidate — the will's command over the body — is itself known only through experience of constant conjunction, and its mechanism is opaque to us; it presupposes the conjunction it was meant to explain (E 7).
4. Repetition changes something — but in us, not in the objects. After many like instances, custom determines the mind to pass from the impression of the cause to a lively idea of the effect, and this **felt determination of the mind** is an impression of reflection (T 1.3.14; E 7).
5. The idea of necessity is copied from that internal impression. Hume's verdict (T 1.3.14): necessity "is something, that exists in the mind, not in objects."
Therefore Hume offers **two definitions of cause** (T 1.3.14; E 7.29): (i) "an object, followed by another, and where all the objects similar to the first are followed by objects similar to the second" — the regularity definition; and (ii) an object followed by another "whose appearance always conveys the thought to that other" — the definition by the mind's customary transition.

**Textual basis.** T 1.3.2 (what is perceived in causal instances), 1.3.14 (the determination of the mind; the two definitions); E 7 (the search through single instances and the will; the definitions at E 7.29). The guide's reconstruction (b) covers the *Enquiry* form.

**Objection.** The two definitions seem to answer different questions and to pull in opposite directions: the first locates causation wholly in objects and their regularities, the second makes it depend on a mind's habits — in which case causation in a world without minds becomes unintelligible, and the second definition looks like a report of our psychology rather than an analysis of *cause*. From the other flank, the **New Hume** readers (Galen Strawson's *The Secret Connexion*, 1989, is the landmark; John Wright and Kenneth Winkler argue similar lines) object that the argument proves too much: Hume constantly writes of "secret powers" and unknown "springs and principles" of nature, which suggests he *believed* in objective necessities and denied only that we can conceive or know them — a "sceptical realism," not a reduction of causation to regularity plus projection.

**Reply.** There is no reply in the text that settles the matter, and the dossier does not invent one: the dispute between the traditional (regularity/projection) reading and the New Hume (sceptical-realist) reading is the live question, with passages musterable on both sides — the two definitions themselves can be cited by each camp, the first favoring the traditionalists, Hume's talk of nature's secret powers favoring the New Humeans. The secure core, on which both readings agree and which the entry states, is epistemic: our evidence never exceeds constant conjunction plus the mind's habitual transition, so any claim about objective necessity goes beyond anything experience shows us. Whether Hume thought there was nonetheless something there, beyond the showing, is exactly what is disputed.

**Argument 4. Liberty and necessity reconciled: compatibilism**

*What it proves:* that the free-will dispute is largely verbal and dissolves once "necessity" and "liberty" are given the meanings everyone actually uses: necessity is the uniformity of conjunction (Argument 3) that human actions display as fully as physical events, and liberty is the power of acting according to one's will — so the two are not merely compatible but mutually required for moral responsibility.

1. Necessity, on the analysis already given, means no more than constant conjunction of like objects and the mind's customary inference from one to the other (E 8; T 2.3.1).
2. Human actions exhibit exactly that uniformity: the same motives, character, and circumstances are regularly followed by the same actions, and everyone — historians, moralists, jailers, friends — reasons from character to conduct as confidently as from cause to effect (E 8; T 2.3.1).
3. **Liberty**, as the word is actually meant, is "a power of acting or not acting, according to the determinations of the will" (E 8.23) — the absence of external constraint, not the absence of causal determination.
4. If actions were *not* necessitated by character and motive, they would be chance occurrences, connected to nothing in the agent — and could not ground praise, blame, reward, or punishment, which all presuppose that actions reveal the person.
Therefore, liberty and necessity are compatible, and necessity is a precondition of morality rather than its enemy: the reconciling project (E 8's own description of its aim) succeeds by fixing meanings (T 2.3.1–2).

**Textual basis.** E 8 ("Of Liberty and Necessity," the *Enquiry*'s mature statement); T 2.3.1 ("Of liberty and necessity") and 2.3.2 ("The same subject continu'd"), where the doctrine is first developed.

**Objection.** The reconciliation buys peace by redefining the contested term. What the libertarian wants — and what moral experience seems to report — is a *categorical* ability to have done otherwise: the very same past, the very same character, and yet a different choice. Hume's "hypothetical" liberty (I would have done otherwise *had I willed otherwise*) does not supply it. The modern form is van Inwagen's consequence argument: if determinism is true, our acts are the consequences of the laws of nature and events before our birth; those are not up to us; so neither are their consequences. A further objection presses equivocation: "necessity" in premise 1 bundles together observed uniformity and the mind's felt determination, and it is not obvious that either, or both together, is what the opponent of free will was ever denying us.

**Reply.** Hume's own reply is built into premise 4 and pressed throughout E 8: liberty of *indifference* — action uncaused by the will's determination — would not be freedom worth wanting, since it detaches action from the agent and makes it mere chance; everything morality needs (responsibility, deliberation, the efficacy of motives) is preserved, indeed only intelligible, under his definitions. The literature has largely followed him: compatibilism in this line remains the majority position, refined by Frankfurt-style cases against the principle that responsibility requires alternative possibilities. The consequence argument and the categorical intuition remain the standing counterweight, and the dossier records the position as the template for later compatibilism — not as a closed question.

**Argument 5. Miracles: testimony against uniform experience**

*What it proves:* not that miracles are impossible — Hume is explicit that the argument concerns evidence — but that testimony can never rationally establish one: when a report of a law-violation is weighed against the uniform experience that constitutes the law, the wise man's proportioning of belief to evidence always comes down against the report.

1. "A wise man, therefore, proportions his belief to the evidence" (E 10.4). All evidence for matters of fact is experience, of ourselves or of others' testimony.
2. A miracle is "a violation of the laws of nature; and as a firm and unalterable experience has established these laws," the proof against a miracle, from the nature of the fact, is as entire as any argument from experience can be (E 10.12).
3. Testimony's force is itself experiential and weaker: it rests on the observed, and frequently disappointed, conformity between reports and facts, and is undermined by the credulity of witnesses, the love of the marvellous, the interest of relators, and the tendency of wonder-reports to flourish chiefly among "ignorant and barbarous nations" and to cancel one another across rival religions (E 10, Part II).
4. When the two bodies of evidence collide, belief follows the stronger, subtracting the weaker: testimony for a miracle must overcome the full weight of uniform experience against it.
Therefore Hume's maxim (E 10.13): "no testimony is sufficient to establish a miracle, unless the testimony be of such a kind, that its falsehood would be more miraculous, than the fact, which it endeavours to establish" — a condition he argues is never met in practice.

**Textual basis.** E 10 ("Of Miracles"), Part I (the weighing argument; definition at E 10.12, maxim at E 10.13) and Part II (the four empirical considerations against actual miracle reports). Hume's own nuance is the Indian prince of E 10.10, who refused belief in ice: the prince reasoned "justly" from his experience, yet frost was not *contrary* to his experience, only not conformable to it — Hume uses the case to mark the line between the merely improbable and the miraculous proper. Full reconstruction in the [study guide](../../study/hume-enquiry.md).

**Objection.** The modern objection is that the weighing is rigged at premise 2. John Earman's *Hume's Abject Failure* (2000) argues that, formalized in Bayesian terms, Hume's argument collapses as an a priori bar: sufficiently many independent, reliable witnesses can in principle raise the probability of even a law-violating event, and treating the law's evidence as maximal double-counts — the witnesses' reports are themselves part of the total experience, not a fixed weight to be thrown against it. Earman's verdict is that as a would-be *a priori* demonstration the argument fails outright.

**Reply.** The defense — Robert Fogelin's *A Defense of Hume on Miracles* (2003) is representative — is that Hume never needed the a priori reading: the maxim is stated conditionally from the start ("unless the testimony be of such a kind..."), and the argument's real work is evidential. On the Bayesian machinery Earman himself supplies, the prior probability of a violation of a law confirmed by uniform experience is extremely low, so testimony would need to be extraordinarily strong to overcome it — and Part II's empirical claim is that actual miracle testimony never approaches that strength. Whether Hume intended an a priori barrier or a (very strong) evidential presumption is itself the standing dispute in the literature; the dossier presents the argument so that both readings are visible, as the guide does, and notes that on the evidential reading it survives its Bayesian translation in refined form.

**Argument 6. The *Dialogues* on design: the analogy weakened, the attributes inverted**

*What it proves:* that the argument from design, conducted by the rules of analogical inference its own defender accepts, yields far less than theism wants: the world's resemblance to a machine is too remote, and the universe too singular, to license inferring an infinite, perfect, single author — and the same analogy, pressed consistently, suggests a finite, fallible, possibly plural maker instead.

1. Cleanthes states the argument (Part II): the world is a great machine, subdivided into lesser machines, with means adapted to ends throughout; the Author of nature resembles the human mind, "though possessed of much larger faculties" — like effects prove like causes.
2. Philo states the rule of analogy and its price: where the resemblance between cases is remote, the inference weakens proportionally. Houses and watches are known artifacts, checkable against other artifacts; the universe is a single, unrepeatable whole, resembling a machine in some respects and an animal or a vegetable in others (Parts II–IV).
3. We have no experience of the origins of worlds. Experience shows order arising from generation and vegetation as readily as from reason; on the evidence alone, the world could as plausibly be modeled on an organism as on a machine, and Philo offers his alternative hypotheses (including, in Part VII, a material universe self-ordered through an internal principle, and generation from a cosmic animal or plant) to show the design inference is not the only reading of the same facts.
4. Press the analogy honestly (Part V) and it returns the wrong God: a cause proportioned to a finite, imperfect effect is itself finite and imperfect — possibly an apprentice deity whose world is a botched first effort, possibly a committee of gods, possibly corporeal and mortal. Cleanthes' analogy, taken strictly, is as serviceable to polytheism and a limited God as to theism.
Therefore, the design argument establishes at most a faint and ambiguous analogy between the cause of cosmic order and human intelligence — approximately the concession Philo finally makes in Part XII: that "the cause or causes of order in the universe probably bear some remote analogy to human intelligence."

**Textual basis.** *Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion*, Part II (Cleanthes' statement; Philo's rules of analogy), Part V (the inversion of the attributes), with the alternatives at Parts VI–VII and Philo's minimal concession in Part XII. The attribution problem below is inseparable from this argument's force.

**Objection — and it is first an internal one.** Who is speaking? The standard reading (Norman Kemp Smith's edition made it canonical) takes Philo, the skeptic, as Hume's spokesman and Cleanthes as the target. But the dialogue refuses to settle: in Part XII it is Philo himself who delivers the most rhapsodic affirmation of design in the book — "a purpose, an intention, a design strikes every where the most careless, the most stupid thinker" — and the narrator Pamphilus, in the closing sentence, judges that Cleanthes' principles "approach still nearer to the truth" than Philo's. Readers dispute whether Part XII is Philo's sincere minimal theism, his irony, or his prudent camouflage in a dangerous century; and whether Hume distributed his own views across Philo and Cleanthes deliberately. A literature objection from theism's side: Cleanthes' rejoinder in the dialogue is that the design inference is not a syllogism but a natural, irresistible recognition — an argument by "irregular" but sound analogy — and modern fine-tuning arguments claim to bypass the machine comparison Philo attacks.

**Reply.** The text's own structure is the partial reply: whatever Philo's sincerity in Part XII, the concession he makes there is stated in the weakest possible terms ("remote analogy," "cause or causes"), and it follows eleven Parts in which every stronger inference has been dismantled by agreed rules; a spokesman-reading that gives Cleanthes the victory must explain why his position survives only in Philo's most guarded sentence and in a pupil-narrator's verdict. The literature remains divided — which is why this dossier, following the entry, treats "who speaks for Hume?" as an open question built into the work's design rather than a problem with a fileable answer. On fine-tuning, the Humean counters (the singularity of the universe, the proportionality of cause to effect) are redeployed in the current literature; the debate is live.

**Argument 7. The problem of evil in the *Dialogues***

*What it proves:* within natural theology's own method — inferring the cause's attributes from the effect, proportioned to what the effect shows — that the world's mixture of good and, especially, suffering blocks the inference to a first cause that is at once omnipotent and perfectly benevolent. It is an evidential argument about what experience licenses, not a disproof of God's existence.

1. The rule, common to the *Dialogues* and to E 11: in inferring a cause from its effect, we may ascribe to the cause only the qualities the effect manifests, in the degree it manifests them.
2. The phenomena include pervasive misery: Demea's catalogue in Part X (the anxieties, diseases, and calamities of human life, and nature's indifference to them) is offered not as rhetoric but as the data any inference must fit.
3. Philo then proposes — attributing them to antiquity, as his own speech makes explicit — "**Epicurus's old questions**": "Is he willing to prevent evil, but not able? then is he impotent. Is he able, but not willing? then is he malevolent. Is he both able and willing? whence then is evil?" (Part X.)
4. Surveying the possible accounts of the first cause's moral character (Part XI), Philo distinguishes four hypotheses: perfect goodness; perfect malice; a mixture of the two in opposition; or neither goodness nor malice. The phenomena — good and evil mixed without respect to desert, nature pursuing its ends regardless of suffering — fit the fourth hypothesis best: the original source of things is entirely indifferent to good and evil alike.
Therefore, from experience alone, we cannot infer a perfectly benevolent and omnipotent governor; the moral attributes of the Deity, like a providential ordering of rewards, exceed what the effect warrants (compare E 11).

**Textual basis.** *Dialogues*, Part X (Demea's misery catalogue; Philo's presentation of Epicurus's questions, explicitly framed as borrowed from Epicurus) and Part XI (the four hypotheses; the indifference conclusion). Cleanthes' counter-pressure in Part X — that Philo and Demea exaggerate the predominance of misery — is the dialogue's main internal resistance, and Philo's challenge to him is to make good the claim from the phenomena.

**Objection.** The standing replies are the literature's, not the dialogue's. The **free-will defense** (Plantinga's version is the benchmark) holds that even omnipotence cannot create significantly free creatures and guarantee they never go wrong, so moral evil is compatible with perfect goodness and power; **soul-making** theodicies (Hick) argue that a world containing real suffering is necessary for moral and spiritual growth, so the suffering is instrumentally justified. A prior objection grants premise 1's rule but denies it binds God: the inference rule governs reasoning *from* nature, and a theist who holds God's goodness on other grounds — revelation, or an a priori proof — is simply not conducting the inference Philo audits.

**Reply.** Hume's side of the reply is internal to the method: the free-will and soul-making defenses, whatever their force as accounts of how God *might* be justified, do not restore the *inference* — they supply possible reasons unobserved in the effect, which premise 1 excludes; and the free-will defense at its strongest addresses moral evil (what agents do), not the natural evil (disease, animal suffering, catastrophe) that fills Demea's catalogue, a gap the contemporary evidential problem of evil (Rowe) inherits directly from Philo. The scope objection is fair and the dossier concedes it: the argument binds only natural theology, which is the target Hume set. Whether the four-hypotheses survey is exhaustive — whether "indifferent nature" really explains the *mixture* better than a good God in a fallen or developing world — remains the debate the *Dialogues* started.

**Argument 8. Personal identity: the bundle theory and the Appendix's confession**

*What it proves:* that introspection finds no simple, identical self — only a bundle of distinct perceptions in flux — so that personal identity is a construction of the imagination working on resemblance and causation among perceptions, not a datum. And then, uniquely in Hume, the dossier must report the author's own verdict that he could not make the account consistent.

1. By the copy-principle test (Argument 1), an idea of self requires an impression of self: some impression that remains invariably the same throughout life.
2. Introspection finds none. "I may venture to affirm of the rest of mankind, that they are nothing but a bundle or collection of different perceptions, which succeed each other with an inconceivable rapidity, and are in a perpetual flux and movement"; and for himself: "I never can catch *myself* at any time without a perception, and never can observe any thing but the perception" (T 1.4.6).
3. What we call the mind is comparable to a theatre — with Hume's immediate caution that the comparison must not mislead: we have "not the most distant notion of the place, where these scenes are represented, or of the materials, of which it is compos'd" (T 1.4.6).
4. The ascription of identity is explained, not observed: perceptions linked by resemblance and causation make the imagination glide smoothly from one to the next, and we mistake that smooth transition for the contemplation of something identical — as we do with plants, animals, and artifacts. Memory's role is central: it furnishes the resemblances and discovers the chain.
Therefore, there is no further fact of a unitary self behind the bundle; personal identity is the imagination's construction (T 1.4.6).
5. **The Appendix's confession.** Reviewing the book, Hume found the account in contradiction with itself. Two principles, he writes, he can neither reconcile nor abandon: "that all our distinct perceptions are distinct existences, and that the mind never perceives any real connexion among distinct existences." His verdict: "I find myself involv'd in such a labyrinth, that, I must confess, I neither know how to correct my former opinions, nor how to render them consistent" (Appendix to the *Treatise*).

**Textual basis.** T 1.4.6 ("Of personal identity"); the Appendix (added to Volume III, 1740), where the inconsistency is confessed. The *Enquiry* omits the topic entirely — one of the recasting's most significant silences.

**Objection.** The classic objection is circularity, pressed by Bishop Butler against Locke's memory criterion and applied with equal force here: memory cannot *constitute* personal identity, because genuine remembering already presupposes that the rememberer is the person who had the experience — false "memories" of another's past are conceivable only because identity is prior to memory, not produced by it. A second objection asks after the bundle's owner: perceptions are someone's perceptions; a collection with no collector seems to describe experiences with the experiencer defined away. And Hume's own objection stands in the text: on premises he would not surrender, he could not explain what unites distinct perceptions into one mind at all.

**Reply.** Hume gives none — the Appendix is the record of a reply failing, and the dossier follows the entry in treating the confession as part of the argument's content, an act of philosophical honesty almost without parallel in the canon. The literature's replies divide. **Reconciliationists** (Don Garrett's reading is the most developed) argue the inconsistency is narrower than the confession suggests and can be located precisely, in the gap between the imagination's feigning and the causal theory of the bundle's cohesion. **Reductionists** take the opposite path: Derek Parfit (*Reasons and Persons*, 1984) revives the bundle theory and argues the Appendix's problem dissolves once we give up the idea that identity is a deep further fact — psychological continuity and connectedness are all there is, and the failure to find a unifying self is the discovery, not the defect. Which response Hume would have taken is unknowable; that both are live options is the argument's legacy.

**Argument 9. Reason is, and ought only to be, the slave of the passions**

*What it proves:* that reason, strictly so called, can never by itself produce or oppose an action: it discovers truth and falsehood, but passions and volitions are not the kind of thing that can be true or false — so motivation always originates in passion, and reason's legitimate office is instrumental, informing the passions and correcting their mistakes.

1. Reason is the discovery of truth or falsehood, and its objects are relations of ideas and matters of fact (T 2.3.3).
2. A passion is not a copy or representation of anything: it is an "original existence," complete in itself, containing no representative quality (T 2.3.3). What does not represent cannot agree or disagree with anything, and so cannot be *contrary to reason* in itself.
3. Demonstration alone moves no one: abstract reasoning about quantity has no influence on action except by directing a pre-existing desire or aversion. Causal reasoning likewise motivates only when it engages an antecedent concern — we deliberate about means because we already want the end.
4. A passion can be called unreasonable only in two derivative cases: when it is founded on a false belief (grieving for a loss that did not occur), or when it chooses means insufficient to its end. Correct the belief, and the passion dissolves — reason's power over passion runs entirely through truth.
Therefore: "Reason is, and ought only to be the slave of the passions, and can never pretend to any other office than to serve and obey them" (T 2.3.3). And its corollary, deliberately provocative: "'Tis not contrary to reason to prefer the destruction of the whole world to the scratching of my finger" (T 2.3.3).

**Textual basis.** T 2.3.3 ("Of the influencing motives of the will"). The "calm passions" — steady desires like the general appetite to good, often mistaken for reason because they operate without felt turbulence — belong to the same section's analysis and prevent a crude reading: Hume's point is not that passion means violent emotion.

**Objection.** The standing objection is the **anti-Humean theory of motivation** (Thomas Nagel's *The Possibility of Altruism*, 1970, and John McDowell's papers are the benchmarks): some beliefs — for instance, the belief that one has a reason to act — seem to motivate without any antecedent desire doing the work, the desire being *derived from* the belief rather than prior to it. If so, premise 3's claim that causal and practical reasoning engage only pre-existing desires begs the question by assuming the Humean psychology it is meant to prove. A second objection targets the "ought" in the conclusion: from the descriptive claim that reason *cannot* motivate alone, nothing normative follows about what it *ought* to be — Hume's slogan seems to commit, in reverse, the is–ought slide of Argument 10.

**Reply.** Hume's own qualifications answer the crudest versions: reason retains absolute authority over truth, passions founded on falsehood are genuinely "unreasonable," and much of what passes for reason in motivation is calm passion — so the slogan is a thesis about the *division of labor*, not a license for irrationality, as the entry insists. The literature's defense (Michael Smith's *The Moral Problem*, 1994) formalizes premise 2 as the direction-of-fit doctrine: beliefs aim to fit the world, desires aim to make the world fit them, and a single state cannot do both at once — which, if sound, makes Hume's psychology a conceptual truth rather than an empirical wager. The Nagel–McDowell challenge remains live; the dossier records the argument as the founding statement of the Humean theory of motivation, with the dispute open.

**Argument 10. Is and ought: the unmarked transition**

*What it proves:* — and what exactly it proves is the dispute this entry must present as a dispute — at minimum, that moral writers slide from propositions about what *is* to propositions about what *ought* to be without explaining the inference, and that until the explanation is given, the deduction of the new relation from the old "seems altogether inconceivable" (T 3.1.1).

1. In every system of morality Hume has met with, the author proceeds for a while with the ordinary copulations of propositions — *is* and *is not*.
2. Then, imperceptibly, the copulations become *ought* and *ought not* — expressing, Hume notes, "some new relation or affirmation" not present in the premises.
3. The change is "of the last consequence": a reason ought to be given for what seems altogether inconceivable — how this new relation can be a deduction from others entirely different from it.
Therefore (on every reading) an author who derives ought from is owes an account of the step; the passage is the demand, whether or not it is also a verdict.

**Textual basis.** T 3.1.1, the closing paragraph of the section arguing that moral distinctions are not derived from reason (T 3.1.1.27). It is a short, almost parenthetical passage — a page at most — and its fame vastly exceeds its length.

**Objection and the dispute over the claim itself.** Three readings divide the literature. (i) **The categorical reading** ("Hume's Law"): the passage states a logical barrier — no valid deduction of an evaluative conclusion from purely factual premises, full stop — and it is the ancestor of the fact/value dichotomy. (ii) **The narrower anti-rationalist reading:** the passage is a corollary of its own section, aimed at the moral rationalists (Wollaston, Clarke) who claimed moral distinctions are demonstrable relations of ideas; Hume's demand is that *they* explain a derivation their systems require, not a universal law he is legislating. (iii) **MacIntyre's challenge** ("Hume on 'Is' and 'Ought'," 1959): Hume himself moves from facts about human nature — our needs, sentiments, and circumstances — to moral conclusions throughout Book III, so the categorical reading would convict Hume by his own rule; the passage must mean something narrower, such as a protest against derivations that ignore the sentiments facts themselves engage. Against the categorical reading specifically, John Searle's institutional counterexample (1964) argues that factual premises about a promise ("Jones uttered the words 'I promise to pay'") entail an obligation without any evaluative premise.

**Reply.** To Searle, the standard reply (the literature's) is that the entailment smuggles in the normativity of the institution: "promise" and "obligation" already carry the ought inside the ostensibly factual premise, so the derivation is analytic bookkeeping, not a crossing of Hume's gap. On the central dispute, the dossier follows the entry: what is not in question is the passage's force as a demand for an explanation of the transition; whether Hume intended a strict barrier, a challenge aimed at rationalists, or something his own system had to navigate around, remains genuinely contested — and Hume's own sentimentalist ethics, which grounds oughts in facts about human sentiment, can be and has been recruited on both sides. No reading commands the field, and the file settles none.

**Argument 11. Moral distinctions are not derived from reason: sentiment, sympathy, and the general point of view**

*What it proves:* the positive foundation of Hume's ethics, in three stages: moral distinctions cannot be the work of reason (they are neither relations of ideas nor matters of fact, and they move us as reason cannot); they are therefore perceptions of sentiment; and because raw sentiment varies, moral judgment corrects itself by sympathy disciplined into a general point of view — which is what makes shared moral verdicts possible.

1. If moral distinctions were derived from reason, virtue and vice would consist either in relations of ideas or in matters of fact (T 3.1.1).
2. Not relations: the relations the rationalists propose — resemblance, contrariety, causation — hold identically where no morality is present. Hume's example: a sapling that grows to overtop and destroy its parent oak stands in all the same relations as a son who murders his father; if parricide's vice were a relation, the oak would be guilty of it (T 3.1.1).
3. Not a matter of fact in the act: examine a case of wilful murder from every angle, and you find only facts — motives, volitions, consequences. "You never can find [the vice], till you turn your reflexion into your own breast, and find a sentiment of disapprobation, which arises in you, towards this action" (T 3.1.1). The vice is not found in the object; it is felt by the spectator.
4. And morality *influences* action and affection, which (by Argument 9) reason alone cannot do.
Therefore, moral distinctions are derived not from reason but from a moral sense or sentiment (T 3.1.1–2).
5. The sentiment is socialized and corrected by **sympathy** — the mechanism by which others' feelings are communicated to us — and by adopting "steady and general points of view," from which variations of position, distance, and interest are ironed out, as they are in judgments of beauty and health (T 3.3.1). The *Enquiry Concerning the Principles of Morals* restates the system with **utility** at its center: qualities please us because they are useful or agreeable to their possessor or to others, and utility pleases through humanity or sympathy (EPM 5); the final sentence on character rests on sentiment (EPM 9; EPM App. 1).

**Textual basis.** T 3.1.1 (the negative argument; the oak and the murder), 3.1.2 (the moral sense), 3.3.1 (sympathy and the general point of view); EPM 5 ("Why Utility Pleases"), EPM 9 (conclusion), and EPM App. 1 (reason and sentiment in moral distinctions). The *Enquiry* version Hume considered "incomparably the best" of his works.

**Objection.** Sentiment varies — between persons, ages, and cultures — and a morality founded on feeling seems condemned to relativism or to reporting mere taste. The general point of view is offered as the corrective, but critics press two questions. First, what makes one standpoint the *general* one, and who occupies it — if the correction is rational (a rule for weighing perspectives), reason has been readmitted through the back door, contrary to premise 4; if it is merely the standpoint of one's own culture, the relativism returns. Second, sympathy itself is partial — strongest for the near and dear — and Hume's corrections (abstracting from our peculiar position) may describe an achievement rarer and more fragile than the convergence of actual moral judgment requires explained.

**Reply.** Hume's own reply is the analogy built into premise 5: we correct sentiments by a general standpoint in exactly the way we correct perceptions by distance and position, without supposing the correction is an act of demonstrative reason — it is sentiment itself, steadied and informed, and moral language's very existence (we speak of characters as if their qualities were stable) shows the correction is constantly performed. The literature's defense (Annette Baier's work is the fullest) reads the general point of view as a social achievement rather than a private technique: moral judgment is negotiated in conversation from a common standpoint, which explains both its real convergence and its real failures. Whether the corrected sentiment deserves the name "sentiment" rather than "practical reason" is part of the standing dispute about how sharp Hume's reason/sentiment line can remain — the argument founds modern sentimentalism, and the dispute is its inheritance.

**Argument 12. Justice as an artificial virtue: convention without contract**

*What it proves:* that the rules of justice — stability of possession, its transfer by consent, the performance of promises — arise neither from a natural instinct nor from an actual contract, but from a convention: a shared sense of common interest, mutually expressed and self-reinforcing, which Hume compares to two men rowing a boat together without ever having made a promise.

1. The **circumstances of justice** (T 3.2.2): nature provides goods with moderate scarcity, and human generosity is confined — we care strongly for ourselves and our near circle, weakly for strangers. Were goods abundant, or benevolence universal, rules of property would be pointless; were scarcity extreme or selfishness total, they would be impossible.
2. No natural motive can do the job directly: public benevolence is too weak and remote, private benevolence too partial, to fix stable possession; unchecked, each person's grasping dissolves society (T 3.2.1–2).
3. The remedy is a **convention**: "a general sense of common interest; which sense all the members of the society express to one another, and which induces them to regulate their conduct by certain rules." It is not a promise: "Two men, who pull the oars of a boat, do it by an agreement or convention, tho' they have never given promises to each other" (T 3.2.2). Each abstains from others' goods on the expectation that others will do likewise, and the expectation, by being generally fulfilled, sustains itself.
4. Once the convention is established, sympathy with the public interest it serves attaches moral approval to its observance: justice becomes a virtue — an **artificial** one, its obligation arising from human artifice rather than a natural sentiment directed at each act (T 3.2.2; EPM 3).
5. Because the utility lies in the whole scheme, the rules must be general and inflexible: a particular act of justice may occasionally run against public or private interest — restoring goods to a profligate, say — yet the rule stands, since the scheme's benefits depend on its not being re-judged case by case (T 3.2.6; EPM App. 3).
Therefore, justice is founded on convention and utility, not on contract or natural law: contract theory gets the order backwards, since promises themselves owe their obligation to a prior convention of the same kind (T 3.2.5).

**Textual basis.** T 3.2.1–2 (the origin of justice; the rowing boat at 3.2.2), with the inflexibility of the rules at 3.2.6 and the conventional basis of promising at 3.2.5; EPM 3 ("Of Justice") and EPM App. 3 (further considerations on justice and the scheme's utility).

**Objection.** Hume himself supplies the sharpest one: the **sensible knave** of EPM 9, who grants the general rule — honesty is the best policy — but takes advantage of every exception where knavery will pay and go undetected. If justice's obligation rests on interest and convention, the knave's reasoning seems unanswerable on Humean grounds; Hume's candor here is famous — he in effect concedes that if a man thinks this reasoning requires an answer, it would be difficult to find one that will appear to him satisfactory and convincing (EPM 9). A second, structural objection presses premise 3 for circularity: a convention sustained by each party's expectation that others will conform sounds very like the trust and rule-following it is meant to explain — critics ask whether Hume has explained obligation's origin or quietly presupposed it.

**Reply.** Hume's own replies are partial and are labeled as such. On the rules' rigidity, his answer is systemic: the convention's benefits exist only if compliance is general and predictable, so flexibility is self-defeating — the inflexibility the objection complains of is the mechanism, not a defect (EPM App. 3). On the knave, his reply is that the knave's calculus is usually wrong — honesty is in fact the best policy across a life, and the knave sacrifices inward peace and self-approval, "the invaluable enjoyment of a character," for gains Hume plainly thinks paltry; but he concedes the exceptional case resists refutation, and the dossier records the concession rather than papering it over. On circularity, the literature's defense is that Hume's convention requires no antecedent obligation — only perceived interest and expressed expectation, bootstrapped gradually (as languages and money arise, in his own analogies) — so expectation can precede obligation and generate it. Whether gradual bootstrapping fully answers the charge remains debated; the argument nonetheless reframed the subject, replacing contract with convention as the standing model of how institutions arise.

**Argument 13. Mitigated skepticism: the conclusion of the system**

*What it proves:* strictly, it proves nothing — and that is its point. It is the system's self-assessment: the skeptical arguments of Arguments 1–3 are genuine and unanswered by reason; but belief is not reason's to give or withhold, nature determines us to believe anyway, and the philosophy that results from holding both truths at once is a mitigated, academic skepticism — modest in its pretensions, confined to what experience can bear, and fully compatible with dining, conversing, and playing backgammon.

1. The skeptical results stand. Reason cannot justify induction (Argument 2), cannot find necessity in objects (Argument 3), and — pressed to the end of Book I of the *Treatise* — cannot even certify itself: every judgment of reason is fallible, and checking it generates a regress that would annihilate all belief and knowledge (T 1.4.1, 1.4.7).
2. But the resulting doubt cannot be lived. Hume's description of his own case after Book I's arguments: "Most fortunately it happens, that since reason is incapable of dispelling these clouds, nature herself suffices to that purpose, and cures me of this philosophical melancholy and delirium... I dine, I play a game of back-gammon, I converse, and am merry with my friends" (T 1.4.7).
3. The cure is not a counter-argument. Book I's recurring remedy is frankly anti-rational: "carelessness and in-attention" (T 1.4.2, of the senses; the pattern governs 1.4.7's conclusion) — an hour of common life dissolves what no refutation could.
4. What survives the cure, reflectively endorsed, is **mitigated skepticism** (E 12): a modest philosophy that (i) confines enquiry to subjects proportioned to our faculties — abstract reasoning about quantity, and experimental reasoning about fact; (ii) cultivates diffidence even there; and (iii) applies the fork as a final test, committing to the flames any volume containing neither, "for it can contain nothing but sophistry and illusion" (E 12.34).
Therefore, the system's destination: Pyrrhonian doubt is the honest result of reason alone and is psychologically impossible to sustain; mitigated skepticism — skeptical about foundations, naturalistic about practice — is the only philosophy a creature like us can actually hold.

**Textual basis.** E 12 ("Of the Academical or Sceptical Philosophy"; the flames at E 12.34); T 1.4.7 ("Conclusion of this book") for the crisis and the backgammon cure, with the carelessness-and-inattention remedy stated at T 1.4.2. The guide's [Section XII discussion](../../study/hume-enquiry.md) maps the *Enquiry* form.

**Objection.** Thomas Reid pressed the charge the entry records: Hume escapes skepticism only by inconsistency — he trusts "nature" and common sense at the backgammon table precisely where his arguments in the study forbid trust, and a skepticism that must be suspended hourly to be livable has not been answered but abandoned. The modern form (Barry Stroud's study of Hume is the locus) sharpens it: explaining *why* we believe, naturalistically, leaves the skeptical *verdict* — that our beliefs are unjustified — entirely untouched, and Hume's contentment with that split may show not a stable philosophy but two incompatible ones stapled together.

**Reply.** The naturalistic reading (Kemp Smith) replies that the two tiers are the position, not a failure of nerve: Hume's skepticism is directed at the *rationalist claim* that our beliefs require and possess rational foundations — a claim he refutes — while his naturalism describes the foundations beliefs actually have, which need no permission from reason to operate. On this reading the backgammon passage is not an escape from the argument but its conclusion enacted. Whether the split is stable — whether one can sincerely judge a belief unjustified and go on holding it undisturbed — is the deepest standing question about Hume, and the dossier leaves it open, as the literature does: it is the form in which Hume's challenge to philosophy's self-image survives.
