# Hume: The Arguments
**English** | [简体中文](../../zh/focus-studies/david-hume/04-arguments.md)

*Focus study · [Dossier home](README.md) · Library entry: [David Hume](../../eras/03-early-modern/david-hume.md) · The system these arguments build: [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md) · Study guide: [*An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding*](../../study/hume-enquiry.md)*

Each argument below is reconstructed in numbered premises in the house style: what the argument proves, the reconstruction, its textual basis, the strongest standard objection, and the best reply, Hume's own where he gives one and the literature's where he does not, labeled as such. Citations use the **Clarendon paragraph references** of the Oxford critical editions, which the Norton and Norton *Treatise*, the Beauchamp *Enquiries* and Millican and Merivale's *Hume Texts Online* (davidhume.org) all print. So (T 1.3.6.11) means *Treatise* Book 1, Part 3, Section 6, paragraph 11; (EHU 4.18) means the first *Enquiry*, Section 4, paragraph 18; (T App.21) means paragraph 21 of the Appendix Hume added to the *Treatise* in 1740; and (Abstract 15) means paragraph 15 of the anonymous *Abstract* of the *Treatise* (1740). Quotations come from *Hume Texts Online* and keep Hume's spelling. The *Treatise* writes "'tis", "shou'd" and "betwixt"; the *Enquiry*, revised over three decades, mostly does not.

The arguments follow the eight-week plan, so each starts from the *Enquiry* where it can and then turns to the longer, less guarded *Treatise*. Arguments 7 and 8 have no full *Enquiry* counterpart.

**Argument 1. The copy principle and the missing shade of blue (*Enquiry* 2; *Treatise* 1.1.1)**

*What it proves:* that every simple idea is copied from a simple impression, so that any term in philosophy can be tested by asking which impression its idea came from. Hume then offers a case that seems to break the rule and declines to give the rule up.

Hume divides the perceptions of the mind into two classes "distinguished by their different degrees of force and vivacity" (EHU 2.3). The livelier are **impressions**, "when we hear, or see, or feel, or love, or hate, or desire, or will"; the fainter are **ideas**, which we have "when we reflect on any of those sensations or movements" (EHU 2.3). The imagination seems free, yet its power "amounts to no more than the faculty of compounding, transposing, augmenting, or diminishing the materials afforded us by the senses and experience" (EHU 2.5).

1. Every complex idea resolves, on analysis, into simple ideas.
2. Every simple idea we examine turns out to be "copied from a similar impression" (EHU 2.6). Even the idea of God comes from "augmenting, without limit" the goodness and wisdom we find in our own minds (EHU 2.6).
3. Where a sense is missing, the matching ideas are missing too: "A blind man can form no notion of colours; a deaf man of sounds" (EHU 2.7). Restore the sense and the ideas follow.
4. Impressions always come first in time, so "our impressions are the causes of our ideas, not our ideas of our impressions" (T 1.1.1.8).
Therefore all our ideas, "or more feeble perceptions are copies of our impressions or more lively ones" (EHU 2.5).

The principle is a tool as well as a thesis. "When we entertain, therefore, any suspicion, that a philosophical term is employed without any meaning or idea (as is but too frequent), we need but enquire, from what impression is that supposed idea derived?" (EHU 2.9). Arguments 5 and 8 put this question to necessary connexion and to the self.

Then comes the counter-example. A man has seen every colour for thirty years except one shade of blue. Lay out the shades from darkest to lightest with that one missing, and "he will perceive a blank, where that shade is wanting" (EHU 2.8). Can he imagine it? Hume answers: "I believe there are few but will be of opinion that he can." The case shows "that the simple ideas are not always, in every instance, derived from the correspondent impressions; though this instance is so singular, that it is scarcely worth our observing, and does not merit, that for it alone we should alter our general maxim" (EHU 2.8). The *Treatise* has the same passage almost word for word (T 1.1.1.10), five paragraphs after saying that for simple perceptions "the rule here holds without any exception" (T 1.1.1.5).

**Textual basis.** EHU 2.1–9 with the note to 2.9; T 1.1.1.1–12; Abstract 5–7.

**Objection.** The principle cannot do the critical work Hume wants. Its support is inductive: we examine ideas and find impressions behind them (premise 2). Hume himself then produces an exception. A generalisation with an admitted exception cannot show that a disputed idea, such as necessary connexion or the self, is empty, because the disputed idea may simply be another exception. Thomas Reid's *Inquiry into the Human Mind* (1764) went further: our notions of extension, hardness and figure copy no sensation, since a sensation resembles nothing outside the mind. Jonathan Bennett (*Locke, Berkeley, Hume: Central Themes*, 1971) argued that Hume confuses where ideas come from with what words mean. See [Thomas Reid](../../eras/03-early-modern/thomas-reid.md).

**Reply.** Hume's own reply is short. The case is "so singular" that the maxim stands (EHU 2.8), and the challenge in EHU 2.6 is open: anyone who denies the principle need only produce an idea with no impression behind it. The literature has added two points. First, the missing shade is not a new kind of idea: it is built from impressions of neighbouring shades by the same augmenting and diminishing that EHU 2.5 already allows, so a principle restated to allow ideas formed from impressions of resembling items survives it. Don Garrett defends a reading along these lines in *Cognition and Commitment in Hume's Philosophy* (1997). Second, Hume rarely uses the principle to declare a word meaningless; in Argument 5 it finds what the idea of necessity contains. Reid's deeper objection, to the theory of ideas Hume shares with [Locke](../../eras/03-early-modern/john-locke.md) and [Berkeley](../../eras/03-early-modern/george-berkeley.md), returns in Argument 7.

**Argument 2. Hume's fork: relations of ideas and matters of fact (*Enquiry* 4.1–2; *Treatise* 1.3.1)**

*What it proves:* that every object of reasoning is either a relation of ideas, certain because its denial is a contradiction, or a matter of fact, whose denial is always conceivable and which only experience can establish.

1. "ALL the objects of human reason or enquiry may naturally be divided into two kinds, to wit, Relations of Ideas, and Matters of Fact" (EHU 4.1).
2. Relations of ideas include geometry, algebra and arithmetic, "every affirmation, which is either intuitively or demonstratively certain." They are "discoverable by the mere operation of thought" (EHU 4.1).
3. "The contrary of every matter of fact is still possible; because it can never imply a contradiction" (EHU 4.2).
4. Whatever is distinctly conceivable implies no contradiction, and so cannot be shown false by demonstration (EHU 4.2, 4.18).
Therefore no matter of fact can be proved by reasoning *a priori*: "That the sun will not rise to-morrow is no less intelligible a proposition, and implies no more contradiction, than the affirmation, that it will rise" (EHU 4.2). Matters of fact beyond present perception and memory must be reached some other way, and Argument 3 asks how.

The fork closes the book as a rule for libraries. Take a volume "of divinity or school metaphysics" and ask whether it contains "any abstract reasoning concerning quantity or number" or "any experimental reasoning concerning matter of fact and existence." If neither: "Commit it then to the flames: For it can contain nothing but sophistry and illusion" (EHU 12.34).

The *Treatise* draws the line differently. Of seven "philosophical relations" it keeps four as objects of knowledge (T 1.3.1.2), and it treats geometry as less exact than arithmetic (T 1.3.1.4–6). The *Enquiry* puts geometry on the certain side.

**Textual basis.** EHU 4.1–2; EHU 12.25–34; T 1.3.1.1–7. The name "Hume's fork" is a later label; Hume never uses it.

**Objection.** Two objections are standard. (i) The fork fails its own test. The claim that every object of reason falls on one side or the other is neither a demonstrable relation of ideas nor an obvious report of experience, so by EHU 12.34 it should go to the flames. The same charge was later pressed against the verification principle of the logical positivists, who took the fork as their model; A. J. Ayer's *Language, Truth and Logic* (1936) is the main text. (ii) [Kant](../../eras/03-early-modern/immanuel-kant.md) argued in the *Prolegomena* (1783) and the *Critique of Pure Reason* (1781, 1787) that the division leaves out a third class, the synthetic *a priori*: judgments that add to our knowledge, as matters of fact do, but are known independently of experience, as relations of ideas are. He counted arithmetic, geometry and the principle that every event has a cause among them. On this view Hume put mathematics on the wrong side, and with it lost the means to answer his own doubts about causation. W. V. O. Quine's "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" (1951) denied that any sharp line between truths of meaning and truths of fact can be drawn. See [Vienna Circle and logical positivism](../../eras/05-20th-century/vienna-circle-logical-positivism.md) and [Quine](../../eras/05-20th-century/w-v-o-quine.md).

**Reply.** The literature's. To (i): the fork is not offered as a demonstration. It is a finding of Hume's science of man about what kinds of reasoning the mind performs, so it sits on the matter-of-fact side, open to revision by experience. Ayer's criterion of meaning is a more ambitious claim. To (ii): the fork cannot settle by definition whether there is synthetic *a priori* knowledge. A Humean can reply that Kant's examples are either analytic or not known *a priori* after all, as many took non-Euclidean geometry and relativity to show for physical space. Quine's attack, if it succeeds, does not restore Kant's class; it removes the distinction both relied on.

**Argument 3. The problem of induction (*Enquiry* 4.14–23; *Treatise* 1.3.6)**

*What it proves:* that no argument, demonstrative or probable, can show that the unobserved will resemble the observed, so that our inferences from experience are not founded on reasoning.

Reasoning about unobserved matters of fact rests on cause and effect (EHU 4.4), known only from experience: Adam "could not have inferred from the fluidity, and transparency of water, that it would suffocate him" (EHU 4.6). Hume then asks: "What is the foundation of all conclusions from experience?" (EHU 4.14).

1. Bread has nourished us; we expect that like bread will nourish us again. The two propositions "are far from being the same," and the step between them is "an inference, which wants to be explained" (EHU 4.16).
2. The step is not intuitive. If it is made by reasoning, there must be "a medium" that links the premises to the conclusion (EHU 4.16).
3. All reasoning is either demonstrative, concerning relations of ideas, or "moral reasoning, or that concerning matter of fact and existence" (EHU 4.18).
4. There is no demonstrative argument, "since it implies no contradiction, that the course of nature may change" (EHU 4.18).
5. Any probable argument would rest on cause and effect, which rests on experience, and "all our experimental conclusions proceed upon the supposition, that the future will be conformable to the past." To prove that supposition by probable arguments "must be evidently going in a circle, and taking that for granted, which is the very point in question" (EHU 4.19).
Therefore "it is not reasoning which engages us to suppose the past resembling the future" (EHU 4.23).

Two confirmations follow. Reason would draw the conclusion as well from one instance as from a hundred, and we do not (EHU 4.20). Infants and animals learn from experience too, so the argument, if there were one, could not be obscure (EHU 4.23).

In the *Treatise*, reason would need the principle "that the course of nature continues always uniformly the same" (T 1.3.6.4), which probability cannot prove since it presupposes it (T 1.3.6.7). "We suppose, but are never able to prove, that there must be a resemblance betwixt those objects, of which we have had experience, and those which lie beyond the reach of our discovery" (T 1.3.6.11).

**What Hume does not say.** He does not say that we should stop trusting experience. "None but a fool or madman will ever pretend to dispute the authority of experience, or to reject that great guide of human life" (EHU 4.20). How strong his conclusion is remains disputed. The traditional reading, from Bertrand Russell's *The Problems of Philosophy* (1912, ch. 6) to Barry Stroud's *Hume* (1977), takes Hume to show that inductive beliefs have no rational justification. Garrett (1997) and David Owen (*Hume's Reason*, 1999) read "reason" here as a specific faculty of inference, and the conclusion as about how the mind works. Peter Millican's reconstructions support a version of the traditional reading.

**Objection.** P. F. Strawson, in *Introduction to Logical Theory* (1952), argued that the demand for a justification of induction is confused. Asking whether it is reasonable to follow inductive evidence is like asking whether the law is legal, since being inductively supported is part of what "reasonable" means. A different line is [Karl Popper](../../eras/05-20th-century/karl-popper.md)'s in *The Logic of Scientific Discovery* (1934, English 1959): induction cannot be justified, and science does not need it, since conjecture and refutation use only deductive logic.

**Reply.** The literature's. To Strawson: even if "reasonable" means "inductively supported," one can still ask whether inductively supported beliefs tend to be true, and that question is the one Hume raised. Wesley Salmon pressed this against Strawson in the 1950s and 1960s. To Popper: relying on a well-tested theory for tomorrow's bridge assumes it will go on surviving tests, which is inductive. Nelson Goodman's *Fact, Fiction, and Forecast* (1955) made the problem harder rather than easier. Evidence that all emeralds so far observed are green equally fits "all emeralds are grue," where "grue" means green if observed before some future date and blue otherwise. Hume's own reply is the "sceptical solution" of Argument 4: the step is made by custom, not by argument. See [Bertrand Russell](../../eras/05-20th-century/bertrand-russell.md) and [Austin and Strawson](../../eras/05-20th-century/austin-and-strawson.md).

**Argument 4. Custom and belief: the sceptical solution (*Enquiry* 5; *Treatise* 1.3.7–8)**

*What it proves:* that the step from past to future is taken by custom, a principle of human nature, and that belief differs from mere imagining by a felt manner of conception, not by any added idea.

1. The inference from experience is not made by reasoning (Argument 3).
2. Yet after the "constant conjunction of two objects, heat and flame, for instance, weight and solidity, we are determined by custom alone to expect the one from the appearance of the other" (EHU 5.5).
3. Custom, a known principle, explains why a hundred instances move us when one does not, which reason could not (EHU 5.5; EHU 4.20).
Therefore "Custom, then, is the great guide of human life" (EHU 5.6). The *Abstract* says it most sharply: "'Tis not, therefore, reason, which is the guide of life, but custom" (Abstract 16).

The solution is "sceptical" because it explains the inference without justifying it. It also says that the inference is not optional: belief in such circumstances is "as unavoidable as to feel the passion of love, when we receive benefits," and these operations "are a species of natural instincts, which no reasoning or process of the thought and understanding is able, either to produce, or to prevent" (EHU 5.8).

The section then asks what belief is.

4. We can imagine anything we can believe, and imagine its contrary, since no matter of fact is demonstrable (Argument 2).
5. If belief were an extra idea joined to a conception, we could join it to any fiction at will, "and consequently be able to believe whatever it pleases; contrary to what we find by daily experience" (EHU 5.10).
6. So belief must be "some sentiment or feeling, which is annexed to the latter, not to the former, and which depends not on the will" (EHU 5.11).
Therefore "belief is nothing but a more vivid, lively, forcible, firm, steady conception of an object, than what the imagination alone is ever able to attain" (EHU 5.12).

The *Treatise* had defined it more briefly as "A lively idea related to or associated with a present impression" (T 1.3.7.5), and drew a bold consequence: "all probable reasoning is nothing but a species of sensation" (T 1.3.8.12).

Custom produces "a kind of pre-established harmony between the course of nature and the succession of our ideas" (EHU 5.21).

**Textual basis.** EHU 5.1–22; T 1.3.7.1–7; T 1.3.8.1–13; T App.2–9, where Hume revised the account of belief; Abstract 15–22.

**Objection.** Two objections recur. (i) Reid argued in the *Essays on the Intellectual Powers* (1785) that vivacity cannot be what makes a state a belief. A reader of a gripping novel conceives its scenes vividly without believing them, while a dim memory of a dull fact is still believed. (ii) The solution is no solution. If custom only explains why we infer, our inferences have no more warrant than a dog's expectations. The "harmony" of EHU 5.21 even seems to assert the correspondence Argument 3 said we cannot establish.

**Reply.** To (i), Hume's own. He had met the novel case already. Two people read the same book, one "as a romance, and another as a true history"; they have the same ideas, but the believer "has a more lively conception of all the incidents" (T 1.3.7.8). In the Appendix and the *Enquiry* he also moved from brightness to the role belief plays, a "firm, steady" conception that gives ideas "a superior influence on the passions and imagination" (EHU 5.12; T App.2–3). To (ii), the literature's. Hume does not only describe custom; he also argues that some customs correct others. In T 1.3.13 he sets out "general rules," and T 1.3.15 gives "Rules by which to judge of causes and effects." Louis Loeb (*Stability and Justification in Hume's Treatise*, 2002) and Garrett (1997) read these as Hume's standard of justified belief: beliefs from mechanisms that survive reflection are better. The "harmony" is an observation within experience, not a proof.

**Argument 5. The idea of necessary connexion (*Enquiry* 7; *Treatise* 1.3.14)**

*What it proves:* that we have no impression of power in bodies or in our wills, and that the idea of necessary connexion is copied from a feeling in the mind, the customary transition from cause to effect.

Argument 1 supplies the question: from what impression is the idea of power or necessary connexion derived (EHU 2.9)?

1. Every idea is copied from an impression (Argument 1).
2. In a single instance of bodies acting we see only succession: "The impulse of one billiard-ball is attended with motion in the second. This is the whole that appears to the outward senses" (EHU 7.6).
3. Nor do we find power in the will. That the body moves when we will is known only by experience, and the means "must for ever escape our most diligent enquiry" (EHU 7.10). Command over our own ideas is no clearer (EHU 7.20).
4. So in no single instance is there any impression of connexion: "All events seem entirely loose and separate. One event follows another; but we never can observe any tye between them. They seem conjoined, but never connected" (EHU 7.26).
5. Many similar instances differ from one only in this: "after a repetition of similar instances, the mind is carried by habit, upon the appearance of one event, to expect its usual attendant" (EHU 7.28).
6. "This connexion, therefore, which we feel in the mind, this customary transition of the imagination from one object to its usual attendant, is the sentiment or impression, from which we form the idea of power or necessary connexion" (EHU 7.28).
Therefore the idea of necessary connexion is copied from an impression of reflection, and the *Treatise* draws the bold moral: "necessity is something, that exists in the mind, not in objects" (T 1.3.14.22).

We put it in objects because the mind "has a great propensity to spread itself on external objects" (T 1.3.14.25). Hume then gives two definitions. A cause is "an object, followed by another, and where all the objects, similar to the first, are followed by objects similar to the second"; or "an object followed by another, and whose appearance always conveys the thought to that other" (EHU 7.29). The first is regularity in the objects. The second is the mind's transition.

**Textual basis.** EHU 7.1–30 with notes; T 1.3.2, 1.3.14.1–36; Abstract 26. EHU 7.9–20 targets [Locke](../../eras/03-early-modern/john-locke.md)'s view that we find power in our will; EHU 7.21–25 targets the occasionalism of [Malebranche](../../eras/03-early-modern/nicolas-malebranche.md).

**Objection.** Reid, in the *Essays on the Active Powers* (1788), attacked both halves. Against premise 3, he held that our own exertions give us a notion of active power. Against the first definition, he gave a counter-example: night has always followed day and day night, yet neither is the cause of the other. A second, interpretive objection divides Hume's readers. On the "New Hume" reading, Hume never denied that real necessary connexions exist in objects. He denied only that we perceive them or can form more than a "relative" idea of them. Passages that speak of "secret powers" (EHU 4.16) and of "ultimate springs and principles" that are hidden from us (EHU 4.12) are read as references to real causal powers beyond our grasp. The main texts are John P. Wright, *The Sceptical Realism of David Hume* (1983), Edward Craig, *The Mind of God and the Works of Man* (1987), and Galen Strawson, *The Secret Connexion* (1989).

**Reply.** To Reid, the literature's. Hume's rules for judging causes (T 1.3.15) direct us to the common cause of day and night, the earth's rotation, which fuller experience reveals. Against exertion, Hume's answer is already in EHU 7.10–20: feeling effort is not perceiving how effort works. To the New Hume, the reply of Kenneth Winkler ("The New Hume," *Philosophical Review*, 1991), Simon Blackburn ("Hume and Thick Connexions," 1990) and Millican. Hume says that when we try to conceive a power beyond the two definitions, "We have no idea of this connexion; nor even any distinct notion what it is we desire to know" (EHU 7.29). By the copy principle, Hume could not consistently believe in powers he says we cannot conceive. "Secret powers" is read as shorthand for finer regularities not yet found. Both sides cite real passages, and the dispute is open. Most agree on a narrower point: our *idea* of necessity, for Hume, is derived from a feeling and projected onto objects.

**Argument 6. Liberty and necessity: the reconciling project (*Enquiry* 8)**

*What it proves:* that the dispute over free will has been verbal, since everyone accepts necessity in human action in Hume's sense, and everyone accepts liberty in the only sense that matters for morals, and the two are compatible.

A dispute two thousand years old, Hume says, probably turns on an ambiguity (EHU 8.1). "I hope, therefore, to make it appear, that all men have ever agreed in the doctrine both of necessity and of liberty, according to any reasonable sense, which can be put on these terms; and that the whole controversy has hitherto turned merely upon words" (EHU 8.3).

1. Necessity, as Argument 5 established, is nothing more than constant conjunction plus the mind's inference from one object to the other (EHU 8.5, 8.27).
2. Human actions show the same constant conjunction with motives, character and circumstances: "The same motives always produce the same actions" (EHU 8.7).
3. Everyone reasons from motive to action as from cause to effect: a prisoner weighs his gaoler's obstinacy as he weighs the walls (EHU 8.19).
4. So everyone accepts necessity in human action, in the only sense in which there is necessity anywhere (EHU 8.16, 8.22).
5. Liberty, as applied to action, cannot mean the absence of all connexion with motives, which would be chance. "By liberty, then, we can only mean a power of acting or not acting, according to the determinations of the will" (EHU 8.23).
6. This "hypothetical liberty is universally allowed to belong to every one, who is not a prisoner and in chains" (EHU 8.23).
Therefore liberty and necessity, rightly understood, are compatible: "Here then is no subject of dispute" (EHU 8.23).

Necessity is even required for morality. Laws presuppose that rewards and punishments work regularly (EHU 8.28). Blame attaches to persons through their character, and an action that "proceeded from nothing in him, that is durable and constant" could not make anyone guilty. On the doctrine that denies necessity, "a man is as pure and untainted, after having committed the most horrid crime, as at the first moment of his birth" (EHU 8.29). Liberty is required too: actions done under external violence earn neither praise nor blame (EHU 8.31).

People deny necessity partly from "a false sensation or seeming experience" of indifference when we act (EHU 8.22 n. 18; T 2.3.2.2). The *Treatise* separates "the liberty of spontaniety, as it is call'd in the schools, and the liberty of indifference" (T 2.3.2.1).

**Textual basis.** EHU 8.1–36 with notes; T 2.3.1–2. The phrase "reconciling project" is Hume's own (EHU 8.23).

**Objection.** Reid in the *Active Powers* (1788) and Kant in the *Critique of Practical Reason* (1788) gave the classic objection. Hume's liberty is a power to do what one wills, and leaves untouched the question whether one could have willed otherwise. If the causes run back before his birth, his choices are no more his than a stone's fall. Kant called such "comparative" freedom a wretched subterfuge, no better than the freedom of a turnspit. Peter van Inwagen's *An Essay on Free Will* (1983) made this the "consequence argument": our acts follow from the laws and the remote past, neither of which is up to us.

**Reply.** Hume's own: an act not determined by character would be chance, which "is a mere negative word" (EHU 8.25), and would make the agent less responsible, not more (EHU 8.29). He leaves one objection unanswered. If God is the first cause of the whole chain, God seems to be the author of sin. Hume calls these "mysteries, which mere natural and unassisted reason is very unfit to handle" (EHU 8.36), and sends philosophy back to common life. The literature's reply comes from later compatibilists. P. F. Strawson ("Freedom and Resentment," 1962) argued, in a spirit close to Hume's, that resentment and gratitude are natural attitudes that no theory of determinism could lead us to give up. Harry Frankfurt ("Alternate Possibilities and Moral Responsibility," 1969) argued that responsibility does not require the ability to do otherwise. Hume's text is still the founding statement of the compatibilist side.

**Argument 7. Belief in body: the senses, reason and the imagination (*Treatise* 1.4.2; *Enquiry* 12.7–16)**

*What it proves:* that our belief in bodies with a continued existence, independent of the mind, comes neither from the senses nor from reason, but from the imagination; and that the philosophers' attempt to improve on it, by distinguishing perceptions from objects, has no support from reason or the imagination.

Hume refuses one question and takes up another: "We may well ask, What causes induce us to believe in the existence of body? but 'tis in vain to ask, Whether there be body or not? That is a point, which we must take for granted in all our reasonings" (T 1.4.2.1). The belief has two parts: that objects continue when not perceived, and that they are distinct from the mind (T 1.4.2.2).

1. The senses cannot give the idea of continued existence, since that would require them to operate when they are not operating (T 1.4.2.3).
2. The senses cannot give the idea of distinct existence either. They "convey to us nothing but a single perception, and never give us the least intimation of any thing beyond" (T 1.4.2.4).
3. Reason does not produce it. Children and peasants hold it without argument, and "philosophy informs us, that every thing, which appears to the mind, is nothing but a perception, and is interrupted, and dependent on the mind" (T 1.4.2.14).
4. So the belief comes from the imagination, working on two features of some impressions: their **constancy**, as when the mountains and trees "return upon me without the least alteration" after I look away (T 1.4.2.18), and their **coherence**, as when the fire has burned down in my absence by the amount it usually burns (T 1.4.2.19).
5. The imagination smooths over the gaps between resembling perceptions and feigns a continued existence (T 1.4.2.20–44).
6. Pressing one eye and seeing double shows that perceptions depend on our organs (T 1.4.2.45), so philosophers separate perceptions from objects. This "double existence" has no support from reason, since we never observe an object beside a perception (T 1.4.2.47), nor from the imagination (T 1.4.2.48).
Therefore the philosophical system is "the monstrous offspring of two principles, which are contrary to each other" (T 1.4.2.52).

The *Enquiry* is shorter. Instinct makes us take "the very images, presented by the senses, to be the external objects" (EHU 12.8), an opinion "soon destroyed by the slightest philosophy": the table seems to shrink as we step back, so what was present "was, therefore, nothing but its image" (EHU 12.9). Whether external objects cause our perceptions is a question of fact, "But here experience is, and must be entirely silent" (EHU 12.12), and Descartes' appeal to God's veracity is "a very unexpected circuit" (EHU 12.13). The upshot is a dilemma: the opinion of external existence, "if rested on natural instinct, is contrary to reason, and if referred to reason, is contrary to natural instinct" (EHU 12.16). A second argument, credited to [Berkeley](../../eras/03-early-modern/george-berkeley.md) (EHU 12.15 n. 32), extends mind-dependence from secondary to primary qualities (EHU 12.15–16).

The *Treatise* section ends in Hume's own doubt: "I feel myself at present of a quite contrary sentiment, and am more inclin'd to repose no faith at all in my senses, or rather imagination" (T 1.4.2.56). The doubt is "a malady, which can never be radically cur'd," and "Carelessness and in-attention alone can afford us any remedy" (T 1.4.2.57).

**Textual basis.** T 1.4.2.1–57; T 1.4.4 on the modern philosophy of primary and secondary qualities; EHU 12.7–16 with n. 32.

**Objection.** Reid's *Inquiry* (1764) located the trouble in a premise Hume shares with Descartes, Locke and Berkeley: the "ideal theory," on which the immediate objects of the mind are always its own perceptions. Grant it and Hume's conclusion follows. Reid denied it: in perception we are aware of the table itself, and conviction of its existence comes with the perceiving as a first principle of common sense. Hume's argument is then a reduction to absurdity of the theory of ideas. Later direct realists, including J. L. Austin in *Sense and Sensibilia* (1962), argued that the step in EHU 12.9, from the table's seeming to shrink to our seeing only an image, is a fallacy. See [Thomas Reid](../../eras/03-early-modern/thomas-reid.md).

**Reply.** Hume's own reply is the first sentence of the section. He does not ask whether there are bodies, and he says the belief is one "we must take for granted in all our reasonings" (T 1.4.2.1). He offers a natural history of the belief, and claims only that the philosophers' system has no better footing than the vulgar one. Here he and Reid agree more than Reid admitted: the belief is natural, irresistible and not the product of argument. To Austin, the literature's reply is that the argument can be restated without assuming we see images: how things look varies while the things do not, and the variable look needs an account. That debate is open. Hume's "carelessness and in-attention" (T 1.4.2.57) is the first form of the mitigated scepticism he defends in Argument 9.

**Argument 8. Personal identity: the bundle and the Appendix second thoughts (*Treatise* 1.4.6; App.10–21)**

*What it proves:* in Book 1, that we have no idea of a simple, persisting self, and that the identity we ascribe to the mind is a fiction of the imagination. In the Appendix, that this account of what unites the mind fails, for a reason Hume cannot remove.

Some philosophers, Hume says, think we are intimately conscious of a self, of "its perfect identity and simplicity" (T 1.4.6.1). He runs the copy principle against them.

1. Every real idea is derived from some one impression (T 1.4.6.2; Argument 1).
2. An impression giving rise to the idea of self "must continue invariably the same, thro' the whole course of our lives," since that is how the self is supposed to exist (T 1.4.6.2).
3. "But there is no impression constant and invariable. Pain and pleasure, grief and joy, passions and sensations succeed each other" (T 1.4.6.2).
4. Introspection confirms it: "when I enter most intimately into what I call myself, I always stumble on some particular perception or other, of heat or cold, light or shade, love or hatred, pain or pleasure. I never can catch myself at any time without a perception, and never can observe any thing but the perception" (T 1.4.6.3).
5. Perceptions "have no need of any thing to support their existence" (T 1.4.6.3).
Therefore there is no idea of a simple, persisting self, and we are "nothing but a bundle or collection of different perceptions, which succeed each other with an inconceivable rapidity, and are in a perpetual flux and movement" (T 1.4.6.4).

The mind is "a kind of theatre," but "They are the successive perceptions only, that constitute the mind" (T 1.4.6.4). We ascribe identity anyway because the imagination passes so easily along related perceptions that it feels like viewing one unchanging object (T 1.4.6.6). So "The identity, which we ascribe to the mind of man, is only a fictitious one" (T 1.4.6.15). The work is done by resemblance, which memory supplies, and causation, which makes the mind like "a republic or commonwealth" (T 1.4.6.18–19). Memory "does not so much produce as discover personal identity" (T 1.4.6.20). The hard questions about when identity begins and ends are "rather as grammatical than as philosophical difficulties" (T 1.4.6.21).

**The Appendix.** Less than two years after Book 1 appeared, Hume withdrew his confidence. "Upon a more strict review of the section concerning personal identity, I find myself involv'd in such a labyrinth, that, I must confess, I neither know how to correct my former opinions, nor how to render them consistent" (T App.10). He still accepts the case against a simple self (T App.11–19). The trouble is with the positive account. "If perceptions are distinct existences, they form a whole only by being connected together. But no connexions among distinct existences are ever discoverable by human understanding" (T App.20). And: "there are two principles, which I cannot render consistent; nor is it in my power to renounce either of them, viz. that all our distinct perceptions are distinct existences, and that the mind never perceives any real connexion among distinct existences" (T App.21). Either a simple subject or perceived connexions would end the difficulty; he has neither, and pleads "the privilege of a sceptic" (T App.21). The *Enquiry* drops the topic.

**What the Appendix problem is.** This is disputed, since the two principles are not inconsistent as stated. One common reading takes the problem to be that the felt transition that binds the bundle needs a mind to feel it, so the account presupposes what it explains. Don Garrett ("Hume's Self-Doubts about Personal Identity," *Philosophical Review*, 1981) argued that Hume's principles give no way to say why a perception belongs to one bundle and not to another that is qualitatively alike. Galen Strawson (*The Evident Connexion*, 2011) argued that a mind needs real connexions among its perceptions, which Hume's principles forbid him to find. No reading has won general agreement.

**Textual basis.** T 1.4.6.1–23; T App.10–21; T 2.1.11.4 on the "idea, or rather impression of ourselves." The Appendix was published with Book 3 in 1740.

**Objection.** Joseph Butler, in the dissertation "Of Personal Identity" appended to *The Analogy of Religion* (1736), had already argued against Locke that memory presupposes identity and cannot constitute it. Reid (*Essays on the Intellectual Powers*, 1785) added that a perception is the act of a perceiver, so a bundle of perceptions with no one perceiving is incoherent, and Hume's premise 4 has someone entering into himself. Book 2 of the *Treatise* seems to agree with the critics, since it says "the idea, or rather impression of ourselves is always intimately present with us" (T 2.1.11.4).

**Reply.** The literature's, since Hume never returned to the topic. First-person talk is ordinary speech, and introspection reports perceptions, not a perceiver distinct from them. To the tension with Book 2, Hume distinguished identity "as it regards our thought or imagination" from identity "as it regards our passions or the concern we take in ourselves" (T 1.4.6.5); the "impression of ourselves" in Book 2 can be read as the lively idea of the bundle, which is all Book 1 allows. On memory, Hume already agreed that it discovers identity more than it produces it (T 1.4.6.20). The fullest modern defence of a bundle view is Derek Parfit's *Reasons and Persons* (1984). Parfit argued that identity matters less than we think; what matters is psychological continuity, which holds in degrees. Whether that answers the Appendix problem, or only accepts Hume's conclusion that the questions are "grammatical" (T 1.4.6.21), is itself debated. See [Derek Parfit](../../eras/05-20th-century/derek-parfit.md).

**Argument 9. Mitigated scepticism (*Enquiry* 12.21–34; *Treatise* 1.4.7)**

*What it proves:* that the right response to the sceptical results of Arguments 3–8 is neither total doubt nor a return to dogmatism. Excessive scepticism cannot be lived and does no good. A **mitigated** or "academical" scepticism can be lived and does good: it makes us modest, and it confines enquiry to common life and to what experience can check.

Section 12 sorts sceptical objections. The **popular** ones, drawn from disagreement between nations and the shifting judgments of sickness and youth, are weak, because "the great subverter of Pyrrhonism or the excessive principles of scepticism, is action, and employment, and the occupations of common life" (EHU 12.21). The **philosophical** ones are strong. They are the results of Sections 4–7: all our reasoning about unobserved matters of fact rests on cause and effect, we have no idea of that relation beyond constant conjunction, and nothing but "custom or a certain instinct of our nature" carries us to the inference (EHU 12.22). Hume grants that these cannot be refuted. He argues instead about what follows from them.

1. The philosophical objections cannot be answered by argument (EHU 12.22).
2. Yet no one can hold total doubt for long: "Nature is always too strong for principle" (EHU 12.23).
3. If total doubt were held, it would do no good, since "all human life must perish, were his principles universally and steadily to prevail" (EHU 12.23).
4. A philosophy that cannot be held and would do harm if held should not be adopted as a way of thinking.
5. A sceptic who has felt the force of the objections, and has been returned to belief by nature, keeps two results: "a degree of doubt, and caution, and modesty, which, in all kinds of scrutiny and decision, ought for ever to accompany a just reasoner" (EHU 12.24), and "the limitation of our enquiries to such subjects as are best adapted to the narrow capacity of human understanding" (EHU 12.25).
Therefore the reasonable position is mitigated scepticism: believe and reason in common life, with modesty, and do not go beyond it.

The limitation is sharp. Demonstration reaches only "quantity and number" (EHU 12.27); since "Whatever is may not be" (EHU 12.28), existence can be proved only from cause and effect (EHU 12.29). A volume offering neither kind of reasoning can contain "nothing but sophistry and illusion" (EHU 12.34). Philosophy survives, because "philosophical decisions are nothing but the reflections of common life, methodized and corrected" (EHU 12.25).

The *Treatise* reaches the same point by a darker route. In T 1.4.1 reason, applied to its own fallibility again and again, "must, by continually diminishing the original evidence, at last reduce it to nothing" (T 1.4.1.8). In T 1.4.7 the choice is "betwixt a false reason and none at all" (T 1.4.7.7). Hume describes himself "inviron'd with the deepest darkness" (T 1.4.7.8) until nature cures him: "I dine, I play a game of back-gammon, I converse, and am merry with my friends" (T 1.4.7.9). He then states a rule for which beliefs keep their claim on us: "Where reason is lively, and mixes itself with some propensity, it ought to be assented to. Where it does not, it never can have any title to operate upon us" (T 1.4.7.11). Don Garrett (*Cognition and Commitment in Hume's Philosophy*, 1997) named this the **Title Principle**.

**Textual basis.** EHU 12.21–34; T 1.4.1.7–8; T 1.4.7.7–11. Section 12's opening paragraphs (EHU 12.3–5) set out Descartes' "antecedent" scepticism and the "consequent" scepticism that follows enquiry; EHU 12.6–20 treat the senses and abstract reasoning.

**Objection.** Thomas Reid (*An Inquiry into the Human Mind*, 1764) held that Hume's system, built on the theory of ideas, leads by its own logic to total scepticism, and that Hume escapes only by abandoning his principles when he leaves the study. Either the arguments are sound and we ought to doubt everything, or nature's beliefs are in order and the arguments contain an error, which Reid located in the theory of ideas. A second form asks what makes one propensity authoritative and another not. Superstition is also lively and mixes with propensity, so if nature's say-so is the test, Hume cannot condemn what he wants to condemn. See [Thomas Reid](../../eras/03-early-modern/thomas-reid.md).

**Reply.** Hume's own, in part. He does not claim that nature's beliefs are justified by argument. He claims that the arguments against them, though unanswerable, "can have no other tendency than to show the whimsical condition of mankind, who must act and reason and believe" (EHU 12.23). Reid's dilemma assumes that a belief must be either proved or given up. Hume denies that assumption, so the dilemma does not move him. To the second form, the Title Principle and the rules for judging causes (T 1.3.15) supply a filter. Reasoning that carries a propensity and survives correction by general rules keeps its title. Superstition fails because it rests on propensities that reflection on experience undoes, as Section 10 shows for miracle reports. Whether this is a principled filter is disputed. Norman Kemp Smith ("The Naturalism of Hume," *Mind*, 1905; *The Philosophy of David Hume*, 1941) read Hume as a naturalist for whom scepticism clears the ground for nature's beliefs. Richard Popkin (1951) and Robert Fogelin (*Hume's Skepticism in the Treatise of Human Nature*, 1985) give the sceptical side more weight. Garrett defends the Title Principle as Hume's considered standard. See [Pyrrho and the sceptics](../../eras/01-ancient/pyrrho-of-elis-and-the-skeptics.md) for the ancient positions Hume names.

**Argument 10. Of miracles (*Enquiry* 10)**

*What it proves:* that no testimony can make it reasonable to believe that a miracle has happened, and above all that no miracle can be proved "so as to be the foundation of a system of religion" (EHU 10.36). It is an argument about evidence. It does not claim that miracles are impossible.

Hume models the argument on one by John Tillotson, Archbishop of Canterbury, against the real presence in the Eucharist (EHU 10.1). He hopes his own "will, with the wise and learned, be an everlasting check to all kinds of superstitious delusion" (EHU 10.2). The first part rests on the account of probability in Section 6, applied to testimony.

1. "A wise man, therefore, proportions his belief to the evidence" (EHU 10.4). Where experience is uniform it amounts to a **proof**; where it is mixed it gives only **probability**, measured by the balance of cases.
2. Our trust in testimony rests on experience of the usual agreement between reports and facts, and nothing else (EHU 10.5).
3. "A miracle is a violation of the laws of nature" (EHU 10.12). A footnote defines it more exactly as "a transgression of a law of nature by a particular volition of the Deity, or by the interposition of some invisible agent" (EHU 10.12n23.1).
4. A law of nature is established by uniform experience, so "there is here a direct and full proof, from the nature of the fact, against the existence of any miracle" (EHU 10.12).
5. So a report of a miracle sets a proof from testimony, at best, against a full proof from experience. The wise man weighs the two: "I weigh the one miracle against the other; and according to the superiority, which I discover, I pronounce my decision, and always reject the greater miracle" (EHU 10.13).
Therefore "no testimony is sufficient to establish a miracle, unless the testimony be of such a kind, that its falsehood would be more miraculous, than the fact, which it endeavours to establish" (EHU 10.13).

Part 2 argues that the condition is never met in fact. No miracle has had enough witnesses of good sense and integrity, with much to lose, in a public place (EHU 10.15). The "passion of surprize and wonder" inclines us to believe marvels (EHU 10.16). Miracle reports "abound among ignorant and barbarous nations" (EHU 10.20). Rival religions' miracles cancel one another (EHU 10.24). Hume then marks the limits. Uniform worldwide testimony to eight days of darkness from 1 January 1600 should be believed (EHU 10.36). The same testimony to Queen Elizabeth's return from the grave should not, and a miracle "ascribed to any new system of religion" carries "a full proof of a cheat" (EHU 10.37–38). The section ends with faith as "a continued miracle in his own person" (EHU 10.41).

**Textual basis.** EHU 10.1–41, with the note at 10.12n23 and the Indian prince who would not believe in ice (EHU 10.10 and n22). EHU 6 supplies the account of probability.

**Objection.** Three critics made the classic objections. George Campbell (*A Dissertation on Miracles*, 1762) argued that the argument is circular: our knowledge of the laws of nature rests largely on testimony, and testimony has a natural credibility that does not wait for experience of its reliability, against premise 2. Richard Price (*Four Dissertations*, 1767) argued that testimony routinely overcomes great improbability. A report of the winning number in a lottery is believed though the prior chance of that number was tiny. In the twentieth century John Earman (*Hume's Abject Failure*, 2000) put the point in probabilistic terms. Several independent witnesses, each only moderately reliable, can together make an improbable event probable, so the maxim, read as a bar to all such belief, is false. Earman also argues that premise 4 begs the question. If "uniform experience" is to count against the miracle, it must already exclude the reported case.

**Reply.** The literature's, with Hume's text behind it. Robert Fogelin (*A Defense of Hume on Miracles*, 2003) and Peter Millican argue that the maxim is a condition stated in terms of comparative probability, which a modern Bayesian can accept: belief is warranted only if the falsity of the testimony would be less probable than the event. Hume never says the condition cannot be met. The darkness case of EHU 10.36 shows that he thinks it can. The work is done by Part 2's empirical claims about how miracle reports arise, and those claims do not beg the question. Price's lottery case fails as a counterexample, since a lottery draw breaks no law and its outcome is expected to be some number or other. Hume's own reply to the circularity charge is the Indian prince: testimony to frost "bore so little analogy" to his experience that he "reasoned justly" in demanding strong evidence (EHU 10.10). The weighing uses our whole experience, including our experience of testimony, so it is not circular. How Earman's multiple-witness point applies to the historical cases Hume discusses remains disputed.

**Argument 11. The design argument and Philo's objections (*Dialogues* 2–8; *Enquiry* 11)**

*What it proves:* in Cleanthes' hands, that God exists and resembles a human mind. In Philo's hands, the objections prove that the argument establishes far less than it claims, at most a cause of order with some remote analogy to intelligence, and nothing of its goodness, unity or infinity.

The *Dialogues* were published in 1779, after Hume's death. Pamphilus narrates a conversation among three speakers. **Cleanthes** argues for God from experience; **Demea** holds an orthodox faith that rests on a priori proof and on God's incomprehensibility; **Philo** is a sceptic. Cleanthes gives the argument in Part 2.

1. "Look round the world": it is "nothing but one great machine, subdivided into an infinite number of lesser machines" (DNR 2.5).
2. Its adaptation of means to ends "resembles exactly, though it much exceeds, the productions of human contrivance" (DNR 2.5).
3. Like effects have like causes.
4. Human contrivances are caused by design and intelligence.
Therefore "the Author of Nature is somewhat similar to the mind of man; though possessed of much larger faculties, proportioned to the grandeur of the work, which he has executed" (DNR 2.5).

Philo's objections run through Parts 2–8. (i) The analogy is weak. A house shows a builder because houses are "precisely that species of effect, which we have experienced to proceed from that species of cause" (DNR 2.8). (ii) Thought is one principle of order among many, and we cannot move "from parts to the whole" (DNR 2.18). (iii) The universe is unique, and we have no experience of the origin of worlds (DNR 2.24). (iv) If order needs a cause, so does the order in the divine mind, so "It were better, therefore, never to look beyond the present material world" (DNR 4.9). (v) Taken strictly, the analogy gives a finite, imperfect, perhaps plural cause: the world might be "the first rude essay of some infant deity, who afterwards abandoned it" (DNR 5.12). (vi) Other analogies fit better: "The world plainly resembles more an animal or a vegetable, than it does a watch or a knitting-loom" (DNR 7.3). (vii) A revised Epicurean hypothesis, finite matter in infinite time, could explain apparent order without design, since an unadjusted animal could not survive (DNR 8.2, 8.9).

The *Enquiry* makes the core point through a friend who imagines himself Epicurus addressing the Athenians. "When we infer any particular cause from an effect, we must proportion the one to the other" (EHU 11.12). From the world we may infer only what is "exactly sufficient to produce the effect," and nothing beyond it about providence or a future state. Hume's narrator then adds a doubt of his own, that a cause can be known at all from a single effect with no parallel (EHU 11.30).

**Textual basis.** DNR 2.5–24; 3.1–10; 4.9; 5.1–12; 6–8; EHU 11.12–30. The final position is in DNR 12.2 and 12.33.

**Objection.** Cleanthes gives the strongest reply himself, in Part 3. Philo's objections, he says, would apply to cases where design is beyond doubt. If "an articulate voice were heard in the clouds," speaking wisely to every nation in its own language, no one would hesitate to ascribe it to design, though the case is as unique as the world (DNR 3.2). Anatomize the eye and "the idea of a contriver" comes upon you "with a force like that of sensation" (DNR 3.7). If the inference breaks the rules of logic, its force "proves clearly, that there may be arguments of a like irregular nature" (DNR 3.8). At this point, the narrator reports, "Philo was a little embarrassed and confounded" (DNR 3.10). Thomas Reid made a related case (*Essays on the Intellectual Powers of Man*, 1785, Essay VI). We infer design from its marks by a first principle, as we infer other people's minds from their words and actions, without any experience of the minds themselves. Philo's demand for experience of the origin of worlds would forbid that inference too.

**Reply.** Philo's own, given late. In Part 12 he grants that "A purpose, an intention, a design strikes every where the most careless, the most stupid thinker" (DNR 12.2). He grants the irregular argument its force, and then measures what it delivers. Natural theology reduces to the proposition "That the cause or causes of order in the universe probably bear some remote analogy to human intelligence," a proposition that "affords no inference that affects human life" (DNR 12.33). This keeps every one of the earlier objections. They were never meant to show that the world has no orderly cause. They were meant to show that the analogy cannot be extended to God's goodness, unity, infinity or care for us, which is what religion needs. Who speaks for Hume is disputed. Kemp Smith's edition of the *Dialogues* (1935) argued for Philo. Hume told Gilbert Elliot in 1751 that he had made Cleanthes the hero, and some readers take that at face value. William Paley's *Natural Theology* (1802) restated the design argument as if Philo had not written. Darwin's *On the Origin of Species* (1859) later gave the kind of natural explanation of adaptation that Philo's Part 8 only sketched. See [Epicurus](../../eras/01-ancient/epicurus.md) and [Cicero and Lucretius](../../eras/01-ancient/cicero-and-lucretius.md), whose *On the Nature of the Gods* is the dialogue's model.

**Argument 12. The problem of evil (*Dialogues* 10–11)**

*What it proves:* two things of different strength. First, that if God's goodness is to be inferred from the world, as Cleanthes' method requires, the world's misery blocks the inference. Second, more tentatively, that the most probable hypothesis about the first causes is that they are neither good nor malicious.

Part 10 opens with Demea and Philo agreeing that human life is miserable. Demea wants misery to show our need of faith; Philo turns it on Cleanthes. He restates "Epicurus's old questions": "Is he willing to prevent evil, but not able? then is he impotent. Is he able, but not willing? then is he malevolent. Is he both able and willing? whence then is evil?" (DNR 10.25). The logical form is set aside a few pages later. Philo grants that "pain or misery in man is compatible with infinite power and goodness in the Deity" (DNR 10.35). The argument that remains is about inference.

1. On Cleanthes' method, God's attributes must be inferred from the world as we find it, and from nothing else (DNR 10.35).
2. The world contains great misery, mixed with good.
3. A world of mixed good and ill is not what one would expect beforehand from a very powerful, wise and benevolent being (DNR 11.4).
4. A mere possible compatibility of evil with goodness does not license an inference to goodness: "The consistence is not absolutely denied, only the inference" (DNR 11.4).
Therefore the world gives no ground for inferring God's infinite goodness, or even a goodness like the human.

Part 11 supports premise 3 with four circumstances on which most natural evil depends: pain as well as pleasure moves animals (DNR 11.6); the world runs by general laws (DNR 11.7–8); powers are given out with "great frugality" (DNR 11.9); and nature's springs are inaccurately adjusted (DNR 11.11). None appears necessary, though we are too ignorant to be sure (DNR 11.12). He then states the second result. Of four hypotheses about the first causes, perfect goodness, perfect malice, both, or neither, "Mixt phenomena can never prove the two former unmixt principles," the uniformity of laws tells against the third, and "The fourth, therefore, seems by far the most probable" (DNR 11.15). Nature appears as "a blind Nature, impregnated by a great vivifying principle" (DNR 11.13).

**Textual basis.** DNR 10.1–36; 11.1–21. Demea's answer from a future state is at DNR 10.29; Philo's claim of victory is at DNR 10.36.

**Objection.** The traditional answer is theodicy. Demea gives the oldest form: "This world is but a point in comparison of the universe: this life but a moment in comparison of eternity" (DNR 10.29), so present evils are put right elsewhere. Leibniz's *Theodicy* (1710) argued that this is the best possible world and that its evils are the cost of the best whole. In the twentieth century Alvin Plantinga's free-will defence (*God, Freedom, and Evil*, 1974) answered J. L. Mackie's charge ("Evil and Omnipotence," 1955) that the theist's beliefs are inconsistent. A world with free creatures may be better than one without, and God cannot make free creatures who always choose rightly. On this view the problem of evil is solved, or at least shown to be no proof against God. A later reply, sceptical theism (Stephen Wykstra, 1984), adds that we should not expect to see God's reasons, so the failure to see them is no evidence that there are none. See [Leibniz](../../eras/03-early-modern/gottfried-wilhelm-leibniz.md).

**Reply.** Philo's own, and it anticipates most of this. Plantinga's defence answers the logical problem, which Philo concedes at DNR 10.35. It does not answer the evidential problem, which is Philo's real argument. Philo also allows that if God's goodness "could be established on any tolerable reasons a priori," evils "might easily, in some unknown manner, be reconcilable to it" (DNR 11.12). The difficulty is for a theist who, like Cleanthes, has given up a priori proof and must read goodness off the world. Demea's future state and Leibniz's best world are possibilities, and "A mere possible compatibility is not sufficient" (DNR 10.35). Sceptical theism is close to the position Philo urges throughout, that "these subjects exceed all human capacity" (DNR 10.34), and it costs the theist the design argument. If we cannot judge God's purposes from the world, we cannot infer them from it either. The four circumstances involve no free choice, so the free-will defence leaves them untouched. William Rowe's evidential argument ("The Problem of Evil and Some Varieties of Atheism," 1979) is the modern descendant of Part 11.

**Argument 13. Reason is, and ought only to be, the slave of the passions (*Treatise* 2.3.3)**

*What it proves:* that reason alone can never move us to act, and can never oppose a passion in directing the will. The so-called combat of reason and passion is really a contest between passions, often between calm ones that we mistake for reason and violent ones.

Hume names his target: the view, on which "the greatest part of moral philosophy, antient and modern, seems to be founded," that we ought to regulate our actions by reason and subdue any passion that resists it (T 2.3.3.1). The argument uses the division of the understanding from Argument 2.

1. Reason works in two ways: demonstration, concerning relations of ideas, and probable reasoning, concerning cause and effect (T 2.3.3.2).
2. Demonstration alone never causes action. Its "proper province is the world of ideas," while the will places us "in that of realities"; mathematics guides action only by informing our judgments of cause and effect (T 2.3.3.2).
3. Causal reasoning alone never causes action either. The prospect of pain or pleasure gives rise to aversion or propensity, and reasoning only shows us the causes and effects of what we already care about: "the impulse arises not from reason, but is only directed by it" (T 2.3.3.3).
4. So reason alone cannot produce volition.
5. Only a contrary impulse can oppose an impulse, and if reason produced such an impulse it could produce volition, contrary to step 4 (T 2.3.3.4).
Therefore reason cannot oppose passion, and "Reason is, and ought only to be the slave of the passions, and can never pretend to any other office than to serve and obey them" (T 2.3.3.4).

A second argument supports the first. A passion "contains not any representative quality, which renders it a copy of any other existence" (T 2.3.3.5), so it cannot disagree with what it represents and cannot be contrary to reason. It is unreasonable only loosely, when it rests on a false belief or chooses insufficient means (T 2.3.3.6). Hence: "'Tis not contrary to reason to prefer the destruction of the whole world to the scratching of my finger" (T 2.3.3.6). What we call reason in conduct is usually a **calm passion**, such as "the general appetite to good, and aversion to evil" (T 2.3.3.8), and strength of mind is the prevalence of calm passions over violent ones (T 2.3.3.10).

**Textual basis.** T 2.3.3.1–10; the doctrine is reused at T 3.1.1.6 and restated in EPM Appendix 1.

**Objection.** Moral rationalists rejected premise 3. Richard Price (*A Review of the Principal Questions in Morals*, 1758) held that the understanding perceives right and wrong directly, and that this perception is itself a motive. Kant made the strongest version of the point (*Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals*, 1785). Hume allows only **hypothetical** imperatives, which tell us what to do if we want some end. Kant argued that morality needs a **categorical** imperative, which binds a rational agent whatever she wants, so pure reason must be practical. Christine Korsgaard ("Skepticism about Practical Reason," 1986) added an argument from within Hume's own view. Even instrumental reasoning, taking the means to one's end, is a rational requirement that an agent can fail to meet. Hume cannot both recognise it and deny reason all authority over action. See [Immanuel Kant](../../eras/03-early-modern/immanuel-kant.md) and the [Kant dossier](../immanuel-kant/README.md).

**Reply.** Partly Hume's own, partly the literature's. Hume's thesis concerns what can produce motivation. Reason still redirects passion whenever it corrects a false belief or a choice of means, and the passion then yields at once (T 2.3.3.7). Hume can grant Korsgaard that failing to take known means is a defect while denying that seeing the defect moves anyone who lacks a desire for the end. Michael Smith ("The Humean Theory of Motivation," *Mind*, 1987) restated the argument in terms of **direction of fit**, an idea he takes from Elizabeth Anscombe's *Intention* (1957). Beliefs aim to fit the world; desires aim to make the world fit them; motivation needs a state of the second kind. The disagreement with Kant remains open. It turns on whether there are reasons for action that hold independently of an agent's desires, the question now called internalism and externalism about reasons (Bernard Williams, "Internal and External Reasons," 1979, reprinted in *Moral Luck*, 1981). See [Bernard Williams](../../eras/05-20th-century/bernard-williams.md).

**Argument 14. Moral distinctions not derived from reason, and is/ought (*Treatise* 3.1.1)**

*What it proves:* that we do not tell virtue from vice by reason, either by demonstration or by inference about matters of fact. Moral distinctions are felt, so they rest on a moral sense (Argument 15 and T 3.1.2). The section closes with the observation about *is* and *ought*.

Hume's targets are moral rationalists such as Samuel Clarke and William Wollaston, for whom right and wrong are eternal relations known by reason. Section 3.1.1 gives three arguments.

**(a) The argument from motivation.**

1. Moral judgments influence action: "Morals excite passions, and produce or prevent actions" (T 3.1.1.6).
2. "Reason of itself is utterly impotent in this particular" (T 3.1.1.6), by Argument 13.
Therefore "The rules of morality, therefore, are not conclusions of our reason" (T 3.1.1.6).

**(b) The argument from representation.**

1. "Reason is the discovery of truth or falshood" (T 3.1.1.9).
2. Passions, volitions and actions are "original facts and realities, compleat in themselves," and cannot agree or disagree with anything (T 3.1.1.9).
Therefore actions cannot be contrary or conformable to reason, and their praiseworthiness or blame cannot consist in that.

**(c) The arguments from cases.** If vice were a relation, it would be present wherever the relation held. A sapling that overtops and destroys its parent oak stands in the relations of a parricide, yet no one finds guilt in the tree (T 3.1.1.24); animal incest has no moral turpitude (T 3.1.1.25). Nor is vice a matter of fact. In wilful murder you find "only certain passions, motives, volitions and thoughts," and "The vice entirely escapes you, as long as you consider the object. You never can find it, till you turn your reflection into your own breast, and find a sentiment of disapprobation" (T 3.1.1.26). Vice and virtue are like colours and heat, which "modern philosophy" places in the mind (T 3.1.1.26).

The section ends with an observation Hume calls "of some importance." In every system of morality he has met, the author moves from propositions joined by *is* to propositions joined by *ought*. The change "is imperceptible; but is, however, of the last consequence," and "a reason should be given, for what seems altogether inconceivable, how this new relation can be a deduction from others, which are entirely different from it" (T 3.1.1.27).

**Textual basis.** T 3.1.1.1–27; T 3.1.2.1–4 for the positive view; EPM Appendix 1 for the later restatement.

**Objection.** Two objections dominate. (i) Against argument (a), moral **externalists** such as David Brink (*Moral Realism and the Foundations of Ethics*, 1989) deny premise 1 in its strong form. Moral judgments usually motivate, but an amoralist can judge an act wrong and not care, so the motivating force may come from a separate desire to do right. If so, moral judgments could be beliefs delivered by reason after all. (ii) Against the *is/ought* passage, John Searle ("How to Derive 'Ought' from 'Is'," *Philosophical Review*, 1964) offered a counterexample. Jones uttered certain words; in doing so he promised to pay Smith five dollars; so he placed himself under an obligation; so he ought to pay. Each step seems to follow from institutional facts. Alasdair MacIntyre ("Hume on 'Is' and 'Ought'," 1959) argued that Hume never meant a ban, since his own ethics moves from facts about sentiment to moral conclusions.

**Reply.** To (i), the literature's. Hume needs only that moral judgments characteristically engage the passions, which externalists grant, and his moral sense theory explains that link without a separate desire. To (ii), R. M. Hare ("The Promising Game," 1964) replied that Searle's steps require a premise accepting the rules of promising, and that premise is evaluative, so no *ought* comes from pure *is*. On MacIntyre's point, the [library entry](../../eras/03-early-modern/david-hume.md) is right that the passage is a demand for an explanation and that its scope is debated. Hume's own words support a modest reading. The passage does not say no *ought* can follow; it says the step "shou'd be observ'd and explain'd," and that attending to it shows that vice and virtue are "not founded merely on the relations of objects, nor is perceiv'd by reason" (T 3.1.1.27). It is a corollary of arguments (a)–(c). G. E. Moore's "naturalistic fallacy" (*Principia Ethica*, 1903) is a different claim about the definition of "good" and should not be read back into Hume. See [G. E. Moore](../../eras/05-20th-century/g-e-moore.md) and [the moral sense school](../../eras/03-early-modern/moral-sense-school.md).

**Argument 15. Justice as an artificial virtue, and the circle in the motive to justice (*Treatise* 3.2.1–2; *Enquiry concerning the Principles of Morals* 3)**

*What it proves:* that justice, the virtue of respecting property and keeping promises, is **artificial**. It depends on human conventions that arise from our circumstances, and no natural motive would lead us to it before those conventions existed. Justice is approved because of its usefulness to society.

The argument starts from a principle about virtue. Actions are approved as signs of motives, so "the first virtuous motive, which bestows a merit on any action, can never be a regard to the virtue of that action, but must be some other natural motive or principle" (T 3.2.1.4). To suppose otherwise "is to reason in a circle." Hume then asks why I should repay a loan.

1. An action is virtuous only if it springs from a motive that is virtuous independently of a regard to the action's virtue (T 3.2.1.4).
2. Before any convention, there is no natural motive that would reliably lead me to repay a loan or leave others' goods alone. Self-interest "is the source of all injustice and violence" (T 3.2.1.10). Concern for the public is too remote and too weak (T 3.2.1.11). Private benevolence toward the owner fails when he is a miser or my enemy (T 3.2.1.13).
3. So the only motive to just acts would be a regard to their justice, which by step 1 cannot be the first motive. Hume calls this "an evident sophistry and reasoning in a circle" (T 3.2.1.17).
Therefore "the sense of justice and injustice is not deriv'd from nature, but arises artificially, tho' necessarily from education, and human conventions" (T 3.2.1.17).

Section 3.2.2 explains the convention. Selfishness and limited generosity meet external goods that are easily transferred and scarce "in comparison of the wants and desires of men" (T 3.2.2.16). Each sees it is in his interest to leave others their goods if they do the same. This "general sense of common interest," mutually expressed, is a convention without a promise: "Two men, who pull the oars of a boat, do it by an agreement or convention, tho' they have never given promises to each other" (T 3.2.2.10). Single just acts may harm the public, but "the whole plan or scheme" is necessary to society (T 3.2.2.22). The circle is broken by two distinct obligations: "self-interest is the original motive to the establishment of justice: but a sympathy with public interest is the source of the moral approbation, which attends that virtue" (T 3.2.2.24). The second *Enquiry* argues the same result more briefly. Imagine abundance, or perfect benevolence, and justice would be "totally USELESS" (EPM 3.3, 3.6). Hence "public utility is the sole origin of justice" (EPM 3.1). Justice is like a vault, "where each individual stone would, of itself, fall to the ground" (EPM App. 3.5).

**Textual basis.** T 3.2.1.1–19; T 3.2.2.1–28; EPM 3.1–48; EPM Appendix 3.

**Objection.** Thomas Reid (*Essays on the Active Powers of Man*, 1788, Essay V, chapter 5) denied that justice is artificial. Even a child has natural notions of favour and injury, and resents an injury before any convention exists. Reid also rejected premise 1. A regard to duty, to what is right as such, is itself a natural principle of action in us, so it can be the first motive to a just act and no circle arises. A second objection concerns the genesis. A convention that each keeps only so long as others keep it seems to need trust before it can start, and Hume gives no account of how that trust arises among the selfish people he describes. See [Thomas Reid](../../eras/03-early-modern/thomas-reid.md).

**Reply.** To Reid, Hume's own in part. Premise 1 is a general claim about what makes motives virtuous, and the natural virtues satisfy it, because they have natural motives, such as a parent's affection or benevolence, that are virtuous apart from any regard to virtue. Hume grants that a regard to duty can move us once a sense of virtue exists, as when a man lacking an affection acts from duty to supply it (T 3.2.1.8). He denies only that it can be the first motive, the one that makes the act virtuous in the first place. Justice alone, he argues, has no natural motive to play that role. Hume also grants Reid more than Reid allows. "Mankind is an inventive species," and justice may be called natural in the sense of being "inseparable from the species"; "Tho' the rules of justice be artificial, they are not arbitrary" (T 3.2.1.19). To the genesis objection, the literature's. Hume says the convention "arises gradually, and acquires force by a slow progression" (T 3.2.2.10), and David Lewis (*Convention*, 1969) gave a game-theoretic account of how such conventions can form and become stable without promises. Whether Hume's account explains the moral obligation of justice, and not only its usefulness, is a further dispute. Annette Baier (*A Progress of Sentiments*, 1991) gives a sympathetic reconstruction of Hume's account. Others, following Reid, think it explains why we approve of justice in others and leaves unexplained why each of us should be just. That question is Argument 16. See [John Rawls](../../eras/05-20th-century/john-rawls.md), whose "circumstances of justice" in *A Theory of Justice* (1971) take Hume's account as their source, and [Thomas Hobbes](../../eras/03-early-modern/thomas-hobbes.md), from whom Hume's account of the origin of property differs on the role of promising and the sovereign.

**Argument 16. The sensible knave (*Enquiry concerning the Principles of Morals* 9.22–25)**

*What it proves:* less than it seems to promise. Section 9 has argued that virtue is in our interest. Hume admits one exception. Justice can seem a losing policy to a clever person who breaks its rules only when it pays and is never caught. Hume's reply is that such a person loses goods he cannot see, and that no argument will answer him if he does not feel their loss.

"Treating vice with the greatest candour," Hume finds no pretext for preferring it to virtue for self-interest, "except, perhaps, in the case of justice" (EPM 9.22). The knave's reasoning is this.

1. Without a general regard to property, no society could subsist (EPM 9.22).
2. But a single act of injustice, in some cases, adds much to a person's fortune "without causing any considerable breach in the social union and confederacy" (EPM 9.22).
3. So "That honesty is the best policy, may be a good general rule; but is liable to many exceptions" (EPM 9.22).
Therefore the wisest person "observes the general rule, and takes advantage of all the exceptions" (EPM 9.22).

The knave is Hume's version of Glaucon's challenge in Plato's *Republic* II, the story of Gyges and his ring of invisibility. Hume's answer has three parts. He first concedes: if a man thinks this reasoning "much requires an answer, it will be a little difficult to find any, which will to him appear satisfactory and convincing" (EPM 9.23). Second, an honest person values "Inward peace of mind, consciousness of integrity, a satisfactory review of our own conduct" (EPM 9.23), and these are lost by the knave. Third, knaves are usually caught. They plan to cheat "with moderation and secrecy," but "a tempting incident occurs, nature is frail, and they give into the snare" (EPM 9.24). Even the successful ones are "the greatest dupes," trading "the invaluable enjoyment of a character, with themselves at least, for the acquisition of worthless toys and gewgaws" (EPM 9.25).

**Textual basis.** EPM 9.21–25. The Treatise's account of the "natural obligation" of justice as interest, and of the remoteness of that interest in large societies, is at T 3.2.2.22–24.

**Objection.** The standard objection is that Hume's answer works only for people who do not need it. The knave by hypothesis lacks the "antipathy to treachery and roguery" of "ingenuous natures" (EPM 9.23), and the claim that knaves are usually caught is a generalisation the cleverest knave can beat. David Gauthier ("Artificial Virtues and the Sensible Knave," *Hume Studies*, 1992) pressed a sharper version. Hume's own account of justice in Argument 15 grounds it in interest, so if the knave's interest is served by injustice, Hume's theory cannot call him irrational. Plato had tried to show that justice is good for its possessor whatever the consequences, and Hume seems to give up that project. See [Plato](../../eras/01-ancient/plato.md) and the [Plato dossier](../plato/README.md).

**Reply.** Hume's own reply is to concede the point and change the question. On Argument 13, no argument can supply a motive to someone who lacks the relevant passion, so the failure to convince the knave is what Hume's theory predicts. The question Section 9 answers is whether a person of ordinary sentiments, reflecting on his life, has reason to regret being honest. Hume argues that he does not. The knave's error, on this reading, is a defect of character judged from the general point of view, and not a mistake in reasoning. Gauthier offered a different reply, developed in *Morals by Agreement* (1986): a rational agent does better over time by becoming a "constrained maximizer" who keeps agreements, because others can detect and exclude those who will not. That reply keeps Hume's grounding of justice in interest and strengthens his third point, that knaves betray themselves. Whether Hume's concession is a weakness or a mark of honesty about the limits of moral argument still divides his readers.
