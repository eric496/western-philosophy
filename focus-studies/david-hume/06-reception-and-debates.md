# Hume Received — Failure, Fame, Eclipse, Return, and the Live Debates
**English** | [简体中文](../../zh/focus-studies/david-hume/06-reception-and-debates.md)

*Focus study: David Hume · [Dossier home](README.md) · [Library entry](../../eras/03-early-modern/david-hume.md)*

Hume is the rare philosopher whose reception reverses itself completely, twice. The book now widely called the greatest work of philosophy in English "fell dead-born from the press," by its author's own account; the author became famous anyway, as an essayist and historian, while his philosophy was answered by a school founded to refute it. The nineteenth century read him mainly as a historian and, when it read the philosophy, as a warning. The twentieth century rebuilt him twice — as the positivists' patron and as Kemp Smith's naturalist — and the rebuilt Hume is now contested on every front at once: skeptic or naturalist, regularity theorist or causal realist, the gap's discoverer or its first crosser, Philo's author or Cleanthes'. This file tells the story by era, states the live debates at their current state of play — including the debate over the footnote on race in "Of National Characters," where the dossier's rule is the plainest statement of the record and the fairest statement of the dispute — and closes with the standard misconceptions in full form.

## Immediate reception: the dead-born book and the famous infidel (1739–1776)

The *Treatise* (1739–40) sold poorly and was reviewed dismissively where it was reviewed at all; Hume's retrospective verdict in *My Own Life* — "it fell dead-born from the press" — is self-pitying and substantially accurate. What his lifetime made of him was something else. The *Essays* (1741–42 onward) were polite successes; the first *Enquiry* (1748) and the *Enquiry Concerning the Principles of Morals* (1751) circulated as the work of a leading man of letters; and the *History of England* (1754–62) made him, by his own account, "not only independent, but opulent" — the most widely read Hume of the eighteenth century is the historian.

Alongside the fame ran the reputation that governed his career: "the infidel," in pious mouths the atheist of the age. It cost him the Edinburgh chair in 1745 — opponents circulated a pamphlet of extracts from the *Treatise* to exhibit his principles on religion and morality — and a Glasgow chair in 1752, and it shaped his own conduct as an author: the essays on suicide and the immortality of the soul were printed and suppressed, and the *Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion*, substantially complete by the 1750s and revised to the end of his life, were withheld for posthumous publication (1779). The deathbed completed the contemporary picture: Adam Smith's published letter on Hume's cheerful, jesting, unconverted death was read by the devout as a scandal and by Hume's friends as the life consistent with the work. Both readings agreed on the fact.

## The Scottish reply: common sense organizes (1762–1770s)

The first systematic answers came from Hume's own country and shared his empiricist starting point, which is why they still matter. George Campbell's *Dissertation on Miracles* (1762) replied directly to the miracles essay, arguing that Hume had misstated the force of testimony — the opening move of a debate about testimony that has never closed. Thomas Reid's *Inquiry into the Human Mind* (1764) diagnosed the whole way of ideas as the error: if ideas are the only immediate objects of thought, Hume's bundle and Berkeley's immaterialism are not abuses of the system but its consequences, and the remedy is to reject the premise for principles of common sense built into our constitution. Reid sent the draft to Hume and received courtesy in return; the school he founded with James Oswald (*An Appeal to Common Sense in Behalf of Religion*, 1766–72) and the Aberdeen circle made "common sense" the standing Scottish answer for two generations. James Beattie's *Essay on Truth* (1770) was the reply's popular form — a bestseller that mingled argument with personal abuse of Hume and won its author a royal pension while Hume, who never answered in print, dismissed him in private. The reply's mixed quality is part of its history: Campbell argues, Reid founds, Beattie performs — and it was Beattie the century mostly read.

## Kant and the German reception (1755–1800)

Germany received Hume in pieces. The first *Enquiry* was translated in 1755 and read widely; the *Treatise* had no complete German translation until 1790–92, after the critical philosophy it provoked already existed. The German Hume was therefore chiefly the Hume of the *Enquiry* — of induction and miracles — filtered through a debate (Tetens, the Berlin Academy, Beattie's German admirers) that Kant inherited along with the texts. Kant's response, traced in [05-conversations.md](05-conversations.md), is the reception's central event: the *Critique of Pure Reason* takes Hume's problem about causation as the type of all metaphysical problems and answers it by relocating necessity into the conditions of experience. The Kantians' Hume thereafter became a stage — the skeptic whose question was right and whose resources were insufficient — and that staged figure, as much as the texts, is what the nineteenth century inherited.

## The nineteenth century: the historian's shadow and Green's verdict (1800–1900)

In Britain the utilitarians kept one Hume alive: the moral and political philosopher of utility, sympathy, and convention, transmitted through Bentham to the Mills. But in general reputation the *History* eclipsed the philosophy; "Hume" to most educated readers meant the historian of England, and the *Treatise* survived as a connoisseur's enthusiasm. The academic verdict, when it came, was delivered by idealism. T. H. Green's long introduction to the Green and Grose edition of Hume's philosophical works (1874–75) — for decades the standard edition, and Green's essay the standard account — read Hume as the completion and thereby the exposure of the Lockean line: press the theory of ideas honestly and you arrive at a world of disconnected impressions in which knowledge, self, and morals all dissolve. Green meant it as a demonstration that empiricism must be transcended; its unintended effect was to fix the "skeptical collapse" reading of Hume — Hume as the reductio of his tradition — as the default for two generations of students, even among those who had never opened Green.

## The twentieth century: two rebuildings (1905–2000)

The first rebuilding was Norman Kemp Smith's. His articles on "The Naturalism of Hume" (*Mind*, 1905) and his *Philosophy of David Hume* (1941) argued that the skeptical arguments are subordinate in Hume's design: the *Treatise*'s real doctrine is that belief is the work of nature — instinct, custom, feeling — with reason its occasional instrument, so that Hume is not a skeptic who happens to relax but a naturalist whose skepticism is one department of a positive science of man. Nearly all subsequent Hume scholarship positions itself relative to Kemp Smith's reversal.

The second rebuilding ran in the opposite direction. The logical positivists conscripted the skeptical auditor: Ayer's *Language, Truth and Logic* (1936) presents the fork — every meaningful proposition is either a relation of ideas or a matter of fact — as the foundation of the verification criterion, and the Vienna Circle claimed Hume as ancestry. In this service the label **"Hume's fork"** had its career: it is a later, textbook name for the *Enquiry*'s opening distinction, not a phrase of Hume's, and its career tracks the positivist appropriation that made it indispensable. The two rebuildings coexisted without reconciling — the naturalist who subordinates reason to belief, the auditor who dismisses metaphysics by criterion — and the profession institutionalized both: the Hume Society and its journal *Hume Studies* (from 1975), and the Clarendon edition of Hume's works, which has been replacing the old texts with critical editions ever since.

## Live debates

**(a) Skeptic or naturalist?** The organizing dispute of modern Hume studies. On the naturalist side, Kemp Smith's thesis (above): the *Treatise*'s subtitle announces a science of man, its skeptical passages are local and instrumental, and feeling and custom are not what remain when philosophy fails but the philosophy itself. On the skeptical side, readers from the Common Sense school to Robert Fogelin argue that the arguments of Book I are meant at full strength — that Hume's skepticism is radical and Pyrrhonian in structure, and the natural beliefs are not a rival doctrine but the phenomenon the skepticism explains: we believe *although* reason condemns the belief, and Hume says so. Barry Stroud's *Hume* (1977) occupies the influential middle: Hume's project is explanatory rather than justificatory — a "science" of why we think as we do — which leaves the skepticism neither endorsed as a creed nor revoked as a stage, and Stroud is candid that this makes Hume's own consistency an open question. The textual crux is *Treatise* 1.4.7, where the skeptical crisis and nature's cure are staged in consecutive paragraphs: which paragraph has the last word is, in the end, what the debate is about.

**(b) The New Hume: causal realism against projectivism.** Did Hume believe in real necessary connections — "secret powers" in objects — while holding that we can form no adequate idea of them? The "skeptical realist" reading (John Wright, *The Sceptical Realism of David Hume*, 1983; Edward Craig; Galen Strawson's *The Secret Connexion*, 1989) answers yes: Hume's denials target our *conception* of power, not its existence; his talk of unknown powers, and his assumption throughout the natural philosophy that nature has a hidden structure, presuppose the realism. The label "New Hume" stuck to the reading as it spread. The older reading answers no: Hume's two definitions of cause exhaust what causation can mean for us; the idea of necessity is the mind's felt determination, projected onto a world of constant conjunctions — the projectivist development of which is Simon Blackburn's quasi-realism, first built for morals and extended to causation. The evidence is genuinely two-sided — "secret powers" passages against the definitions and the copy principle — and the debate now structures editions and first courses on Hume, with intermediate positions (Hume agnostic about powers; Hume's *relative* ideas permitting a thin reference to them) multiplying between the poles.

**(c) Is–ought: barrier, challenge, or neither?** The passage (*Treatise* 3.1.1) is agreed to demand an explanation of the transition from *is* to *ought*; everything else is contested. The strong reading — "Hume's law" — takes him to legislate that no *ought* follows from any set of *is* premises, and it became a load-bearing doctrine of twentieth-century metaethics. The weak reading (which the library entry favors presenting as at least equally supported) takes the passage as a challenge issued mainly against rationalist systems: show the deduction, or admit sentiment is doing the work. A. C. MacIntyre's "Hume on 'Is' and 'Ought'" (1959) complicated both by arguing that Hume himself moves from facts about human nature to evaluations without embarrassment, within a functional conception of human life the passage does not renounce. John Searle's attempted derivation (1964) — from "Jones promised" to "Jones ought" — remains the most discussed alleged counterexample, and replies to it usually concede the form while disputing whether the premises were purely factual. The dossier's rule matches the entry's: the demand for an explanation is Hume's; whether it amounts to a theorem is the debate.

**(d) Who speaks in the *Dialogues*?** Hume engineered the ambiguity: three speakers, a narrator (Pamphilus) who awards the palm to Cleanthes at the close, and a century of readers unconvinced the award is sincere. Philo, the skeptic who dismantles the design argument in Parts II–VIII and the cosmological argument in Part IX, is the majority candidate for Hume's voice — with Part XII complicating everything, since Philo there concedes that the cause of order "probably bears some remote analogy to human intelligence" and professes an admiration for true religion that readers divide between irony, camouflage, and sincerity. Kemp Smith, in his influential edition of the *Dialogues* (1935), made canonical the spokesman reading: Philo speaks for Hume. His nuance attaches not to that assignment but to Philo's own Part XII concession — the thin assent to a cause remotely analogous to human intelligence — which Kemp Smith took to voice Hume's residual religion: a minimal assent to an intelligent cause, spoken by the spokesman himself rather than by a rival. Demea's a priori mysticism has had few claimants as the author's voice. The current state of play is a distribution thesis — Hume speaks through the whole machine, with Philo nearest on criticism and the closing concessions marking the furthest point his caution, or his conviction, allowed in print — held alongside the standing caution that any simple identification has to explain away half the text.

**(e) The footnote in "Of National Characters": the record and the debate.** The facts first. The essay "Of National Characters" — whose own argument is that national differences arise from *moral* causes (government, custom, occupation), not physical ones — was published in 1748 without the passage at issue. In the edition of 1753–54 Hume added a footnote: "I am apt to suspect the negroes, and in general all the other species of men (for there are four or five different kinds) to be naturally inferior to the whites," supporting the claim by an asserted absence of civilized nations, eminent individuals, arts, and sciences among non-white peoples. In the final edition of the *Essays and Treatises* prepared at the end of his life (published 1777), Hume revised the footnote, narrowing its claim to "the negroes" alone; he did not withdraw it. The claim was contested in his own century: Beattie attacked it in the *Essay on Truth* (1770) as both false and evidence of its author's prejudices, and abolitionist writers, James Ramsay among them (1784), answered it with contrary evidence.

The modern debate runs on three tracks, and the dossier reports them without joining. *Context and character:* some scholars treat the footnote as a period prejudice — common, though as Beattie shows not uncontested, in the eighteenth century — and detachable from the philosophy proper; others reply that Hume was elsewhere capable of rising above period prejudice, that the footnote asserts a considered "natural" hierarchy, and that it sits in open tension with its own essay's environmental argument, which Hume, who revised everything, left standing in revised form. *Systematic significance:* one literature asks whether the footnote is an excrescence or a window — whether Hume's accounts of sympathy's partiality, of the "species" of men, and of civilization's stages gave the prejudice philosophical shelter — with scholars divided on how much of the system it touches. *Institutional consequence:* the footnote is the stated ground of public acts, among them the University of Edinburgh's 2020 decision to rename the David Hume Tower; defenders of the honors reply that the philosophy's universal instruments — the copy principle's audit, the moral theory's general point of view — are themselves the resources with which the footnote is refuted. What is not in dispute is the record: added 1753–54, narrowed 1777, never retracted, and criticized from 1770 onward. Readers should know it before they are told what to think of it.

## Misconceptions, in full form

- **The *Treatise* as the masterpiece its author stood behind.** Hume disowned it. The advertisement prefixed to the posthumous *Essays and Treatises* (1777) asks that the pieces in that collection — the *Enquiries*, the essays, the *Dialogues* as they appeared — "may alone be regarded as containing his philosophical sentiments and principles," dismissing the *Treatise* as a juvenile work whose defects of manner he had spent his career correcting. He never reprinted it. The modern valuation, which ranks the *Treatise* first among his works and treats the *Enquiries* as popularizations, is a genuine critical judgment — but it is *our* reversal of his, and reading the *Treatise* as the system's intended definitive statement, rather than as the full-dress draft its author superseded, gets both the texts and the man wrong.
- **Hume the pure destroyer.** The demolition is famous; the building is larger. The *Treatise* announces and executes a positive program: a science of man with laws of association, a theory of belief, a natural history of the passions, a conventionalist account of justice and government, an aesthetic and an economics in the essays. Even the skepticism is instrumental — the audit that clears the ground on which the natural explanations stand. A Hume who only destroys cannot explain why the same book contains the materials from which Smith built moral theory, the utilitarians built politics, and Reid built a philosophy by negation.
- **"Hume denied that causation exists."** He denied that we perceive, or can form an adequate idea of, necessary connection *in the objects*; he affirmed constant conjunction, relied on causal inference in every paragraph including the skeptical ones, and wrote rules for judging causes and effects. The New Hume debate (above) disputes whether he also *believed* in real powers beyond our conception — but no party to it attributes to Hume the view that fires do not really heat or that causal talk is empty. What he removed was a metaphysical picture of the tie, not the practice or the regularity.
- **"Hume the atheist," stated as a finding.** The label was a polemical weapon in his lifetime and is unprovable as a description now. Hume's writings on religion were composed for deniability: the dangerous essays suppressed, the *Dialogues* posthumous and ventriloquized, the public position a mitigated skepticism about all theological systems. His friends' testimony — Boswell's journal of his July 1776 visit, recording a Hume serene about annihilation and amused at Boswell's distress; Smith's letter — establishes that he did not expect personal immortality and did not fear its absence. What he positively believed about a cause of the world's order is exactly what Philo's Part XII concession leaves undecided, and scholarship divides among atheist, agnostic, and attenuated-deist readings of a record designed not to settle it. The dossier's formulation is the entry's: the infidel reputation is a fact; the atheism is an inference.
- **The backgammon caricature.** The cozy summary — Hume refuted reason, shrugged, and went to dinner — misreads *Treatise* 1.4.7, the "Conclusion" of Book I. The text's movement is a crisis, not a shrug: intense reflection issues in "philosophical melancholy and delirium," in which reason, followed honestly, "entirely subverts itself" and leaves no belief better founded than another. Nature's cure is real and Hume names it without embarrassment — "I dine, I play a game of back-gammon, I converse, and am merry with my friends" — but the passage continues: the philosopher returns to his study, finds he *cannot* surrender the curiosity that produced the crisis, and settles not into complacency but into a managed tension, resolving to follow reason where it leads while knowing where it ends. The carelessness is a discipline adopted against the crisis, not evidence he slept through it. That is why the skeptic-versus-naturalist debate (a) is still open: Hume staged both halves himself.
