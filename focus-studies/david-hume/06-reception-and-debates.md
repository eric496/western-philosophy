# Hume Received: The Great Infidel, the Sceptic, the Naturalist, and the Fights Still Open

*Focus study: David Hume · [Dossier home](README.md) · [Library entry](../../eras/03-early-modern/david-hume.md) · [Study guide](../../study/hume-enquiry.md)*

In his lifetime Hume was famous as an essayist and historian and notorious as an enemy of religion. For a century after his death philosophers read him as the skeptic who carried empiricism to its dead end. In the twentieth century he became a naturalist, then an ancestor of logical positivism, and then the subject of one of the largest literatures in the history of philosophy. This file tells that story by era, states seven live debates with their named positions, and closes with the standard misconceptions and the myths about the life. Hume is quoted from *Hume Texts Online* (Millican and Merivale) in his original spelling, cited by Clarendon paragraph: *Treatise* (T), first *Enquiry* (EHU), *Dialogues* (DNR), *Natural History of Religion* (NHR), *My Own Life* (MOL), and essays by title and paragraph.

## The eighteenth century: essayist, historian, infidel

Hume set the legend of the *Treatise*'s failure himself: "It fell dead-born from the press, without reaching such distinction, as even to excite a murmur among the zealots" (MOL 6). The verdict is too strong. The book drew several reviews, including a long hostile one in the *History of the Works of the Learned* (1739), and Hume answered with the anonymous *Abstract* (1740). What is true is that the *Treatise* found no public, and the few readers who mattered read it as a threat.

The threat was religious. In 1745 Hume stood for the Edinburgh chair of ethics and pneumatical philosophy. Opponents circulated a list of the *Treatise*'s dangerous propositions, his friends printed a reply drawn from his letter as *A Letter from a Gentleman to His Friend in Edinburgh*, and he lost. In 1751–52 the Glasgow chair of logic also went to another candidate after clerical opposition. In 1755 and 1756 a faction in the General Assembly of the Church of Scotland tried to have him censured, and the moderate clergy blocked it. "Of Miracles" (1748) and the *Natural History of Religion* (1757) gave the critics their texts. His critics called him "the great infidel," and his biographers have kept the label.

The public that ignored the *Treatise* read everything else. The *Political Discourses* (1752) were "the only work of mine that was successful on the first publication" (MOL 10). The *History of England* (1754–62) made him rich and was the standard English history for about a century. Its volume on the early Stuarts angered the Whigs, and in later revisions, Hume says, he made his alterations "all of them invariably to the Tory side" (MOL 15). In Paris in 1763–66, as embassy secretary under Lord Hertford, he was "le bon David" of the salons. His friendship with [Rousseau](../../eras/03-early-modern/jean-jacques-rousseau.md), whom he brought to England in 1766, ended within months in a public quarrel. To most contemporaries Hume was a man of letters who had once written some dangerous philosophy.

## The first philosophical replies: Reid, Beattie, Price

[Thomas Reid](../../eras/03-early-modern/thomas-reid.md)'s *Inquiry into the Human Mind on the Principles of Common Sense* (1764) read Hume as the honest end-point of the "way of ideas" running from Descartes through [Locke](../../eras/03-early-modern/john-locke.md) and [Berkeley](../../eras/03-early-modern/george-berkeley.md). If the mind perceives only its own ideas, Reid argued, Hume's skepticism follows. The remedy was to reject the theory of ideas and take the principles of common sense as evidence in their own right. Hume saw part of the manuscript and replied civilly. James Beattie's *Essay on the Nature and Immutability of Truth* (1770) made the same case with more abuse and less argument, and sold far better. Hume did not reply in print. In an "Advertisement" for the 1777 edition of his philosophical works he asked to be judged by the *Enquiries* and essays, and disowned the *Treatise* as a juvenile work. The miracles essay drew its own literature: George Campbell's *Dissertation on Miracles* (1762) argued that testimony is an original source of belief, and Richard Price (1767) argued that improbable events are rightly believed on testimony every day.

## Kant and the German reception

[Kant](../../eras/03-early-modern/immanuel-kant.md) wrote in the preface to the *Prolegomena* (1783) that the remembrance of Hume had interrupted his "dogmatic slumber." The [Kant dossier](../immanuel-kant/README.md) discusses what he actually read: the first *Enquiry* was in German from 1755, the *Treatise* only from 1790–92. Kant took the problem of necessary connexion and answered it by making causality a condition of experience. In the German histories that followed, Hume became the skeptic whom the critical philosophy refuted. He also served the other side. J. G. Hamann used Hume's account of belief to argue for faith (see [Hamann and Herder](../../eras/03-early-modern/hamann-and-herder.md)), and F. H. Jacobi's *David Hume on Faith* (1787) did the same.

## The nineteenth century: historian first, then the sceptic of the textbooks

In Britain the *History* kept Hume's name alive while the philosophy sank. [Bentham](../../eras/04-19th-century/jeremy-bentham.md) wrote in the *Fragment on Government* (1776) that Book III of the *Treatise* had shown him that virtue rests on utility. [J. S. Mill](../../eras/04-19th-century/john-stuart-mill.md)'s *System of Logic* (1843) used a regularity account of causation, and Thomas Huxley's *Hume* (1879) read him as a scientific agnostic.

The reading that controlled the next half-century came from the [British idealists](../../eras/04-19th-century/british-idealists.md). T. H. Green and T. H. Grose edited Hume's *Philosophical Works* (1874–75), and Green's long introduction to the *Treatise* argued that Hume had drawn the final consequences of Locke's and Berkeley's premises. If knowledge is built from separate sensations, there can be no knowledge of relations, objects or a self. Hume's skepticism is then the *reductio ad absurdum* of empiricism, and the way out is idealism. Green wrote as a warning to Oxford students, and he made the Locke–Berkeley–Hume sequence the British teaching frame. L. A. Selby-Bigge's Oxford editions of the *Treatise* (1888) and *Enquiries* (1894) kept this Hume in print for a century; the "SBN" page numbers come from them.

## Kemp Smith: the naturalist

Norman Kemp Smith reversed the reading in "The Naturalism of Hume" (*Mind*, 1905) and *The Philosophy of David Hume* (1941). Hume's skeptical arguments, he argued, serve a positive thesis. Reason cannot justify our beliefs in causes, bodies and selves, so nature fixes them through feeling and instinct. The model was Francis Hutcheson's moral sense (see the [moral sense school](../../eras/03-early-modern/moral-sense-school.md)), extended from morals to belief in general, and Kemp Smith thought Hume had worked out Books II and III before Book I. His key text is the *Treatise*'s reply to total skepticism: "Nature, by an absolute and uncontroulable necessity has determin'd us to judge as well as to breathe and feel" (T 1.4.1.7). The claim that reason is the slave of the passions (T 2.3.3.4) becomes the principle of the whole philosophy. Almost all later Hume scholarship starts from Kemp Smith, whether to refine his reading or to limit it.

## The twentieth century: positivism and its problems

The Vienna Circle's manifesto of 1929 named Hume among its forerunners (see [the Vienna Circle](../../eras/05-20th-century/vienna-circle-logical-positivism.md)). A. J. Ayer's *Language, Truth and Logic* (1936) opened its attack on metaphysics with Hume's fork and the first *Enquiry*'s last sentence: "Commit it then to the flames: For it can contain nothing but sophistry and illusion" (EHU 12.34). Ayer's verification principle sharpened Hume's test, and his emotivism descended from Hume's sentimentalism. [Russell](../../eras/05-20th-century/bertrand-russell.md)'s *History of Western Philosophy* (1945) made Hume the dead end of eighteenth-century empiricism and argued that if induction has no justification, sanity has no intellectual advantage over insanity.

Induction became the core problem of philosophy of science. [Popper](../../eras/05-20th-century/karl-popper.md) accepted Hume's logical result and rejected his psychology: science proceeds by conjecture and refutation, not induction. Nelson Goodman's *Fact, Fiction, and Forecast* (1955) replaced the old problem with the "new riddle" of which predicates are projectible. [Quine](../../eras/05-20th-century/w-v-o-quine.md)'s "Epistemology Naturalized" (1969) judged Hume's predicament permanent and recommended an epistemology continuous with psychology, close to Kemp Smith's Hume. In ethics the is–ought passage became "Hume's Law," and Max Black's name for it, "Hume's guillotine" (1964), stuck. It was often run together with [G. E. Moore](../../eras/05-20th-century/g-e-moore.md)'s different "naturalistic fallacy" (1903).

## The revival after 1970

*Hume Studies* began in 1975, and the Hume Society has met annually since the 1970s. Barry Stroud's *Hume* (1977) read him as a naturalist offering an empirical science of the mind, and asked how that science survives his skepticism. Robert Fogelin's *Hume's Skepticism in the Treatise of Human Nature* (1985) argued that the skepticism is real and not dissolved by the naturalism. Annette Baier's *A Progress of Sentiments* (1991) read the *Treatise* whole, with reason corrected by passion and sympathy in Books II and III. Don Garrett's *Cognition and Commitment in Hume's Philosophy* (1997) gave close reconstructions of the main arguments. Peter Millican edited *Reading Hume on Human Understanding* (2002) and, with Amyas Merivale, built *Hume Texts Online*. The Clarendon *Treatise* of David Fate Norton and Mary Norton (2007) and Tom Beauchamp's Clarendon *Enquiries* (1998, 2000) gave the paragraph numbering this dossier uses. Duncan Forbes, Nicholas Phillipson and James Harris (*Hume: An Intellectual Biography*, 2015) put the philosopher back inside the essayist and historian.

## His politics, read by conservatives and liberals

Conservatives point to Hume's attack on the social contract in "Of the Original Contract," his preference for established authority over speculative reform, and the Tory revisions to the *History*. He shares with [Burke](../../eras/03-early-modern/edmund-burke.md) the view that institutions rest on habit and opinion: "It is therefore, on opinion only that government is founded" ("Of the First Principles of Government" 1). Donald Livingston's *Hume's Philosophy of Common Life* (1984) read him as the critic of rationalist politics. Liberals point elsewhere. F. A. Hayek's 1963 lecture on Hume's legal and political philosophy made him the ancestor of spontaneous order and the rule of law, and his essays on trade and money fed into [Adam Smith](../../eras/03-early-modern/adam-smith.md). Forbes's *Hume's Philosophical Politics* (1975) called him a "sceptical Whig," loyal to the Revolution settlement but hostile to Whig myths of an ancient constitution. Thomas Jefferson, for his part, thought the *History* a Tory book unfit for republican students. [Rawls](../../eras/05-20th-century/john-rawls.md) took his "circumstances of justice" from Hume's account of justice as an artificial virtue (T 3.2.2).

## Race: the footnote to "Of National Characters"

"Of National Characters" (1748) argues that national differences come mainly from moral causes, such as government and custom, rather than from climate. In the 1753–54 edition Hume added a footnote that contradicts the essay's own method for one case. Its first version claimed that black people and all the other "species of men" were naturally inferior to whites. The final text, revised in 1776 and printed in 1777, narrows the claim: "I am apt to suspect the negroes to be naturally inferior to the whites" ("Of National Characters" 20 n.). The note denies that any black nation had produced arts or sciences, and dismisses a learned Jamaican, usually identified as the poet Francis Williams, as likely "admired for slender accomplishments, like a parrot, who speaks a few words plainly." Beattie attacked the note in 1770.

The modern debate has four main positions. Richard Popkin ("Hume's Racism," 1977–78) showed that the note was cited by defenders of slavery and linked it to polygenist theories of separate human origins. John Immerwahr ("Hume's Revised Racism," 1992) argued that the late revision shows Hume holding the view more deliberately, not giving it up. Emmanuel Chukwudi Eze (2000) argued that the note expresses Hume's science of human nature and cannot be quarantined from the philosophy. Don Garrett ("Hume's Revised Racism Revisited," 2000) argued that the revision need not mean what Immerwahr says, and that the note violates Hume's own empirical method rather than following from it. All four call the note racist; they disagree about its place in his thought. Felix Waldmann's *Further Letters of David Hume* (2014) printed a letter of 1766 showing Hume helping Lord Hertford toward buying a slave plantation on Grenada. In September 2020 the University of Edinburgh announced, as an interim decision pending review, that the David Hume Tower would be known as 40 George Square, citing his comments on race. A number of Edinburgh scholars criticized the decision.

## Live debates

**(1) The New Hume.** Did Hume reduce causation to regularity in objects plus a habit in the mind, or did he believe in real powers we cannot know? The traditional reading takes the *Treatise* at its word: "necessity is something, that exists in the mind, not in objects" (T 1.3.14.22). The **New Hume** reading of John P. Wright (*The Sceptical Realism of David Hume*, 1983), Edward Craig (*The Mind of God and the Works of Man*, 1987) and Galen Strawson (*The Secret Connexion*, 1989) points to the first *Enquiry*'s hidden powers: "nature has kept us at a great distance from all her secrets" (EHU 4.16), and the "ultimate springs and principles are totally shut up from human curiosity and enquiry" (EHU 4.12). On this reading Hume is a skeptical realist: necessary connexions exist in things, and we can neither perceive them nor form a positive idea of them. Kenneth Winkler ("The New Hume," 1991), Simon Blackburn ("Hume and Thick Connexions," 1990) and Millican ("Against the 'New Hume'," 2007) reply that the talk of secret powers is ordinary usage or the opponent's vocabulary, and that Hume's theory of ideas leaves no content for necessity beyond regularity and the felt transition. P. J. E. Kail (*Projection and Realism in Hume's Philosophy*, 2007) defends a middle position. Rupert Read and Kenneth Richman's collection *The New Hume Debate* (2000) is the standard map.

**(2) Is the induction argument skeptical or descriptive?** The traditional reading, held by Popper, Stroud and Millican, is that inductive inference has no rational justification. The **descriptive reading** says Hume shows only that the inference is not *produced* by reasoning, a claim about which faculty does the work. Tom Beauchamp and Alexander Rosenberg (*Hume and the Problem of Causation*, 1981) argued that his target is the rationalist demand for demonstration. Garrett (1997) argued that the conclusion concerns what causes the inference, not its justification. David Owen's *Hume's Reason* (1999) read "reason" as the faculty of chaining ideas through intermediate steps, so the point is that induction is no such chain. Millican ("Hume's Sceptical Doubts concerning Induction," 2002) replies that Hume asks for a foundation, which is a normative request: "As an agent, I am quite satisfied in the point; but as a philosopher, who has some share of curiosity, I will not say scepticism, I want to learn the foundation of this inference" (EHU 4.21). The descriptive readings have made it standard to separate Hume's psychological and normative claims, and most readers still find a normative skeptical conclusion in Section 4.

**(3) The Appendix problem.** In the Appendix of 1740 Hume withdrew his confidence in his account of personal identity: "I find myself involv'd in such a labyrinth, that, I must confess, I neither know how to correct my former opinions, nor how to render them consistent" (T App.10). He named the trouble: "there are two principles, which I cannot render consistent; nor is it in my power to renounce either of them, viz. that all our distinct perceptions are distinct existences, and that the mind never perceives any real connexion among distinct existences" (T App.21). The two principles do not contradict each other, so the puzzle is what Hume thought they conflict with. Garrett ("Hume's Self-Doubts about Personal Identity," 1981) surveyed the candidates and argued that Hume could not explain what makes a perception belong to one mind rather than another. Stroud (1977) read it as the failure of association to explain the idea of a unified self. Donald Ainslie (2001) located it in the mind's reflection on itself, and Galen Strawson's *The Evident Connexion* (2011) argued that Hume saw he could not explain our experience of a connected mind. There is no consensus, and the *Enquiry*'s silence on personal identity gives no later text to settle it.

**(4) Is and ought.** T 3.1.1.27 complains that moral authors slide from "is" to "ought" without explanation: "This change is imperceptible; but is, however, of the last consequence." The **standard reading**, defended by Antony Flew ("On the Interpretation of Hume," 1963) and W. D. Hudson ("Hume on *Is* and *Ought*," 1964), finds a logical gap: no factual premises entail a moral conclusion. [Alasdair MacIntyre](../../eras/05-20th-century/alasdair-macintyre.md) ("Hume on 'Is' and 'Ought'," 1959) argued that Hume does not deny the step, since his own ethics moves from facts about human needs and approval to moral conclusions, and that the passage attacks the rationalists' and theologians' derivations. John Searle's "How to Derive 'Ought' from 'Is'" (1964) attacked the logical claim itself. Most recent scholars read the passage inside Hume's argument that moral distinctions are not derived from reason (T 3.1.1), as a consequence of his sentimentalism rather than a freestanding law of logic. The library entry's phrasing, a demand for explanation whose strength is debated, reflects that view.

**(5) Who speaks for Hume in the *Dialogues*?** Philo the skeptic, Cleanthes the defender of the design argument and Demea the a priori theologian all get strong speeches. The narrator Pamphilus ends by judging "that Philo's principles are more probable than Demea's; but that those of Cleanthes approach still nearer to the truth" (DNR 12.34). In a letter to Gilbert Elliot of March 1751 Hume said he made Cleanthes the hero and asked for help strengthening his side. The **Philo reading**, Kemp Smith's (1935) and the majority view, treats the verdict as cover. The **Cleanthes reading**, now rare, takes the letter and verdict at face value. A third group, including Nelson Pike and J. C. A. Gaskin, holds that Hume's view emerges from the whole exchange, including Philo's concessions. The hardest text for the Philo reading is Philo's reversal in Part 12: "A purpose, an intention, a design strikes every where the most careless, the most stupid thinker" (DNR 12.2).

**(6) Theist, deist, agnostic or atheist?** Philo goes on to call the dispute between theist and atheist "merely verbal" (DNR 12.7), and reduces natural theology to the proposition "That the cause or causes of order in the universe probably bear some remote analogy to human intelligence" (DNR 12.33). Gaskin (*Hume's Philosophy of Religion*, 1978, 2nd ed. 1988) called this "attenuated deism": a bare intelligent cause with no moral attributes and no practical consequences. Paul Russell (*The Riddle of Hume's Treatise*, 2008) argued that the *Treatise* is already an irreligious work in the line of [Hobbes](../../eras/03-early-modern/thomas-hobbes.md) and Anthony Collins, and that "irreligion" fits Hume better than deism or atheism. Others read him as an agnostic in Huxley's sense. A **fideist reading** takes at face value Philo's claim that "To be a philosophical Sceptic is, in a man of letters, the first and most essential step towards being a sound, believing Christian" (DNR 12.33). It has few defenders, because the same irony closes "Of Miracles": the Christian religion "cannot be believed by any reasonable person without one" (EHU 10.41). What is settled is that Hume rejected every argument for a God with moral attributes. The *Natural History* ends: "The whole is a riddle, an ænigma, an inexplicable mystery" (NHR 15.13).

**(7) Miracles: abject failure or sound argument?** Section 10 argues from "A wise man, therefore, proportions his belief to the evidence" (EHU 10.4) to the maxim "That no testimony is sufficient to establish a miracle, unless the testimony be of such a kind, that its falsehood would be more miraculous, than the fact, which it endeavours to establish" (EHU 10.13). John Earman's *Hume's Abject Failure* (2000) gave a Bayesian reconstruction and argued that the argument is either trivial or rests on an illegitimate a priori bar, and that Hume ignored the force of many independent witnesses, which Price had stressed. Robert Fogelin's *A Defense of Hume on Miracles* (2003) replied that Earman misreads the structure: Part 1 sets a standard for testimony, and Part 2 shows that no actual testimony for a religious miracle meets it. On that reading the argument makes no a priori claim. Millican and others have given Bayesian reconstructions on which the maxim comes out sound. Few now defend an a priori proof that miracle reports must be false, and many think the evidential argument, read Fogelin's way, survives.

## Misconceptions, in full form

- **"Hume says induction is irrational, so we should stop using it."** Hume argues that inductive inference has no foundation in reasoning. He never recommends giving it up, and holds that we could not: "Custom, then, is the great guide of human life" (EHU 5.6). He separates the agent, who is satisfied, from the philosopher, who wants a foundation (EHU 4.21). Whether the result is normative at all is debate (2).

- **"Hume proved that miracles cannot occur."** Section 10 is about testimony. It asks when a report of a miracle can rationally be believed and answers that no actual report meets the standard. It does not claim that a violation of a law of nature is impossible.

- **"Hume denied causation, or denied that we know anything about causes."** Hume denied that we perceive necessary connexion in objects and traced the idea to the mind's customary transition. He relied on causal reasoning throughout; his science of man explains belief, passion and morals by causes. Whether he also believed in hidden powers is debate (1).

- **"Reason being the slave of the passions means Hume recommends irrationality."** The claim (T 2.3.3.4) concerns motivation. Reason discovers truth and falsehood and cannot by itself produce action. A passion based on a false belief gives way when the belief is corrected, and reason keeps its authority over evidence.

- **"Hume's scepticism is Pyrrhonian paralysis."** EHU 12 rejects total doubt as unlivable: "Nature is always too strong for principle" (EHU 12.23). The *Treatise* reports the same cure in person: "I dine, I play a game of back-gammon, I converse, and am merry with my friends" (T 1.4.7.9). What remains is the mitigated skepticism of EHU 12.24–34.

- **"Hume's empiricism is Locke and Berkeley carried to absurdity."** This is Reid's and Green's reading. Hume's fork, his account of belief and his moral theory are his own, and his aim was a positive science of human nature.

- **"The *Enquiry* is simply a summary of the *Treatise*."** It is a recasting. It drops personal identity, adds miracles and providence, and rewrites the arguments on induction and liberty. Hume asked to be judged by it, and the learning path follows him.

## Myths about the life

- **The bog.** Edinburgh tradition says that Hume, by then stout, fell into the bog of the drained Nor' Loch on his way to the New Town, and that the fishwives who found him would not pull out "the great infidel" until he recited the Lord's Prayer and the Creed. The story appears in nineteenth-century collections of Edinburgh anecdotes and has no contemporary source. So does the tale that a friend chalked "St David's Street" on his New Town corner and Hume replied that many a better man had been made a saint.

- **The deathbed.** The documented death is calm. Hume wrote *My Own Life* in April 1776, knowing he was dying, and said he had "never suffered a moment's abatement of my spirits" (MOL 20). James Boswell visited on 7 July 1776 and recorded, with dismay, that Hume denied any belief in an afterlife and was cheerful about annihilation. Adam Smith's letter to William Strahan of 9 November 1776, printed with *My Own Life* in 1777, described him joking about excuses he might give Charon for delay. Smith later said the letter brought him more abuse than his attack on the whole commercial system of Britain. Nineteenth-century religious tracts then spread stories of a deathbed terror or recantation, sometimes credited to an unnamed nurse. No witness of the time supports them, and Boswell, Smith and the physicians William Cullen and Joseph Black all describe the opposite.
