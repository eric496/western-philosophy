# Works and Texts: The Corpus in Reading Order

*Focus study: Hume · [Dossier home](README.md) · Library entry: [David Hume](../../eras/03-early-modern/david-hume.md) · Study guide: [*Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding*](../../study/hume-enquiry.md) · Arguments in full: [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md)*

Hume's philosophy exists twice. He wrote it first as one long book, *A Treatise of Human Nature* (1739–40), published anonymously when he was in his late twenties. When it failed, he wrote it again, in pieces and in a different style: essays from 1741, the *Philosophical Essays Concerning Human Understanding* in 1748 (later the first *Enquiry*), the *Enquiry Concerning the Principles of Morals* in 1751, the *Dissertation on the Passions* in 1757. Around these stand the political and literary essays, a six-volume *History of England*, two essays on religion he withdrew before publication, the *Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion* he held back until after his death, and his letters.

Two facts shape how this corpus is read. The first is the doubling. On most topics the reader has a *Treatise* version and a later one, and they differ in argument as well as tone; in 1775 Hume asked that only the later ones be taken as his views. The second is the line between what he published and what he kept back. His most direct writing on religion reached print only after he died.

## A note on citation before the works

The dossier cites Hume by the **Clarendon paragraph numbers**. The Clarendon Edition of the Works of David Hume (Oxford; general editors Tom L. Beauchamp, David Fate Norton and M. A. Stewart) numbered every paragraph, and the numbers now appear in most student editions. The *Treatise* is cited by book, part, section and paragraph: `T 1.3.6.11` is Book 1, Part 3, Section 6, paragraph 11, with `T Intro.7` for the Introduction and `T App.10` for the Appendix. The first *Enquiry* is cited by section and paragraph, `EHU 4.18`; the second likewise, `EPM 9.22`, with `EPM App1.3` for its appendices. The *Abstract* is cited by paragraph (`Abstract 27`), the *Dialogues* by part and paragraph (`DNR 12.1`), the *Natural History of Religion* by section and paragraph (`NHR 15.13`), *My Own Life* by paragraph (`MOL 6`), and the essays by title and paragraph.

The editions behind the numbers are David Fate Norton and Mary J. Norton's *Treatise* (Clarendon, 2007, two volumes), Beauchamp's first *Enquiry* (Clarendon, 2000) and second *Enquiry* (Clarendon, 1998), and Beauchamp's volume with the *Dissertation on the Passions* and the *Natural History* (Clarendon, 2007). The essays and the *Dialogues* have no Clarendon volume yet.

**SBN pages.** Before the Clarendon edition, Hume was cited by page in L. A. Selby-Bigge's Oxford editions of the *Treatise* (1888) and the *Enquiries* (1894), later revised by P. H. Nidditch. The combined form is **SBN**. These pages persist because almost everything written on Hume between the 1890s and about 2000 cites them. A reader following a footnote in Kemp Smith, Stroud or Mackie needs to find "SBN 469" in a modern text. The dossier gives SBN only where it helps with that.

**Hume Texts Online** ([davidhume.org](https://davidhume.org/)), edited by Peter Millican and Amyas Merivale, gives the full texts in Hume's own spelling, with the Clarendon number and the SBN page at the head of every paragraph. Its line for the miracles maxim begins `[E 10.4, SBN 110-1]`. It prints the *Treatise* Introduction as `T 0.1` onward, which this dossier writes `T Intro.1`; it numbers the *Dialogues*' opening letter as Part 0, and the *Natural History*'s Introduction likewise, which the dossier keeps as `DNR 0.4` and `NHR Intro.1`. Every Hume quotation in the dossier is checked against it.

## The *Treatise* and its defenders

### *A Treatise of Human Nature* (1739–40)

Hume wrote the *Treatise* in France between 1734 and 1737, "first at Rheims, but chiefly at La Fleche, in Anjou" (MOL 5). It came out in two instalments, without his name. Books 1 and 2, "Of the Understanding" and "Of the Passions", were published in London by John Noon in January 1739; *My Own Life* says "the end of 1738" (MOL 5). Book 3, "Of Morals", followed in November 1740 from Thomas Longman, with an Appendix.

The title page announces "An Attempt to introduce the experimental Method of Reasoning into Moral Subjects". *Moral* here means everything human, as against natural: knowledge, passion, morals, politics and criticism. In the Introduction Hume argues that "the science of man is the only solid foundation for the other sciences", and that this science must rest "on experience and observation" (T Intro.7). The Advertisement to Books 1 and 2 says why Book 3 waited: understanding and passions "make a compleat chain of reasoning by themselves", and he would "try the taste of the public" before going on (Advertisement 1739). Politics and criticism never came as parts of the *Treatise*; they went into the essays.

The Appendix has two parts. The first (T App.1–9) adds material on belief for insertion into Book 1. The second (T App.10–21) is the confession about personal identity: on review he finds himself "involv'd in such a labyrinth, that, I must confess, I neither know how to correct my former opinions, nor how to render them consistent" (T App.10). It is a core text for Week 4.

Hume's verdict on the reception is famous: "It fell dead-born from the press, without reaching such distinction, as even to excite a murmur among the zealots" (MOL 6). It is half true. The book had reviews, mostly hostile, in London, Leipzig and the Netherlands. What it lacked was the public Hume wanted.

### The *Abstract* (1740)

Early in 1740 Hume published, again anonymously, *An Abstract of a Book lately Published; entituled, A Treatise of Human Nature*, a pamphlet posing as a reviewer's summary. Its aim was "to render a larger work more intelligible to ordinary capacities, by abridging it" (Abstract Pref. i), and it keeps to one topic: "I have chosen one simple argument, which I have carefully traced from the beginning to the end" (Abstract Pref. iv). That makes Abstract 8–27 Hume's shortest complete statement of the induction argument, read in Week 2. The pamphlet was long ascribed to Adam Smith until J. M. Keynes and Piero Sraffa, reprinting it in 1938, made the case for Hume. It ends by naming his originality: "the use he makes of the principle of the association of ideas", the ties that are "the cement of the universe" (Abstract 35).

### *A Letter from a Gentleman to his Friend in Edinburgh* (1745)

In 1744–45 Hume stood for the chair of moral philosophy at Edinburgh. His opponents circulated charges drawn from the *Treatise*, the "Specimen of the Principles concerning Religion and Morality" (Letter 1). Hume answered in a letter, and a friend had it printed as *A Letter from a Gentleman*. It defends his views on causation, God and morals in gentler terms than the *Treatise*. It did not save the candidacy. Ernest Mossner and John V. Price reprinted it in 1967; Steinberg's Hackett *Enquiry* includes it.

## The recasting

### The essays (1741 onward)

*Essays, Moral and Political* appeared at Edinburgh in two volumes, in 1741 and 1742; *My Own Life* dates the "first part of my Essays" to 1742 (MOL 6). These were short essays in the manner of Addison, for the polite reader. *Three Essays, Moral and Political* (1748) added "Of National Characters", "Of the Original Contract" and "Of Passive Obedience". The *Political Discourses* (Edinburgh, 1752), on commerce, money, interest and the balance of trade, was "the only work of mine that was successful on the first publication" (MOL 10).

From 1753 the essays and the philosophical works were gathered as *Essays and Treatises on Several Subjects*, which Hume revised through edition after edition to 1777. He cut some essays along the way ("Of Essay-Writing", "Of Moral Prejudices" and others) and changed many more. The posthumous edition of 1777, with his last corrections, is the usual copy-text. Miller's Liberty Fund edition prints it, with the withdrawn essays in a section of their own.

### The first *Enquiry* (1748, retitled 1758)

Hume decided the *Treatise* had failed "more from the manner than the matter", and that he had gone "to the press too early" (MOL 8). He cast Book 1 anew. Andrew Millar published the result in London in April 1748, under Hume's name, as *Philosophical Essays Concerning Human Understanding*. The 1758 collected edition retitled it *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding*.

The book is more than a shorter Book 1. It drops space and time, the long section on belief in body, and personal identity. It adds "Of Miracles" (EHU 10) and "Of a Particular Providence and of a Future State" (EHU 11), which bore another title in 1748. Liberty and necessity move in from *Treatise* Book 2 (EHU 8). The first section sets the new terms: philosophy should not cut its reader off from life. "Be a philosopher; but, amidst all your philosophy, be still a man" (EHU 1.6). At first the book was "little more successful than the Treatise" (MOL 8). It became the work through which most readers, Kant among them, knew Hume's theory of knowledge.

### The second *Enquiry* (1751)

*An Enquiry Concerning the Principles of Morals* recasts *Treatise* Book 3. Millar's title page says 1751; *My Own Life* says 1752. Hume thought it "of all my writings, historical, philosophical, or literary, incomparably the best", and recorded that "It came unnoticed and unobserved into the world" (MOL 10).

It keeps the conclusion that morals rest on sentiment and changes the route. Sympathy, central in *Treatise* Book 3, moves into the background; the argument turns on which qualities are useful or agreeable, to their owner or to others. Justice still rests on public utility, but the word "artificial" is gone (EPM 3). Appendix 1, "Concerning Moral Sentiment", restates the case against basing morals on reason alone, and the book closes with "A Dialogue" on moral customs across nations.

### The *Dissertation on the Passions* (1757)

Book 2 shrank into the *Dissertation on the Passions*, one of the *Four Dissertations*: a brief, schematic account of the indirect passions without the long treatment of the will. The claim that reason is the slave of the passions (T 2.3.3.4) has no equal in it, so for that the reader goes to the *Treatise*.

## The *History of England* (1754–62)

In 1752 the Faculty of Advocates made Hume its librarian, which gave him "the command of a large library" (MOL 11). He used it to write a history of England, published backwards in six volumes: the Stuarts (1754, 1756), the Tudors (1759), and the earlier period (two volumes dated 1762). It became a best-seller and made him rich. In his century he was known first as a historian. The *History* is outside this dossier; the Liberty Fund edition (1983) is the accessible text.

## *Four Dissertations* (1757) and the withdrawn essays

In 1755–56 Millar printed a volume of five dissertations: the *Natural History of Religion*, "Of the Passions", "Of Tragedy", "Of Suicide" and "Of the Immortality of the Soul". Before issue, the last two were cut from the printed sheets. The usual account is that Millar feared prosecution, with pressure from William Warburton's circle in the background; how far Hume acted from fear or prudence is disputed. Hume wrote "Of the Standard of Taste" to fill the gap, and the book appeared in 1757 as *Four Dissertations*.

The ***Natural History of Religion*** asks about religion's "origin in human nature" rather than "its foundation in reason" (NHR Intro.1). Hume argues that polytheism came first, from hope and fear about unknown causes, and that theism grew out of it through flattery of a favoured god. The last section ends in suspended judgement: the subject is "a riddle, an ænigma, an inexplicable mystery" (NHR 15.13). Week 6 reads selections.

The withdrawn essays survived in a few printed copies. "Of Suicide" argues that taking one's life breaks no duty to God, neighbour or self. "Of the Immortality of the Soul" finds the arguments for an afterlife weak, and opens with a sentence whose irony the reader is meant to hear: "it is the gospel, and the gospel alone, that has brought life and immortality to light" (Immortality 1). Both appeared in French in 1770 and anonymously in English in 1777. The dossier does not set them; Coleman and Miller both print them.

## The Advertisement of 1775

In October 1775, ill and revising his works for the last time, Hume sent his publisher William Strahan a short Advertisement for the volume containing the two *Enquiries*. It was to go into unsold copies of the current edition and every later one; it stands in the edition of 1777. Its text is not in the dossier's corpus, so it is paraphrased. Most of the principles in these pieces, it says, were first published in a work in three volumes, the *Treatise*, planned before the author left college. Finding it unsuccessful, he saw his error in publishing too early and cast the whole anew. Some writers had still aimed their answers at that "juvenile work", which he never acknowledged. He now asks that these later pieces alone be taken as containing his philosophical sentiments and principles.

In the covering letter Hume called it a complete answer to Thomas Reid and James Beattie, who had attacked the *Treatise* in books of 1764 and 1770 (Greig II). The disowning is read two ways. Some scholars take it at face value and treat the *Enquiries* as the mature position. Others see a tactic against critics quoting a book he never signed, and note that the *Treatise* argues more fully. The dossier follows Hume's preference in reading order, as the [learning path](../../learning-path.md) does, and reads the *Treatise* for what the *Enquiries* leave out: the external world, personal identity, the passions and justice.

## The posthumous works

### *My Own Life* (1776, published 1777)

Hume wrote his autobiography in April 1776, knowing he was dying. Strahan published it in 1777 with Adam Smith's letter on Hume's last months. It is "little more than the History of my Writings" (MOL 1), and it is the source of Hume's own verdicts on his books. It is also a crafted piece, and some of its dates are a year off. Read it in Week 1; Miller prints it at the front of the *Essays*.

### *Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion* (1779)

Hume was drafting the *Dialogues* by 1751, when he sent part of the manuscript to Gilbert Elliot of Minto and asked for help in making Cleanthes, the defender of the design argument, stronger. He revised the work around 1761 and again in 1776, rewriting parts of the last section. Friends advised against publication. He left the manuscript to Strahan, and a codicil of August 1776 passed it to his nephew if it was not published within two and a half years. Strahan let the term lapse, and the nephew, also David Hume, published it in 1779. The manuscript, with Hume's revisions, is at the Royal Society of Edinburgh; Norman Kemp Smith's edition (1935) built its text on it.

The work has twelve parts and a frame. Pamphilus, Cleanthes' pupil, reports to his friend Hermippus a conversation among Cleanthes, the sceptic Philo and the orthodox Demea. The frame explains the form: a question "so obscure and uncertain, that human reason can reach no fixed determination with regard to it" calls for dialogue (DNR 0.4). The ending awards the prize without settling anything: Pamphilus thinks "Philo's principles are more probable than Demea's; but that those of Cleanthes approach still nearer to the truth" (DNR 12.34). Who speaks for Hume is a dispute; Kemp Smith argued for Philo throughout, and others hear Hume across several voices. [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md) sets out the positions.

## The letters

The standard editions are J. Y. T. Greig (ed.), *The Letters of David Hume* (Oxford, 1932, two volumes), and Raymond Klibansky and Ernest C. Mossner (eds), *New Letters of David Hume* (Oxford, 1954), cited here as Greig and *New Letters*. They date the works, as with the Advertisement and the *Dialogues*. They also carry Hume's comments on his own arguments. Writing to Francis Hutcheson in 1739, he answers the charge that Book 3 lacks warmth for virtue by distinguishing the anatomist from the painter (Greig I). In 1751 he advises Gilbert Elliot to read the *Philosophical Essays* and not the *Treatise* (Greig I). The long letter of 1734 to a physician describes his breakdown during the years of study (Greig I); [01-life-and-times.md](01-life-and-times.md) draws on it. The letters are not in the checked corpus, so the dossier paraphrases them.

## The works in the dossier's reading order

The order is the learning path's: the first *Enquiry* first, the *Treatise* beside it as the fuller version, then the *Dialogues*, then the moral works.

### Week 1. EHU 1–3; T Intro and T 1.1.1–7; *My Own Life*

EHU 1 weighs the "easy and obvious" philosophy against the "accurate and abstruse". EHU 2 gives impressions and ideas, the copy principle and the missing shade of blue; EHU 3 the principles of association. Then the *Treatise* versions: the Introduction's science of man, T 1.1.1 on the origin of ideas, T 1.1.4 on association, and T 1.1.7 on abstract ideas, where Hume sides with Berkeley against Locke. **Difficulty:** low; T 1.1.7 moderate. **Place:** the instrument every later argument uses.

### Week 2. EHU 4–5; T 1.3.6–8; *Abstract* 8–27

EHU 4 states the fork and the argument about induction; EHU 5 the sceptical solution, custom and belief. T 1.3.6 gives the *Treatise* version; T 1.3.7–8 the account of belief as a lively idea. The *Abstract* runs the same chain in a dozen pages. **Difficulty:** moderate; T 1.3.7 high, since Hume's account of belief shifts between it and the Appendix. **Place:** the centre of the dossier and of the Stage 3 checkpoint.

### Week 3. EHU 6–8; T 1.3.14; T 2.3.1–2

EHU 6 on probability is short. EHU 7 and T 1.3.14 give necessary connexion and the two definitions of cause; the *Treatise* section is longer and blunter about placing necessity in the mind. EHU 8 and T 2.3.1–2 reconcile liberty with necessity. **Difficulty:** moderate to high. **Place:** causation completed and first applied.

### Week 4. EHU 12; T 1.4.1–2, 1.4.6–7; Appendix 10–21

EHU 12 gives the sceptical arguments and mitigated scepticism, ending with books of "divinity or school metaphysics" committed to the flames (EHU 12.34). The *Treatise* goes further: scepticism about reason (T 1.4.1), belief in body (T 1.4.2), the bundle theory (T 1.4.6), and the conclusion of Book 1, where despair is cured by ordinary life: "I dine, I play a game of back-gammon, I converse, and am merry with my friends" (T 1.4.7.9). The Appendix confession closes the week. **Difficulty:** high; T 1.4.2 is the hardest section in the dossier. **Place:** the *Treatise* selections the learning path allows only after the *Enquiry*.

### Week 5. EHU 9–11; DNR 1–5

EHU 9 on the reason of animals, EHU 10 on miracles, and EHU 11, where a friend's speech argues against inferring a providential god from the order of nature. Then the *Dialogues*' frame and Parts 1–5: sceptic against dogmatist, Cleanthes' design argument, Philo's first objections. **Difficulty:** low to moderate. **Place:** the religion thread opens, with EHU 11 as rehearsal.

### Week 6. DNR 6–12; NHR selections

Parts 6–8 give Philo's rival hypotheses; Part 9 the a priori argument and its refutation; Parts 10–11 the problem of evil; Part 12 Philo's reversal and the verdict. NHR sections 1–2, 6 and 15. **Difficulty:** moderate; Part 12 high, for its irony. **Place:** the design argument tested, and the natural history of belief beside it.

### Week 7. T 2.3.3; T 3.1.1–2; T 3.2.1–2; T 3.3.1 selections

T 2.3.3 argues that reason alone cannot move the will. T 3.1.1 denies that moral distinctions come from reason and ends with the is/ought paragraph (T 3.1.1.27); T 3.1.2 grounds them in a moral sense. T 3.2.1–2 make justice an artificial virtue born of convention. T 3.3.1 gives sympathy and the general point of view. **Difficulty:** moderate; T 3.2.1 high. **Place:** the first version of the ethics, which makes the second *Enquiry* easier to read.

### Week 8. EPM 1, 2, 3, 5, 9, Appendix 1; two essays; capstone

EPM 1 frames reason against sentiment; 2 and 3 treat benevolence and justice; 5 asks why utility pleases; 9 concludes, with the sensible knave (EPM 9.22–25). "Of the Standard of Taste" asks how judgements of beauty can be right if beauty lies in sentiment; "Of the Original Contract" argues against basing government on consent. **Difficulty:** low to moderate. **Place:** Hume's preferred ethics, and his method carried into taste and politics.

## Editions

The rule is the learning path's: Steinberg or Beauchamp for the *Enquiry*, and not Selby-Bigge/Nidditch. Hume wrote in English, so the choice is about text, numbering and notes. Use an edition with Clarendon paragraph numbers.

**The course texts:**

- **First *Enquiry*:** Eric Steinberg (ed.), *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding*, 2nd ed. (Hackett, 1993), with the *Abstract* and *A Letter from a Gentleman*; or Tom L. Beauchamp (ed.), *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding* (Oxford Philosophical Texts, 1999), the student edition of the Clarendon text.
- ***Treatise*:** David Fate Norton and Mary J. Norton (eds), *A Treatise of Human Nature* (Oxford Philosophical Texts, 2000), the student edition of their Clarendon text, with the *Abstract* and annotations.
- ***Dialogues* and NHR:** Dorothy Coleman (ed.), *Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion and Other Writings* (Cambridge Texts in the History of Philosophy, 2007). For NHR in full, Hume Texts Online or Beauchamp's Clarendon volume.
- **Second *Enquiry*:** Tom L. Beauchamp (ed.), *An Enquiry Concerning the Principles of Morals* (Oxford Philosophical Texts, 1998).
- **Essays and *My Own Life*:** Eugene F. Miller (ed.), *Essays, Moral, Political, and Literary*, revised ed. (Liberty Fund, 1987), with the withdrawn essays and *My Own Life*.

**The reference set** is the Clarendon volumes, for checking a reading rather than a first pass.

**Avoid as first reads** the Selby-Bigge editions in Nidditch's revisions, the *Treatise* (2nd ed., 1978) and the *Enquiries* (3rd ed., 1975). Their texts are superseded by the Clarendon ones, they lack paragraph numbers, and their pages give a beginner little help. Keep one only to follow SBN references. Avoid also free reprints without paragraph numbers, many of which modernise the spelling silently; Hume Texts Online is the free text to use.

**A note on quotation.** The dossier quotes Hume from Hume Texts Online, in the original spelling: the *Treatise* has "shou'd", "'tis" and "deriv'd". Each quotation carries its Clarendon reference, so you can find it in any edition above. The letters and the 1775 Advertisement are paraphrased.

## The reading order

1. **Week 1, ideas:** EHU 1–3; T Intro, T 1.1.1–7; *My Own Life*.
2. **Week 2, induction and belief:** EHU 4–5; T 1.3.6–8; *Abstract* 8–27.
3. **Week 3, necessity and freedom:** EHU 6–8; T 1.3.14; T 2.3.1–2.
4. **Week 4, scepticism and the self:** EHU 12; T 1.4.1–2, 1.4.6–7; T App.10–21.
5. **Week 5, religion opened:** EHU 9–11; DNR 1–5.
6. **Week 6, design and evil:** DNR 6–12; NHR selections.
7. **Week 7, the *Treatise*'s ethics:** T 2.3.3; T 3.1.1–2; T 3.2.1–2; T 3.3.1 selections.
8. **Week 8, the second *Enquiry* and two essays:** EPM 1, 2, 3, 5, 9, App. 1; "Of the Standard of Taste"; "Of the Original Contract"; the capstone.
