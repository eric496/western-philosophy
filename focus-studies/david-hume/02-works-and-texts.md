# Works and Texts: The Corpus in Reading Order
**English** | [简体中文](../../zh/focus-studies/david-hume/02-works-and-texts.md)

Hume's textual problem is the opposite of Spinoza's. Almost nothing was left in a desk: he published steadily for four decades, revised his books through edition after edition, withdrew pieces he no longer liked, and in his last year tried to dictate by formal notice which of his works posterity should count as his philosophy. The corpus is therefore *managed*, and the order in which you read it is partly his own design. This dossier follows the learning path's order (Stage 3, week 10), deepened: the two *Enquiries* first, as Hume demanded; the *Dialogues* next; then the *Treatise* itself, in selections — read last, and read against its author's instruction that it be disregarded. The essays, the suppressed pieces, the *History*, and the autobiography and letters stand behind those four as the public career and the record. Citation conventions and edition rulings are given with each work and gathered at the end.

## 1. An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding (1748; retitled 1758)

Published in 1748 as *Philosophical Essays Concerning Human Understanding* and given its familiar title in the 1758 edition, this is the recasting of *Treatise* Book I that Hume wanted read in its place — and the dossier's door. It is short (about 130 pages), written in the clearest prose Hume ever achieved, and deceptive in proportion: its conclusions look tamer than they are until a qualifying clause turns. It drops the *Treatise*'s long machinery of space, time, and personal identity, sharpens the arguments it keeps, and adds two of his most consequential pieces of writing, on miracles and on providence.

**Section map.** I: the two species of philosophy and the science of man. II: impressions, ideas, and the copy principle. III: the association of ideas. IV: the fork (relations of ideas vs. matters of fact) and the problem of induction. V: the skeptical solution — custom. VI: probability. VII: the idea of necessary connection and the two definitions of cause. VIII: liberty and necessity (compatibilism). IX: the reason of animals. X: miracles and testimony. XI: a particular providence and a future state. XII: academical philosophy — mitigated skepticism, and the fork applied to libraries in the famous closing paragraph. **Use:** read it whole, first. The library's [study guide](../../study/hume-enquiry.md) accompanies it section by section, with the three load-bearing arguments (induction, necessary connection, miracles) reconstructed in full; this dossier treats the guide as its companion rather than repeating it. **Editions:** Beauchamp (Oxford) is the scholarly text and the one this dossier's citations assume. Steinberg (Hackett) is the affordable student alternative; Millican's Oxford World's Classics edition is a sound third option. Do **not** read it in Selby-Bigge/Nidditch: that text has been superseded (the learning path's ruling, kept here).

## 2. An Enquiry Concerning the Principles of Morals (1751)

The recasting of *Treatise* Book III, and the book Hume judged — in *My Own Life* — "incomparably the best" of his writings. The verdict is informative: the second *Enquiry* is the philosophy with the scaffolding removed. Where the *Treatise* derives morals from sympathy's machinery, the *Enquiry* proceeds by cataloguing the qualities we approve — distinguishing those **useful** or **agreeable** to their possessor from those useful or agreeable to others — and letting utility and sentiment explain approbation as it goes. **Section map:** I: the general principles of morals (reason and sentiment both given offices). II–III: benevolence and justice. IV: political society. V: why utility pleases. VI–VIII: qualities useful or agreeable to ourselves and to others. IX: conclusion — the definition of virtue. Appendices: of self-love; of moral sentiment; some farther considerations with regard to justice; of some verbal disputes. The learning path assigns selections; **the dossier reads it whole** — it is brief, it is his favorite, and Section III's account of justice as an artificial virtue is the ethics' counterpart to the first *Enquiry*'s Section VII. **Editions:** Beauchamp (Oxford Philosophical Texts, on the Clarendon text) is the ruling, with citation by section and paragraph keyed to it.

## 3. Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion (published 1779)

The dossier's third read and Hume's most carefully withheld book. He had the work substantially drafted by 1751 — his letters to Gilbert Elliot show it in progress that year — and he revised it at intervals for the rest of his life, making final additions in the summer of 1776. He repeatedly arranged for posthumous publication and repeatedly met reluctance: Adam Smith, asked to see it through the press, declined; his publisher hesitated too. It appeared in 1779, published by his nephew David Hume, its title page naming no bookseller. **Manuscript caution:** Hume never published this book himself, and the text that survives is his autograph manuscript with its layers of revision — a fact modern editions respect by taking that manuscript, with his final corrections, as copy-text. Three speakers dispute the argument from design and its neighbors: Demea (orthodoxy), Cleanthes (the design argument's Newtonian champion), and Philo (the skeptic) — and who speaks for Hume is undecided by design; the narrator Pamphilus closes by judging Philo's principles "more probable" than Demea's but Cleanthes' "still nearer to the truth," a verdict the dossier treats as part of the machine, not a key to it. **Shape:** twelve Parts — the design argument at full strength in Parts II–VIII, the problem of evil in X–XI, Philo's startling closing concessions and reversals in XII. **Editions:** Coleman (Cambridge Texts, 2007), the learning path's ruling, kept here: based on the manuscript and bundling the shorter religious writings that belong with it. Kemp Smith's edition (1935) is historically important — its introduction launched the naturalist reading of Hume — but it is not the working text. Cite by Part (DNR 12).

## 4. A Treatise of Human Nature (1739–40) — read last, in selections, with the Abstract (1740)

The masterpiece arrives fourth, on purpose. **Structure:** Book I, *Of the Understanding* (space and time; knowledge and probability, including causation at 1.3; the skeptical system and personal identity at 1.4); Book II, *Of the Passions* (direct and indirect passions, the will and liberty); Book III, *Of Morals* (moral distinctions, justice and the artificial virtues, the is–ought passage at 3.1.1). Books I–II were published anonymously in January 1739; Book III followed in 1740, with an Appendix in which Hume confessed his account of personal identity inconsistent — a confession no summary should soften and which [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md) treats as a standing tension. In *My Own Life* he recorded that it "fell dead-born from the press." In 1740 he also published, anonymously, *An Abstract of a Book Lately Published* — a lucid summary and defense, aimed precisely at the causation argument and the theory of belief. Long attributed to Adam Smith, the *Abstract* has been generally accepted as Hume's own work since Keynes and Sraffa argued the attribution in 1938.

**The supersession, and the dossier's policy.** In the Advertisement prefixed to the posthumous *Essays and Treatises* (1777), Hume formally disowned the *Treatise*: noting that critics had "directed all their batteries against that juvenile work, which the Author never acknowledged," he concluded, "the Author desires, that the following Pieces may alone be regarded as containing his philosophical sentiments and principles." The dossier honors the Advertisement's *sequence* — the *Enquiries* first, the *Treatise* after — but not its verdict. The *Treatise* contains whole doctrines the *Enquiries* dropped (personal identity and the Appendix; the full theory of the passions, on which the second *Enquiry*'s ethics silently rests; the is–ought passage, which appears nowhere in the *Enquiries*), and much modern scholarship treats it as the fuller statement of the two. Read last, its differences from the recastings become the lesson. **Use:** read in the dossier's selections — 1.3 (causation and belief), 1.4.6 with the Appendix (personal identity), 2.3.3 (reason and the passions), Book III Part I (moral distinctions and is–ought), and 3.2 (justice). **Editions:** Norton & Norton (Oxford) is the modern scholarly edition and the dossier's reading text; the Clarendon critical edition (Norton & Norton) settles textual questions. Cite as **T 1.3.6** — book.part.section — and give the Selby-Bigge page, revised by Nidditch (SBN), which remains the common reference grid of the scholarship even for readers working from Norton: "T 1.3.6, SBN 87." For the *Treatise*, unlike the first *Enquiry*, SBN is superseded as a reading text but not as a page standard.

## 5. The essays: Essays Moral and Political (1741–42) and the essay corpus

Hume's public career ran on essays. The first *Essays Moral and Political* appeared in Edinburgh in two volumes (1741, 1742) and grew, volume on volume, into the collected *Essays and Treatises on Several Subjects*. The *Political Discourses* (1752) — "Of Commerce," "Of Money," "Of Interest," "Of the Balance of Trade" — are founding documents of political economy; the literary essays ("Of the Standard of Taste" among them; see §6) founded much of modern aesthetics. The corpus is curated like the philosophy: Hume quietly withdrew several early pieces from later collections — among them "Of Essay-Writing," "Of Moral Prejudices," and "Of the Study of History" — and what he cut is part of the evidence about the writer he decided to be. **Use:** the dossier reads a working selection (the epistemological and political heavyweights plus the taste essays) rather than the whole corpus; the plan in [07-study-plan.md](07-study-plan.md) names the set. **Editions:** Miller's *Essays: Moral, Political, and Literary* (Liberty Fund, revised ed., 1987) is the standard complete modern edition and the citation source.

## 6. A Dissertation on the Passions, with Four Dissertations (1757) — and the two suppressed essays

*A Dissertation on the Passions* is Book II of the *Treatise* compressed and calmed: the same doctrine of direct and indirect passions, the will, and liberty, restated in a fraction of the space and without most of the machinery. It appeared in *Four Dissertations* (title page dated 1757): *The Natural History of Religion*; *Of the Passions*; *Of Tragedy*; and *Of the Standard of Taste*. The volume's prehistory is the censorship episode of Hume's career. On the standard scholarly account, it was first printed as *Five Dissertations*, containing two further essays, **"Of Suicide"** and **"Of the Immortality of the Soul"**; before publication, under threat of prosecution for blasphemy pressed on his publisher, Hume withdrew both, and "Of Tragedy" and "Of the Standard of Taste" took their place. Printed sheets of the suppressed pair escaped in a few copies, and the essays reached print by other routes — "Of Suicide" in French translation (1770), and both in English after his death, without his authorization. The dossier states the episode in that hedged form because the details rest on the surviving sheets and the correspondence rather than on any statement of Hume's. **Use:** read the *Dissertation* alongside the *Treatise* selections as the passions' short statement, and the *Natural History* with the *Dialogues* — it is the *Dialogues*' prose sibling on religion's origins. The Clarendon Hume Edition now covers these texts (Beauchamp's *A Dissertation on the Passions; The Natural History of Religion*); Miller's collection carries the two literary dissertations.

## 7. The History of England (1754–61/62)

Six volumes, written and published backwards — the Stuarts first, the Saxons last — because the recent centuries were where the party lies Hume wanted to correct were warmest. Vol. I (1754: James I and Charles I) and Vol. II (1756: 1649 to the Revolution) made the *History*'s reputation and drew fire from Whig and Tory alike, which Hume took, with some justice, as evidence of the impartiality he aimed at; Vols. III–IV (1759: the Tudors) and Vols. V–VI (title page dated 1762: Julius Caesar to Henry VII) completed it. This, not the philosophy, made him famous and financially independent in his lifetime — "le bon David" was first the historian. **Use:** the dossier treats the *History* as background reading in selections — the Charles I chapters above all, where the historian's method and the philosopher's account of testimony and faction meet — not as a sixth philosophical text. **Editions:** the Liberty Fund six-volume edition (ed. William B. Todd), a reprint of the 1778 text, is the standard accessible set.

## 8. My Own Life (1777) and the letters, as sources

Hume wrote his autobiography in April 1776, four months before his death; it was published in 1777, prefixed to the posthumous *Essays and Treatises*, together with Adam Smith's letter to William Strahan describing the deathbed composure that made the little book famous. *My Own Life* is the dossier's source for the career's shape in Hume's own telling — the *Treatise*'s failure, the second *Enquiry* as "incomparably the best," the succession of employments — and it should be read as what it is: a composed final self-portrait by a master of prose, evidence of the first order that still requires checking against the record. The letters supply the checking. Greig's two-volume edition of the *Letters* (1932) remains the standard text, and the correspondence is where the dossier's chronology comes from: the *Dialogues*' composition and publication maneuvers, the *Treatise*'s reception, the quarrel with Rousseau — all are documented there before they are documented anywhere else. Cite letters by correspondent and date.

## Citation conventions — the file in one place

- ***Treatise*:** book.part.section (T 1.3.6; paragraph numbers from the Norton edition where a pinpoint is needed), with the Selby-Bigge/Nidditch page always available (SBN). The *Abstract* is cited by its paragraph numbers in the Norton *Treatise* volume.
- ***Enquiries*:** section.paragraph in Beauchamp's editions (EHU 4.19; EPM 9.1) — the section numbers are stable across every modern edition, the paragraph numbers are Beauchamp's.
- ***Dialogues*:** by Part (DNR 2, DNR 12), in Coleman.
- **Essays and *History*:** essays by title (numbering is not stable across editions), in Miller; the *History* by volume.
- **Letters:** by correspondent and date, in Greig.
