## What this plan is, and where it plugs in

The [learning path](../../learning-path.md) meets Hume in **Stage 3 (Early Modern to Kant)** as a core figure (●) at row 10, after Berkeley (row 8) and Montesquieu (row 9) and before Rousseau (row 11) and Kant (row 13). Its block has a fixed order: the *Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding* **whole**, with §§1–3, 4–5, 7, 10 and 12 as the core; then the *Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion* in selection or whole; then the *Enquiry Concerning the Principles of Morals* in selection; and *Treatise* I.iv selections **only after** the *Enquiry*. Its editions are Steinberg (Hackett) or Beauchamp (Oxford) for the *Enquiry*, not Selby-Bigge/Nidditch, and Coleman (Cambridge Texts) for the *Dialogues*. Its warning: starting with the *Treatise* is the classic error, since Hume himself replaced it with the *Enquiries*. This dossier keeps those editions and that order and widens the block to eight weeks: the first *Enquiry* in Weeks 1–5, the *Dialogues* whole in Weeks 5–6, the *Treatise*'s ethics in Week 7, the second *Enquiry* in Week 8. It adds the *Treatise* beside each part of the first *Enquiry*, so that arguments the *Enquiry* states briefly are met at full length, and it adds *My Own Life* and two essays, "Of the Standard of Taste" and "Of the Original Contract".

One ruling needs stating plainly. The path puts all *Treatise* I.iv selections after the whole *Enquiry*. This plan reads each *Treatise* section after the *Enquiry* section it expands: T 1.1 after EHU 1–3, T 1.3 after EHU 4–8, T 1.4 after EHU 12. So Week 4's *Treatise* I.iv follows the *Enquiry*'s theory of knowledge and its sceptical close, but precedes EHU 9–11, which Week 5 pairs with the *Dialogues*. That is this dossier's reading of the path's rule, not the path's wording. Its point survives: you never meet a *Treatise* argument before its *Enquiry* version. [02-works-and-texts.md](02-works-and-texts.md), "The works in the dossier's reading order," gives the reasons week by week.

Run it as one block in place of Stage 3's Hume row. The path asks for two map-level entries from its row 12 first: [Bayle and Voltaire](../../eras/03-early-modern/bayle-and-voltaire.md) between Leibniz and Hume, and [the moral sense school](../../eras/03-early-modern/moral-sense-school.md) before Hume's moral philosophy, which this plan places at the start of Week 7. Week 8's "Of the Original Contract" leads straight into Rousseau's *Social Contract* at row 11.

**Total cost, stated honestly: ≈47 hours all-in across eight weeks: 36 hours at the desk, ≈4.0 hours of assigned media, and ≈7.5 hours of the daily [Anki](https://apps.ankiweb.net) [FSRS](https://docs.ankiweb.net/deck-options.html) queue (about 8 minutes a day for 56 days, 448 minutes).** The table rounds each week's 56 minutes of cards to 0.9 hours, so its columns sum to 36 + 4.0 + 7.2 = 47.2. With the cards unrounded (7.47 hours) and the media in exact minutes (238 minutes, 3.97 hours), the total is 47.4. Weekly totals run from 4.9 to 7.2 hours. Optional items are not counted. [08-bibliography.md](08-bibliography.md) gives measured lengths for Millican's lectures and the *In Our Time* programme, but none for *Philosophize This!*, whose pages print no running time. This plan prices each of its episodes at about 35 minutes, an estimate from the length of the published transcripts that fits the path's own range of 30–35 minutes.

**The accounting against the path, stated plainly: these hours sit on top of Stage 3's budget (16 weeks, 72 desk hours, ≈111 all-in), they are not hidden inside it.** The path fixes no week count for its Hume row. At Stage 3's rate of about 4.5 desk hours a week, the row reads as roughly three weeks, about 13.5 desk hours; that is this dossier's estimate, not the path's figure. Most of the media is already in the path. *Philosophize This!* eps. 51–54 lie inside its Stage 3 relay (≈9 hours from Locke to Kant), Millican's Hume lectures inside its in-stage *General Philosophy* subset (the whole course runs about 7 hours), and the [MIT OCW 24.01](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/24-01-classics-of-western-philosophy-spring-2016/) notes are its own Session B companion. About 3.3 of the 4.0 media hours are therefore not new cost; the 42-minute *In Our Time* programme is. Take away the row's desk time and its share of the card queue (about 3 hours) as well, and the net addition to Stage 3 is roughly 27 hours, again an estimate.

Stage 3 has three other dossiers. The [Descartes dossier](../rene-descartes/README.md) opens it at ≈45 hours all-in, a net addition of roughly 30 by [its own plan](../rene-descartes/07-study-plan.md). The [Kant dossier](../immanuel-kant/README.md) closes it at ≈47 hours all-in by [its own plan](../immanuel-kant/07-study-plan.md). ## Week 1 — Ideas: *My Own Life*, EHU 1–3, *Treatise* 1.1 (~5.1 hrs: 4 desk + 0.2 media + 0.9 cards)

**Primary.** *My Own Life* (MOL 1–21) first, the short account Hume dated 18 April 1776, about twenty minutes; keep it for Week 8, when [01-life-and-times.md](01-life-and-times.md) sets it against the record. Then EHU 1–3, about an hour. EHU 1 (1.1–17) weighs the easy philosophy of manners against the accurate and abstruse kind; 1.12–17 state the programme. EHU 2 (2.1–9) gives impressions and ideas, the copy principle, the missing shade (2.8), and the principle as a test of meaning (2.9). EHU 3 (3.1–3) names the principles of association. Then the *Treatise* versions, about two hours: the Introduction (T Intro 1–10) on the science of man; T 1.1.1 (1–12), with the missing shade at T 1.1.1.10; T 1.1.2–3, short, on impressions of reflection and on memory and imagination; T 1.1.4 (1–7) on association; T 1.1.5–6, which you may skim; and T 1.1.7 (1–18) on abstract ideas, where Hume sides with Berkeley against Locke. Read T 1.1.7 twice.

**The guide.** Its Section I–III entries; its **checkpoints 1–3** in Session C; its misreading 2.

**Alongside.** [01-life-and-times.md](01-life-and-times.md), from "Edinburgh and Ninewells" through "The *Treatise* and the *Abstract*, 1737–1740," and "*My Own Life* as a source"; [02-works-and-texts.md](02-works-and-texts.md), its citation note and the entries for the *Treatise* and the first *Enquiry*; [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md), "A science of man" through "Abstract ideas," with tension (4); [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 1; [05-conversations.md](05-conversations.md), "Locke," "Berkeley on abstract ideas" and "Newton's method." The entries on [Locke](../../eras/03-early-modern/john-locke.md) and [Berkeley](../../eras/03-early-modern/george-berkeley.md) as a refresher. In Session B, the IEP's ["Hume, Imagination"](https://iep.utm.edu/hume-ima/) if T 1.1.3 and 1.1.7 stall you.

**Media.** *Review:* Peter Millican, [*General Philosophy*](https://podcasts.ox.ac.uk/series/general-philosophy), part [2.6, "David Hume"](https://podcasts.ox.ac.uk/26-david-hume) (13 minutes), after the reading. *Optional, not counted:* *In Our Time*, ["The Enlightenment in Scotland"](https://www.bbc.co.uk/programmes/p00548ln) (5 December 2002, 45 minutes).

**Checkpoints (the dossier's own).**
1. *What separates an impression from an idea?* Force and liveliness: feeling heat is livelier than recalling it (EHU 2.1–3; T 1.1.1.1). Impressions of reflection, the passions, arise from ideas, as an idea of pain produces fear (T 1.1.2.1). Then say why a vivid daydream troubles the criterion. [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md), "Perceptions."
2. *State the copy principle, its two supports, the missing shade, and the use Hume makes of the principle.* Every simple idea is copied from a resembling simple impression (T 1.1.1.7; EHU 2.5). Supports: any idea, even of God, analyses into copied parts; a person lacking a sense lacks its ideas (EHU 2.6–7). The missing shade can be supplied from the gradation (EHU 2.8), and Hume calls it too singular to matter. Then the test: ask from what impression a suspect term is derived (EHU 2.9). Objection: a generalisation with an admitted exception cannot prove a disputed idea empty. [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 1.
3. *Name the principles of association and the work they do.* Resemblance, contiguity, cause and effect (EHU 3.3; T 1.1.4.1). They explain why the imagination's combinations follow regular paths, a kind of attraction in the mental world (T 1.1.4.6). Causation carries Weeks 2–4.
4. *What does Hume take from Berkeley on abstract ideas, and what does he add?* That a general idea is a particular one annexed to a general term (T 1.1.7.1). Hume adds the mechanism: custom ties the word to a set of resembling ideas, any of which the mind can call up when the present one misleads (T 1.1.7.7–8). Locke's abstract triangle is the target. [05-conversations.md](05-conversations.md), "Berkeley on abstract ideas."

**Reconstructions.** The copy principle and the missing shade (Argument 1), in ten numbered lines, ending with the test of EHU 2.9.

**Cards.** The first 5 cards of the dossier's [deck](cards.tsv): the argument card for Argument 1 and concept cards on impressions and ideas, the copy principle, the association of ideas, and abstract ideas. Import after the week's reading, never before. If you use the library's shipped decks in [`study/cards/`](../../study/cards/README.md), suspend any Hume card these duplicate. Deck at 5.

## Week 2 — Induction and belief: EHU 4–5, *Treatise* 1.3.6–8, the *Abstract* (~6.4 hrs: 4.5 desk + 1.0 media + 0.9 cards)

**Primary.** EHU 4 (4.1–23), the most important section of the plan, read twice. Part 1 (4.1–13): the fork (4.1–2), all reasoning about matters of fact rests on cause and effect (4.4), causes are known only from experience, as Adam could not infer from water's fluidity and transparency that it would suffocate him (4.6). Part 2 (4.14–23): neither demonstration (4.18) nor probable reasoning (4.19) can found the step from past to future. EHU 5 (5.1–22): custom as the sceptical solution (5.1–9), then belief as a feeling and the harmony between our habits and nature (5.10–22). Then T 1.3.6 (1–16), the *Treatise* version; T 1.3.7 (1–8) on belief as a lively idea; T 1.3.8 (1–17) on how impressions enliven related ideas. Finally the *Abstract* 8–27, Hume's own 1740 summary with the billiard balls, about twenty minutes. *Optional:* T 1.3.1 (1–7), the seven philosophical relations behind the fork.

**The guide.** Its Section IV–V entries; its reconstruction **(a)**, written book-closed before you open it; its **checkpoints 4–7**; its misreadings 1 and 3.

**Alongside.** [02-works-and-texts.md](02-works-and-texts.md), "The *Abstract* (1740)"; [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md), "Relations and Hume's fork," "Causation and induction" and "Custom and belief"; [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Arguments 2–4; [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md), live debate (2). In Session B, the SEP's ["The Problem of Induction"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/induction-problem/), and the IEP's ["Hume, David: Causation"](https://iep.utm.edu/hume-cau/), which also serves Week 3.

**Media.** *Review:* Millican, [3.1, "Hume's Argument Concerning Induction"](https://podcasts.ox.ac.uk/31-humes-argument-concerning-induction) and [3.2, "Responses to Hume's Famous Argument"](https://podcasts.ox.ac.uk/32-responses-humes-famous-argument) (13 and 10 minutes), after your reconstruction of Argument 3. [*Philosophize This!*](https://www.philosophizethis.org/), ep. 51, ["David Hume Pt. 1"](https://www.philosophizethis.org/podcast/david-hume-7waml) (about 35 minutes, an estimate), at the end of the week; its is/ought segment returns in Week 7.

**Checkpoints (the dossier's own).**
1. *State the fork, its test, and the standard objection.* Relations of ideas are known by thought alone and their denial is a contradiction; matters of fact are known by experience and their contrary is always conceivable (EHU 4.1–2). The *Treatise* gets the same line by sorting its seven relations into those that depend only on the ideas compared and those that do not (T 1.3.1.1–2). Objection: the fork is neither kind, so by EHU 12.34 it should go to the flames. Reply: it is a finding of the science of man, on the matter-of-fact side. [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 2.
2. *Run the problem of induction, and give Strawson's and Popper's replies with the answer to each.* Unobserved matters of fact are inferred by cause and effect (4.4); causal knowledge is from experience (4.6–7); the inference needs the principle that the future resembles the past; demonstration cannot prove it (4.18), and probable reasoning cannot without circularity (4.19). Strawson: asking whether induction is reasonable is like asking whether the law is legal. Answer: we can still ask whether inductive beliefs tend to be true. Popper: science needs only deduction. Answer: trusting a well-tested theory tomorrow is inductive. This is the path's Stage 3 checkpoint "if you can only pass one"; answer it aloud. [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 3.
3. *What is the sceptical solution, and why do critics say it solves nothing?* Custom, a principle of human nature, carries the mind from an impression to its usual attendant after repeated conjunction (EHU 5.5–6), and nature has fitted our habits to the world (EHU 5.21–22). Critics: this says why we infer, not whether we should. Hume's position: Section 4 showed that the justification demanded cannot be had. [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 4; [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md), live debate (2).
4. *What is belief, and what is Reid's objection?* An idea conceived in a more lively, firm and steady manner, enlivened by a related present impression (EHU 5.10–12; T 1.3.7.5–7); the same story read as romance and as history gives the same ideas, conceived differently (T 1.3.7.8). Reid: a gripping novel is conceived vividly and not believed. Then give Hume's shift from brightness to firmness and steadiness. [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 4.

**Reconstructions.** The problem of induction (Argument 3; guide (a) is the check, not the source), before Millican 3.1, and custom and belief (Argument 4). The fork (Argument 2) is carried by its card. Argument 1 gets its +2-day and +1-week touches.

**Cards.** +6: the argument cards for Arguments 2, 3 and 4, and concept cards on relations of ideas and matters of fact, on custom, and on belief as force and vivacity. Suspend the shipped core card on Hume's induction problem if you imported it. Deck at 11.

## Week 3 — Necessity and freedom: EHU 6–8, *Treatise* 1.3.14 and 2.3.1–2 (~5.7 hrs: 4.5 desk + 0.3 media + 0.9 cards)

**Primary.** EHU 6 (6.1–4), short: probability of chances, with the die marked one way on four sides (6.2), and probability of causes (6.4). EHU 7 (7.1–30). Part 1 (7.1–25) hunts for the impression of power in one body acting on another (7.6–8), in the will moving the body and commanding ideas (7.9–20), and in the occasionalists' God (7.21–25). Part 2 (7.26–30) finds it in the mind's customary transition and gives the two definitions of cause (7.29). EHU 8 (8.1–36): Part 1 (8.1–26) argues that everyone believes in necessity, meaning constant conjunction and inference, and defines liberty (8.23); Part 2 (8.27–36) argues that morality needs necessity and meets the charge that God becomes the author of sin (8.32–36). Then T 1.3.14 (1–36), longer and blunter, placing necessity in the mind (T 1.3.14.22) with the two definitions at T 1.3.14.31; and T 2.3.1–2 (1–18, 1–8), necessity in human action, with the liberty of spontaneity set against the liberty of indifference (T 2.3.2.1).

**The guide.** Its Section VI–VIII entries; its reconstruction **(b)**, written book-closed before checking; its **checkpoints 8–10**; its misreading 7.

**Alongside.** [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md), "Probability," "Necessary connexion and the two definitions" and "Will and liberty," with tensions (2) and (6); [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Arguments 5 and 6; [05-conversations.md](05-conversations.md), "Malebranche and the Cartesians on causation" and "Hobbes"; [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md), live debate (1). The entry on [Malebranche](../../eras/03-early-modern/nicolas-malebranche.md). In Session B, the SEP's ["Hume on Free Will"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/hume-freewill/) and ["Kant and Hume on Causality"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/kant-hume-causality/); keep notes on the second for the gate essay.

**Media.** *Review:* Millican, [7.3, "Hume on Liberty and Necessity"](https://podcasts.ox.ac.uk/73-hume-liberty-and-necessity) and [7.4, "Making Sense of Free Will and Moral Responsibility"](https://podcasts.ox.ac.uk/74-making-sense-free-will-and-moral-responsibility) (10 minutes each), after your reconstruction of Argument 6. *Optional, not counted:* *In Our Time*, ["Free Will"](https://www.bbc.co.uk/programmes/b00z5y9z) (10 March 2011, 45 minutes).

**Checkpoints (the dossier's own).**
1. *How does custom explain the two kinds of probability?* With a die marked one way on four faces and another on two, the mind runs over the faces and the larger number enlivens one idea more (EHU 6.2). Where experience is mixed, as with rhubarb that usually purges, belief follows the proportion of past cases (EHU 6.4). Both are custom dividing its force.
2. *Where does Hume look for the impression of power, why does each place fail, and where does he find it?* Not in a collision, where we see only motion after motion (EHU 7.6–8); not in the will, whose power we know only by experience and whose working is hidden (EHU 7.9–20); not in God, which takes us into fairy land and gives no idea of power either (EHU 7.21–25). It appears after repeated instances, as a felt transition of the mind (EHU 7.28). Then give Reid's day-and-night counter-example and the reply from Hume's rules for judging causes (T 1.3.15). [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 5.
3. *State the New Hume reading, its proponents and opponents, and what both sides grant.* Wright, Craig and Galen Strawson: Hume believed in real powers and denied only that we perceive them, citing the secret powers of EHU 4.16. Winkler, Blackburn and Millican: Hume says we have no idea of a connexion beyond the two definitions (EHU 7.29), so by the copy principle he could not consistently believe in one. Both grant that our idea of necessity comes from a feeling. The dispute is open. [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md), live debate (1).
4. *What is the reconciling project, and what do Reid and Kant object?* Necessity is constant conjunction and inference, found in human action as in nature (EHU 8.4–20); liberty is a power of acting according to the will, which only a prisoner in chains lacks (EHU 8.23); an act not caused by character would be chance and make the agent less responsible (EHU 8.25, 8.29). Reid and Kant: this leaves open whether one could have willed otherwise. Hume leaves the author-of-sin problem unanswered (EHU 8.36). [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 6.

**Reconstructions.** Necessary connexion and the two definitions (Argument 5; guide (b) is the check) and liberty and necessity (Argument 6), before Millican 7.3. Arguments 3 and 4 get their +2-day and +1-week touches.

**Cards.** +3: the argument cards for Arguments 5 and 6, and the concept card on the two definitions of cause. Deck at 14.

## Week 4 — Scepticism, body and self: EHU 12, *Treatise* 1.4, the Appendix (~7.2 hrs: 5 desk + 1.3 media + 0.9 cards)

**Primary.** EHU 12 (12.1–34) first. Part 1 (12.1–16): Cartesian antecedent doubt set aside (12.3–4), the senses' images taken for objects by instinct (12.7–8) and shown to be images by the slightest philosophy (12.9), experience silent on their cause (12.12), and Descartes' appeal to God's veracity dismissed (12.13). Part 2 (12.17–23): nature defeats Pyrrhonism in action (12.21–23). Part 3 (12.24–34): mitigated scepticism, ending with the books committed to the flames (12.34). Then the *Treatise*, the hardest reading of the plan, about three hours. T 1.4.1 (1–12): corrections for possible error would reduce all evidence to nothing (T 1.4.1.6), yet we believe, since belief is an act of the sensitive part of our nature (T 1.4.1.8). T 1.4.2 (1–57), belief in body: read 1–14 and 45–57 closely and the middle, on constancy, coherence and feigned continued existence, at a steady pace. T 1.4.6 (1–23), personal identity, with the bundle at T 1.4.6.4. T 1.4.7 (1–15), from despair to backgammon (T 1.4.7.9) and back to philosophy (T 1.4.7.12). Last, the Appendix 10–21, Hume's second thoughts on the self.

**The guide.** Its Section XII entry; its **checkpoint 14**; its misreadings 5 and 6.

Use live debate (2) of [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md) to say whether the argument is sceptical or descriptive, and say whether Goodman's grue makes the problem worse. Leave a marked space for Kant's reply.
2. **Does the argument on miracles depend on the theory of causation?** Reconstruct the two definitions of cause (Argument 5) and the maxim (Argument 10). If a law of nature is a constant conjunction, what is a violation of one, and does EHU 10.12's uniform experience beg the question, as Earman charges? Defend a reading of the maxim against Campbell or Earman, and say whether the New Hume reading changes your answer.
3. **Who speaks for Hume in the *Dialogues*?** Reconstruct Arguments 11 and 12. Read Part 12 against EHU 11 and NHR: is Philo's reversal attenuated deism (Gaskin), an ironic retreat (Paul Russell), or EHU 12's scepticism applied to religion? Use live debates (5) and (6), and decide.
4. **Sceptic or naturalist?** Kemp Smith argued that feeling governs reason across the *Treatise*. Test the claim against T 1.4.2, T 1.4.7 and EHU 12 (Arguments 7 and 9), the Appendix (Argument 8), and T 2.3.3 (Argument 13). Do Weeks 7–8 show where Hume was going all along, or does Book 1's crisis stay unresolved?
5. **What Kant will have to answer.** Lay out three Humean claims Kant attacks: that causal necessity is a feeling projected onto objects (Argument 5), that no existence can be demonstrated, so natural theology rests on analogy (Argument 11 and DNR 9), and that reason alone cannot move the will (Arguments 13 and 14). Give Hume's best case for each and an objection from Reid or Price, and say which claim is most exposed. If you run the [Kant dossier](../immanuel-kant/README.md), keep this one for then: it prepares the stage's last checkpoint, with Descartes and Hume as the foils Kant answers.

## Mastery checklist — you are done when you can…

- □ State Hume's problem of induction and one reply (Kant's, Popper's, or a Humean naturalist reply). *(The path's Stage 3 exit checkpoint, verbatim; the path calls it the center of the stage.)*
- □ State Hume on causation as constant conjunction, and on miracles (the maxim, quoted or closely paraphrased). *(The path's Stage 3 exit checkpoint, verbatim.)*
- □ State the copy principle with its two supports and the missing shade, and say how Hume uses it as a test of meaning.
- □ Draw the fork, apply it to *the sun will rise tomorrow*, and give the self-refutation objection with its reply.
- □ Explain custom and belief as the sceptical solution, and what Reid's novel reader shows against the account of belief.
- □ Trace the search for the impression of power, give both definitions of cause, and set out the New Hume debate with its main names.
- □ State the reconciling project of EHU 8 and the objection Reid and Kant press against it.
- □ Explain why neither the senses nor reason produce the belief in body, state the bundle theory, and say what Hume confessed in the Appendix, with two readings.
- □ Distinguish Pyrrhonian from mitigated scepticism, and say how T 1.4.7 gets from despair back to philosophy.
- □ Run Cleanthes' design argument, Philo's objections, the problem of evil and Philo's reversal, with two readings of Part 12.
