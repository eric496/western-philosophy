# Study Plan — Hume in Seven Weeks

## What this plan is, and where it plugs in

The [learning path](../../learning-path.md) meets Hume in **Stage 3 (Early Modern)**, as a core figure (●) at row 10 — the stage's hinge: Locke and Berkeley behind him, Kant ahead of him and unintelligible without him. The path's Hume block runs one week at the stage's rhythm: the *Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding* **whole**, the *Dialogues* in selections, the second *Enquiry* in selections, and *Treatise* I.iv selections last, in that order — because Hume himself superseded the *Treatise* with the *Enquiries*, and starting with the *Treatise* is the classic error his own Advertisement warns against. This dossier keeps the path's order and its editions, and expands the block to seven weeks: both *Enquiries* read whole, the *Dialogues* read whole with the library's debate map in hand, the *Treatise* selections widened from I.iv to the three books' load-bearing sections, and a closing week for the reception debates and the capstone.

Run it as one block in place of Stage 3's Hume week, after Berkeley (row 8) and before the political-philosophy run that leads to Kant (row 15). The sequence around it is load-bearing in both directions: Hume's audit only lands if the way of ideas is fresh from Locke and Berkeley, and Kant's *Prolegomena* — which names Hume's causation challenge as the interruption of his dogmatic slumber — reads as a reply only if Hume came first.

**Total cost, stated honestly: ≈39 hours of scheduled study across seven weeks (~5.5 hrs/week), plus the daily [Anki](https://apps.ankiweb.net) [FSRS](https://docs.ankiweb.net/deck-options.html) queue (~10 min/day ≈ 6 hrs across the plan) — ≈45 hours all-in.** And the accounting against the path, stated plainly: **these hours sit on top of the learning path's Stage 3 budget, they are not hidden inside it.** The path already spends roughly one of Stage 3's weeks on Hume; this dossier replaces that pass with seven full weeks, so count a net addition of roughly 38 hours to Stage 3 if you run the dossier as written.

**Reading texts.** Follow the path's rulings exactly. *Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding*: Beauchamp (Oxford World's Classics) or Steinberg (Hackett) — **not** Selby-Bigge/Nidditch (superseded; the path's ruling). The library's [study guide to the *Enquiry*](../../study/hume-enquiry.md) is the assigned companion for Weeks 1–2 and is not duplicated here. *Enquiry Concerning the Principles of Morals*: Beauchamp (Oxford). *Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion*: Coleman (Cambridge Texts in the History of Philosophy) — manuscript-based; cite by Part. *Treatise*: Norton & Norton (Oxford) for the selections; cite by book.part.section (T 1.3.6), with Selby-Bigge/Nidditch page numbers (SBN) where a secondary source needs one. The *Essays* in Miller's Liberty Fund edition for the Week 6 selections.

**The guide is your companion, by design.** Weeks 1–2 assign the Enquiry guide's section walkthroughs and checkpoint sets alongside the primary text; the checkpoints printed below are the dossier's own additions, aimed at what the guide leaves to [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md) and [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md).

## Week 1 — The science of man, applied: *Enquiry* §§1–7 (~5.5 hrs)

**Primary.** The first *Enquiry* through the causation sections, with the guide section-by-section: §1 (the two species of moral philosophy), §2 (impressions and ideas; the missing shade), §3 (association), §4 (the induction problem — the core of the dossier), §5 (the skeptical solution: custom), §6 (probability), §7 (necessary connection, the two definitions). This is the path's core and the dossier's foundation; read slowly.

**Alongside.** [01-life-and-times.md](01-life-and-times.md) (why the *Enquiry* is a recasting of a failed book, and what rode on it), [02-works-and-texts.md](02-works-and-texts.md), and [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md) through the causation section.

**Checkpoints (book closed).**
1. *State the copy principle and its one admitted exception.* Every simple idea copies a preceding impression (§2); the missing shade of blue is Hume's own counterexample, raised and dismissed by Hume himself — say why the dismissal is (or is not) legitimate. [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 1.
2. *State the problem of induction as a dilemma.* Any justification of the uniformity supposition is either demonstrative (impossible — the contrary is conceivable) or probable (circular — it presupposes uniformity). §4; Argument 2.
3. *What are Hume's two definitions of "cause," and why are there two?* Constant conjunction of resembling objects, and the determination of the mind to infer — one from the objects, one from the inference; whether they name one thing or two is a live dispute (the New Hume debate). §7; Argument 3.

**Reconstructions.** The problem of induction, first blank-page reconstruction (200–400 words); enters the +2d/+1wk/+3wk rhythm (revisit in Weeks 1, 2, and 4's retrieval sessions).

**Cards.** First 6 of [cards.tsv](cards.tsv) (copy principle; impressions vs. ideas; association; Hume's fork; induction; custom).

## Week 2 — Liberty, miracles, skepticism: *Enquiry* §§8–12 (~5.5 hrs)

**Primary.** §8 (liberty and necessity — the compatibilist settlement), §9 (the reason of animals), §10 (miracles), §11 (particular providence — the *Dialogues* in embryo), §12 (the Academic philosophy: mitigated skepticism as the resting point). Guide alongside throughout.

**Alongside.** [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Arguments 4 (compatibilism), 5 (miracles), and 13 (mitigated skepticism).

**Checkpoints (book closed).**
1. *Why does Hume think the liberty–necessity dispute is merely verbal?* Because "liberty" as usually felt (acting as one wills) and "necessity" as observed in human action (uniformity + inference) are compatible, and the felt incompatibility rests on confusing liberty with its opposite — indifference. §8; Argument 4.
2. *State the miracles argument in one sentence, then its strongest modern objection.* No testimony suffices to establish a miracle unless its falsehood would be more miraculous than the fact (§10); Earman's Bayesian charge — that the maxim confuses prior improbability with an a priori bar — is the objection to beat; Fogelin's evidential reading is the defense. Argument 5.
3. *What does mitigated skepticism permit that Pyrrhonism does not?* Common life: belief governed by custom, inquiry confined to subjects proportioned to our faculties (§12). Argument 13.

**Reconstructions.** Miracles, first reconstruction; induction revisited (+1wk).

**Cards.** Next 6 (belief as vivacity; necessary connection; compatibilism; miracles maxim; mitigated skepticism; relations of ideas vs. matters of fact).

## Week 3 — The moral *Enquiry* whole: *Principles of Morals* (~5.5 hrs)

**Primary.** The second *Enquiry* whole — the path reads it in selections; the dossier reads all nine sections and Appendices 1–3, because this is the book Hume called "incomparably the best" of his writings (*My Own Life* — his judgment, quoted as his). Watch the machinery change from the *Treatise*: sympathy broadens into a general principle of humanity, and the argument runs on utility and agreeableness rather than on the associationist engine.

**Alongside.** [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md) (sympathy, the moral sense, artificial virtues), [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md) Arguments 11 and 12 in their EPM dress.

**Checkpoints (book closed).**
1. *How does the EPM's moral machinery differ from the Treatise's?* Name the two main differences (sympathy's role; the fourfold source scheme — useful or agreeable, to self or others) and one thing that does not change (moral distinctions founded on sentiment, not reason alone — Appendix 1).
2. *What work does the "general point of view" do?* It corrects sympathy's partiality: we judge character from the standpoint of those affected, steadily and in common, so that moral language can be shared. Argument 11.
3. *Why is justice artificial in Hume, and why is that not a criticism of it?* Its rules arise from convention under scarcity and limited generosity, not from a promise or a natural instinct — and their artificiality is what makes them exceptionless. Argument 12.

**Reconstructions.** Justice as convention, first reconstruction.

**Cards.** Next 6 (sympathy; general point of view; moral sense; artificial virtue; convention; is–ought — imported early, cashed out in Week 6).

## Week 4 — Natural religion on trial: the *Dialogues* whole (~5.5 hrs)

**Primary.** *Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion*, Parts I–XII whole, in Coleman's edition. Read it as drama first — who concedes what, when — and only then as doctrine. The speaker problem is constitutive, not a puzzle with an answer key: Pamphilus awards the palm to Cleanthes in the closing paragraph, while the strongest arguments belong to Philo; the dossier presents the distribution debate (Kemp Smith's Philo thesis and its critics) as a dispute, and so should you.

**Alongside.** [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md) Arguments 6 (design) and 7 (evil); [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md) §(d).

**Checkpoints (book closed).**
1. *Reconstruct Philo's attack on the design analogy (Part II) in three premises.* The universe is a unique case; analogical inference weakens with dissimilarity; the effect licenses no attributes beyond what the effect requires.
2. *What are the four hypotheses of Part XI, and what do they show?* The cause(s) of the world may be perfectly good, perfectly evil, both, or neither; the phenomena (mixed good and evil, indifferently distributed) fit "neither" — the argument targets inference to moral attributes, not existence as such. Argument 7.
3. *What, on the most defensible reading, survives Philo's skepticism in Part XII?* A thin, ambiguous proposition — that the cause of order probably bears some remote analogy to human intelligence — stated by Philo himself; whether it is irony, concession, or sincerity is the debate.

**Reconstructions.** The design argument and Philo's reply, first reconstruction; induction revisited (+3wk — the rhythm completes).

**Cards.** Next 5 (design analogy; problem of evil; Philo/Cleanthes distribution; the two definitions of cause; bundle theory — imported early, cashed out in Week 5).

## Week 5 — The *Treatise*, Book I: the skeptical engine room (~5.5 hrs)

**Primary.** The *Treatise* selections, in the dossier's order: 1.1.1 (impressions and ideas in their native form), 1.3.6 (induction again — compare the *Enquiry*'s version line by line; the dossier treats the differences as data), 1.3.14 (necessary connection, the two definitions in full dress), 1.4.1 (the skeptical reductio of reason itself), 1.4.6 (personal identity), 1.4.7 (the conclusion: melancholy, backgammon, and the discipline of nature), and the *Appendix* (the confession on personal identity — the two principles he can neither renounce nor reconcile).

**Alongside.** [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md) Arguments 3 and 8; [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md) on the bundle and the Appendix fault-line.

**Checkpoints (book closed).**
1. *What does 1.4.7 add that the Enquiry never says as nakedly?* The skeptical crisis as lived: reason's self-subversion produces not doctrine but melancholy, cured by nature — "I dine, I play a game of back-gammon" — with philosophy resumed afterward on nature's terms, not reason's. Argument 13.
2. *State the Appendix confession exactly.* All perceptions are distinct existences; the mind never perceives any real connection among distinct existences — and the bundle theory needs a connection it cannot supply. Argument 8.
3. *T 1.3.6 vs. E 4: one difference that matters.* (Open by design; the *Treatise*'s version embeds the dilemma inside a theory of belief the *Enquiry* only sketches — defend your candidate difference from both texts.)

**Reconstructions.** The bundle theory, first reconstruction.

**Cards.** Next 5 (impression of reflection; vivacity revisited as belief's manner; the Appendix confession; skeptical vs. naturalist reading; New Hume).

## Week 6 — The *Treatise*, Books II–III, and the essayist (~5.5 hrs)

**Primary.** T 2.3.1–2 (necessity in the passions), 2.3.3 ("Reason is, and ought only to be the slave of the passions" — read the whole section, not the slogan), 3.1.1 (moral distinctions not derived from reason; the is–ought paragraph at its end, 3.1.1.27), 3.2.2 (justice's origin in convention — the rowing-boat passage), 3.3.1 (the general point of view). Then the essayist: "Of the Standard of Taste" (1757) — Hume's aesthetics in one essay, and a test of whether the general-point-of-view machinery travels.

**Alongside.** [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md) Arguments 9, 10, 11, 12 in their *Treatise* dress; [05-conversations.md](05-conversations.md) whole.

**Checkpoints (book closed).**
1. *State the slave-of-the-passions argument without the slogan.* Reason alone can never oppose a passion in directing the will (it discovers relations and facts; only a contrary passion checks a passion) — "reason is… the slave" is the conclusion, not the premise. Argument 9.
2. *What does the is–ought paragraph demand, minimally?* That the transition from *is* to *ought* — "an important distinction," expressing "a new relation or affirmation" — be observed and explained; whether it states an unbridgeable law (the categorical reading) or a narrower anti-rationalist point is the dispute. Argument 10.
3. *Why is there no promise at the origin of Hume's justice?* Because a promise itself presupposes the convention it would be needed to found; convention (like the rowing boat) precedes contract. Argument 12.

**Reconstructions.** Is–ought, first reconstruction; justice revisited (+1wk).

**Cards.** Next 4 (slave of the passions; sensible knave; Hume on taste's standard; the 1777 Advertisement).

## Week 7 — The debates, and the capstone (~6 hrs)

**Primary.** [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md) whole — the reception eras, the five live debates (skeptic vs. naturalist; New Hume; is–ought; the *Dialogues* speaker; the "Of National Characters" footnote's record and the modern dispute, stated there in full) — and [08-bibliography.md](08-bibliography.md) selections: pick the two secondary works you will read after the dossier (the plan's recommendation: Stroud's *Hume* now, Baier next).

**The gate-essay relationship.** Stage 3's gate essay asks: *"What, if anything, justifies our expectation that the future will resemble the past?"* — Hume's problem, with at least two replies, one of them Kant's. This dossier's capstone is that essay's Hume half, written to gate standard (the replies, Kant's included, belong to the path's Kant block and the stage gate itself — do not write Kant's half early, on a Hume-only evidence base).

**Capstone essay (800–1,200 words).** Choose one:
1. *Hume's fork, applied to Hume.* The Enquiry's closing test consigns volumes containing neither abstract reasoning nor matter of fact to the flames. Apply the fork to Hume's own science of man: which side of it does the copy principle fall on, and does the system survive its own audit?
2. *Two books, one philosopher?* The 1777 Advertisement disowns the *Treatise*; the dossier reads it anyway. Defend or attack the scholarly practice of overriding an author's own verdict on his work — with the *Treatise*/*Enquiry* differences as your evidence.
3. *The architecture of the resting point.* Mitigated skepticism is either a stable philosophical position or skepticism's surrender to habit, depending on the reading. Take one side, against the strongest version of the other (Kemp Smith's naturalism and Stroud's skepticism are your two poles).
4. *Philo's concession.* Part XII's thin theism: irony, prudence, or belief? Argue from the distribution of arguments across all twelve Parts, and say what your reading does to the *Dialogues* as a whole.

**Mastery checklist.** You are done when you can:
- State the copy principle, raise the missing shade against it, and assess Hume's dismissal — in one breath.
- Reconstruct the problem of induction as a dilemma, and name why each horn blocks its escape.
- Give both definitions of "cause" and explain what the New Hume debate is *about*, without settling it.
- Explain Hume's compatibilism and why he thought the traditional dispute was verbal.
- State the miracles maxim, and run the Earman–Fogelin dispute over it accurately from both sides.
- Reconstruct Philo's design critique and his four hypotheses on evil, flagging the speaker problem as you go.
- State the bundle theory, then quote the Appendix's two irreconcilable principles.
- Derive "slave of the passions" from its argument rather than reciting the slogan.
- Present is–ought as a dispute among three readings, with the paragraph's own words as the evidence.
- Explain justice as convention without contract — and answer the sensible knave as Hume did, partial concession included.
- Say what mitigated skepticism licenses and forbids, and why backgammon is a philosophical move in T 1.4.7.
- Place Hume in the reply chain in both directions: Locke and Berkeley behind him; Reid, Kant, and the positivists ahead — with the specific point of contact in each case.
