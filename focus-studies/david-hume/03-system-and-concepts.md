*Focus study · [Dossier home](README.md) · Library entry: [David Hume](../../eras/03-early-modern/david-hume.md) · Arguments in full: [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md) · Study guide: [*An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding*](../../study/hume-enquiry.md)*

## A science of man

Hume's system is one project: to explain how human beings think, feel and act by the methods that had worked in natural philosophy. The title page of *A Treatise of Human Nature* (Books 1 and 2, January 1739; Book 3 with the Appendix, November 1740) calls it "An Attempt to introduce the experimental Method of Reasoning into Moral Subjects." *Moral* means everything human: belief, passion, conduct, society. Book 1 treats the understanding, Book 2 the passions, Book 3 morals. The first *Enquiry* (1748, titled *Philosophical Essays concerning Human Understanding* until 1758) recast Book 1, the second *Enquiry* (1751) Book 3, and the dissertation "Of the Passions" (1757) Book 2.

The Introduction states the plan. Every science depends on human nature, "Even Mathematics, Natural Philosophy, and Natural Religion," since they "lie under the cognizance of men, and are judged of by their powers and faculties" (T Intro 4). Instead of "taking now and then a castle or village on the frontier," philosophy should march "directly to the capital or center of these sciences, to human nature itself" (T Intro 6). And "as the science of man is the only solid foundation for the other sciences, so the only solid foundation we can give to this science itself must be laid on experience and observation" (T Intro 7). Like Newton, Hume looks for a few general principles and declines to explain them further: "None of them can go beyond experience, or establish any principles which are not founded on that authority" (T Intro 10).

The *Enquiries* are not abridgements. They drop space and time, most of the external world, personal identity and the indirect passions, and they add miracles and providence. In an Advertisement written in 1775 for the 1777 edition of his *Essays and Treatises*, Hume disowned the *Treatise* and asked to be judged by the later works. The [learning path](../../learning-path.md) follows him by reading the first *Enquiry* first. This file takes each doctrine from the text that states it best and names that text.

**Texts and quotation.** Quotations are Hume's own words, from Peter Millican and Amyas Merivale's *Hume Texts Online* (davidhume.org), in the original spelling ("'tis," "shou'd," "connexion"). References are to Clarendon paragraph numbers: T for the *Treatise* (book, part, section, paragraph), with T Intro and T App. for its Introduction and Appendix; EHU and EPM for the two *Enquiries*; DNR for the *Dialogues* (part and paragraph); NHR for the *Natural History of Religion*; the *Abstract* and essays by paragraph.

## Perceptions

Hume's word for any content of the mind is "perception." "All the perceptions of the human mind resolve themselves into two distinct kinds, which I shall call Impressions and Ideas. The difference betwixt these consists in the degrees of force and liveliness, with which they strike upon the mind" (T 1.1.1.1). Impressions are "all our sensations, passions and emotions, as they make their first appearance in the soul"; ideas are "the faint images of these in thinking and reasoning." The *Enquiry* calls ideas "the less forcible and lively" kind (EHU 2.3).

Perceptions are **simple** or **complex**. The idea of red is simple; the idea of an apple combines colour, taste and shape. Impressions are of **sensation** or of **reflexion**. Sensations arise "in the soul originally, from unknown causes." Impressions of reflexion, the passions and desires, arise "in a great measure from our ideas" (T 1.1.2.1): pain is felt, its idea returns, and the idea produces fear. Ideas of **memory** keep the order and some of the force of their impressions; ideas of **imagination** are free to separate and recombine (T 1.1.3).

Hume says in a note that he restores the word idea "to its original sense, from which Mr. Locke had perverted it" (T 1.1.1.1 n. 2), since Locke used it for every object of thought. Sorting perceptions by vivacity makes the difference between feeling and thinking a matter of degree. See the library entry on [Locke](../../eras/03-early-modern/john-locke.md).

## The copy principle and the missing shade

The first principle of the system is "That all our simple ideas in their first appearance are deriv'd from simple impressions, which are correspondent to them, and which they exactly represent" (T 1.1.1.7). The evidence is experimental. Every simple idea we examine resembles a simple impression, and a man born blind has no ideas of colour (EHU 2.7). Complex ideas need not copy anything: a golden mountain joins two ideas we already have (EHU 2.5).

The principle is a tool for criticism. When a term is suspected of having no meaning, "we need but enquire, from what impression is that supposed idea derived? And if it be impossible to assign any, this will serve to confirm our suspicion" (EHU 2.9). Hume applies the test to substance, necessary connexion and the self.

He also raises a counter-example himself. A man who has known colours for thirty years has never seen one shade of blue. Shown the other shades in order, he notices the gap. "Now I ask, whether 'tis possible for him, from his own imagination, to supply this deficiency, and raise up to himself the idea of that particular shade, tho' it had never been conveyed to him by his senses?" (T 1.1.1.10). Most would say he can. Yet "this instance is so singular, that it is scarcely worth our observing, and does not merit, that for it alone we should alter our general maxim" (EHU 2.8). Argument 1 in [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md) reconstructs both; tension 4 below asks why he let the exception stand.

## Association

If ideas were "entirely loose and unconnected, chance alone wou'd join them" (T 1.1.4.1). Instead the imagination is "guided by some universal principles," working as "a gentle force, which commonly prevails." There are "only three principles of connexion among ideas, namely, Resemblance, Contiguity in time or place, and Cause or Effect" (EHU 3.2). A portrait leads to the person, a room to the next room, a wound to the pain.

Hume compares association to gravity: "a kind of Attraction, which in the mental world will be found to have as extraordinary effects as in the natural" (T 1.1.4.6). Its causes "must be resolv'd into original qualities of human nature, which I pretend not to explain." The *Abstract* names it as the author's main claim to "so glorious a name as that of an inventor" and calls these relations "the cement of the universe" (Abstract 35). The rest of the system leans on it. Belief, the indirect passions and sympathy are all association at work.

## Abstract ideas

Hume adopts Berkeley's account of general ideas, calling it "one of the greatest and most valuable discoveries that has been made of late years in the republic of letters" (T 1.1.7.1). On that account "all general ideas are nothing but particular ones, annexed to a certain term, which gives them a more extensive signification." Locke had supposed the mind could form the idea of a triangle neither equilateral, isosceles nor scalene. Hume denies this, since every idea copies an impression and every impression is fully determinate.

He adds a mechanism. A particular idea becomes general through a custom attached to a word. Hearing the word, we form an idea of one triangle, and the custom stands ready to call up others, so that a false general claim drawn from the one before us is checked (T 1.1.7.7–8). See the library entry on [Berkeley](../../eras/03-early-modern/george-berkeley.md).

## Relations and Hume's fork

The *Treatise* lists "seven different kinds of philosophical relation, viz. resemblance, identity, relations of time and place, proportion in quantity or number, degrees in any quality, contrariety, and causation" (T 1.3.1.1). Only the four that depend wholly on the ideas compared can yield knowledge.

The *Enquiry* simplifies this into what later readers call **Hume's fork**. "All the objects of human reason or enquiry may naturally be divided into two kinds, to wit, Relations of Ideas, and Matters of Fact" (EHU 4.1). The first are known "by the mere operation of thought," like the theorems of geometry. In the second, "the contrary of every matter of fact is still possible" (EHU 4.2): that the sun will not rise tomorrow implies no contradiction. The book ends by applying the fork to libraries: "Commit it then to the flames: For it can contain nothing but sophistry and illusion" (EHU 12.34). Argument 2 sets out the fork. Whether it is the later analytic/synthetic distinction is disputed, since Hume's test is conceivability and his examples are all mathematical.

## Causation and induction

Matters of fact beyond memory and the senses are reached only by cause and effect: "All reasonings concerning matter of fact seem to be founded on the relation of Cause and Effect" (EHU 4.4). Causal knowledge comes from experience. Adam, with perfect faculties, could not have inferred from the transparency of water that it would drown him (EHU 4.6).

Why then do we infer the unobserved from the observed? If reason did it, "it wou'd proceed upon that principle, that instances, of which we have had no experience, must resemble those, of which we have had experience, and that the course of nature continues always uniformly the same" (T 1.3.6.4). No demonstration supports that principle, since "we can at least conceive a change in the course of nature" (T 1.3.6.5). No experimental argument supports it either, since all such arguments "proceed upon the supposition, that the future will be conformable to the past," and to prove it by them "must be evidently going in a circle" (EHU 4.19). This is the **problem of induction**, though the phrase is not Hume's (Argument 3).

The *Treatise* also denies that "whatever begins to exist, must have a cause of existence" is intuitively or demonstratively certain (T 1.3.3.1), since an uncaused beginning is conceivable. In a letter to John Stewart of February 1754 he denied ever holding that anything might arise without a cause; his point was that our certainty has another source.

## Custom and belief

The **sceptical solution** of EHU 5 names the source. "Custom, then, is the great guide of human life. It is that principle alone, which renders our experience useful to us, and makes us expect, for the future, a similar train of events with those which have appeared in the past" (EHU 5.6). The *Abstract* is blunter: "'Tis not, therefore, reason, which is the guide of life, but custom" (Abstract 16). Custom is an original principle of human nature, and Hume does not try to explain it (Argument 4).

Custom produces **belief**. Belief adds no new idea to what is conceived; it changes the manner of conceiving. "An opinion, therefore, or belief may be most accurately defin'd, A lively idea related to or associated with a present impression" (T 1.3.7.5). The *Enquiry* admits the definition is hard and piles up synonyms: "belief is nothing but a more vivid, lively, forcible, firm, steady conception of an object, than what the imagination alone is ever able to attain" (EHU 5.12). The present impression passes its force along the customary relation, as our idea of a friend is enlivened "upon the appearance of the picture of an absent friend" (T 1.3.8.3). The Appendix shifts the emphasis: "belief consists merely in a certain feeling or sentiment," one that does not depend on the will (T App. 2).

## Necessary connexion and the two definitions

The copy principle meets its main test with necessary connexion. Single instances of bodies show nothing: "we never can, by our utmost scrutiny, discover any thing but one event following another" (EHU 7.26). The will's command over the limbs shows no power either, since we feel the volition and see the motion but not what joins them (EHU 7.9–20). "All events seem entirely loose and separate. One event follows another; but we never can observe any tye between them. They seem conjoined, but never connected" (EHU 7.26).

The idea appears only after repetition, and its source is in the mind. "This connexion, therefore, which we feel in the mind, this customary transition of the imagination from one object to its usual attendant, is the sentiment or impression, from which we form the idea of power or necessary connexion" (EHU 7.28). The *Treatise* is sharper: "Upon the whole, necessity is something, that exists in the mind, not in objects" (T 1.3.14.22). We take it for a quality of things because "the mind has a great propensity to spread itself on external objects" (T 1.3.14.25).

Hume then defines cause twice, considering the relation "either as a comparison of two ideas, or as an association betwixt them" (T 1.3.14.31). The first definition gives a regularity: "An object precedent and contiguous to another, and where all the objects resembling the former are plac'd in like relations of precedency and contiguity to those objects, that resemble the latter." The second adds the mind: "A cause is an object precedent and contiguous to another, and so united with it, that the idea of the one determines the mind to form the idea of the other, and the impression of the one to form a more lively idea of the other" (T 1.3.14.31). The *Enquiry* gives the pair as "an object, followed by another, and where all the objects, similar to the first, are followed by objects similar to the second" and "an object followed by another, and whose appearance always conveys the thought to that other" (EHU 7.29). Between them it adds a counterfactual gloss: "Or in other words, where, if the first object had not been, the second never had existed." Hume concedes that both are "drawn from circumstances foreign to the cause" (Argument 5; tension 2).

## Probability

To keep ordinary usage, Hume would "distinguish human reason into three kinds, viz. that from knowledge, from proofs, and from probabilities" (T 1.3.11.2). Knowledge comes from comparing ideas. Proofs are causal arguments from uniform experience, "entirely free from doubt and uncertainty," such as that the sun will rise. Probability proper is reasoning short of that.

Probability of **chances** arises where we cannot tell which of several outcomes will occur. "Though there be no such thing as Chance in the world; our ignorance of the real cause of any event has the same influence on the understanding" (EHU 6.1). Probability of **causes** arises from mixed experience: "Being determined by custom to transfer the past to the future," we weigh contrary instances and believe in proportion (EHU 6.4). The miracles essay rests on this rule: "A wise man, therefore, proportions his belief to the evidence" (EHU 10.4; Argument 10).

T 1.3.13 adds **unphilosophical probability**, the biases of belief, such as the greater force of recent or vivid instances and prejudice "deriv'd from general rules, which we rashly form to ourselves" (T 1.3.13.7). Reflection yields further general rules that correct the first ones (T 1.3.13.12), so the science of belief becomes a guide to judging well.

## The external world

T 1.4.2 begins with a concession. "We may well ask, What causes induce us to believe in the existence of body? but 'tis in vain to ask, Whether there be body or not? That is a point, which we must take for granted in all our reasonings" (T 1.4.2.1). The question is why we believe in bodies that exist continuously and distinctly from us.

The senses cannot produce the belief, since they give only present perceptions. Reason cannot, since ordinary people hold it without argument. It comes from the **imagination**. The constancy and coherence of certain impressions (the desk looks the same each time I return) lead the imagination to feign a continued existence across the gaps (T 1.4.2.18–24). Reflection shows that perceptions depend on our organs, as when pressing one eye doubles what we see, so philosophers posit a double existence, interrupted perceptions and continuous objects. Their system is "the monstrous offspring of two principles, which are contrary to each other" (T 1.4.2.52). Hume ends the section with no defence of either: "Carelessness and in-attention alone can afford us any remedy" (T 1.4.2.57).

The *Enquiry* keeps the outline. People trust their senses "by a natural instinct or prepossession" (EHU 12.7), and the belief is "soon destroyed by the slightest philosophy" (EHU 12.9). Argument 7 sets out both versions.

## The self

"For my part, when I enter most intimately into what I call myself, I always stumble on some particular perception or other, of heat or cold, light or shade, love or hatred, pain or pleasure. I never can catch myself at any time without a perception, and never can observe any thing but the perception" (T 1.4.6.3). With no impression of a simple, unchanging self there is no idea of one. People are "nothing but a bundle or collection of different perceptions, which succeed each other with an inconceivable rapidity, and are in a perpetual flux and movement" (T 1.4.6.4). The mind "is a kind of theatre," though Hume warns that we have no notion of the place where the scenes are played.

We ascribe identity to the bundle because resemblance and causation make the imagination's passage along it smooth, and we mistake the easy passage for the view of one unchanging thing (T 1.4.6.6). Memory supplies resemblances and reveals causal links, which we then extend past what we remember (T 1.4.6.18–20). Book 2 treats the self as the object of pride, a richer self with body, character and possessions. Hume keeps the two questions apart: identity "as it regards our thought or imagination" and "as it regards our passions" (T 1.4.6.5).

The Appendix withdrew his confidence. "Upon a more strict review of the section concerning personal identity, I find myself involv'd in such a labyrinth, that, I must confess, I neither know how to correct my former opinions, nor how to render them consistent" (T App. 10). Tension 1 and Argument 8 take this up. The *Enquiry* drops the subject.

## Scepticism

Book 1 ends in crisis. T 1.4.1 argues that "all knowledge degenerates into probability," since any demonstration may contain an error, and that judging our own reliability again and again would reduce belief to nothing. Nature saves us where argument cannot: "Nature, by an absolute and uncontroulable necessity has determin'd us to judge as well as to breathe and feel" (T 1.4.1.7). The Conclusion (T 1.4.7) finds the imagination, the source of all belief, divided against itself. Hume falls into melancholy and is cured by company. "I dine, I play a game of back-gammon, I converse, and am merry with my friends; and when after three or four hour's amusement, I wou'd return to these speculations, they appear so cold, and strain'd, and ridiculous, that I cannot find in my heart to enter into them any farther" (T 1.4.7.9).

He does return, on terms Don Garrett (*Cognition and Commitment in Hume's Philosophy*, 1997) calls the **title principle**: "Where reason is lively, and mixes itself with some propensity, it ought to be assented to. Where it does not, it never can have any title to operate upon us" (T 1.4.7.11). Reasoning that rides on a natural propensity, such as causal inference about common life, may be followed.

The *Enquiry* frames a choice between two scepticisms. **Pyrrhonian** or *excessive* scepticism cannot be lived: "Nature is always too strong for principle," and "no durable good can ever result from it" (EHU 12.23). **Mitigated** or *academical* scepticism "may be both durable and useful." It is "the result of this Pyrrhonism, or excessive scepticism, when its undistinguished doubts are, in some measure, corrected by common sense and reflection" (EHU 12.24). It teaches modesty and "the limitation of our enquiries to such subjects as are best adapted to the narrow capacity of human understanding" (EHU 12.25). The opening section had said as much: "Be a philosopher; but, amidst all your philosophy, be still a man" (EHU 1.6). Argument 9 sets out the position. See the library entry on [Pyrrho and the Skeptics](../../eras/01-ancient/pyrrho-of-elis-and-the-skeptics.md).

## The passions

The passions are impressions of reflexion, which Hume also calls *secondary* impressions (T 2.1.1.1). They divide into calm and violent and into **direct** and **indirect**. "By direct passions I understand such as arise immediately from good or evil, from pain or pleasure. By indirect such as proceed from the same principles, but by the conjunction of other qualities" (T 2.1.1.4). Desire, aversion, hope, fear, joy and grief are direct. Pride and humility, love and hatred are indirect.

An indirect passion has an **object** and a **cause**. The object of pride and humility is the self, "that identical person, of whose thoughts, actions, and sensations we are intimately conscious" (T 2.2.1.2); the object of love and hatred is another person. The cause is a quality of something related to the object, such as a fine house I own. Pride arises by a **double relation**. The idea of the house is related to the idea of me, and the pleasure the house gives is related to the pleasant feeling of pride. "From this double relation of ideas and impressions, the passion is deriv'd" (T 2.1.5.5). Association now runs between impressions as well as ideas.

## Will and liberty

The will is "nothing but the internal impression we feel and are conscious of, when we knowingly give rise to any new motion of our body, or new perception of our mind" (T 2.3.1.2). Human actions show the same constant conjunction with motives and character as bodies show with their causes, and we infer from them as confidently. A prisoner reckons with the gaoler's obstinacy as he does with the walls (EHU 8.19). Since necessity is constant conjunction plus the mind's inference, actions are necessary in the only sense the word has. Liberty taken as freedom from causes would be the same as chance. Its appeal comes from confusing "the liberty of spontaniety, as it is call'd in the schools, and the liberty of indifference," freedom from violence and freedom from causes (T 2.3.2.1).

The *Enquiry* offers a reconciliation. The dispute "has hitherto turned merely upon words" (EHU 8.3). By liberty "we can only mean a power of acting or not acting, according to the determinations of the will" (EHU 8.23), which everyone has who is not in chains. Necessity is required for morality, since blame presupposes that actions flow from lasting character (EHU 8.29–31). This is the **compatibilist** view (Argument 6). Compare the library entry on [Hobbes](../../eras/03-early-modern/thomas-hobbes.md).

## Reason and motivation

T 2.3.3 sets out to prove "first, that reason alone can never be a motive to any action of the will; and secondly, that it can never oppose passion in the direction of the will." Demonstrative reasoning concerns relations of ideas and moves no one. Probable reasoning shows causes and effects and so tells us how to get what we already want. The impulse comes from the prospect of pleasure or pain. Hence: "Reason is, and ought only to be the slave of the passions, and can never pretend to any other office than to serve and obey them" (T 2.3.3.4). A passion represents nothing, so it cannot be contrary to reason. "'Tis not contrary to reason to prefer the destruction of the whole world to the scratching of my finger" (T 2.3.3.6). A passion is called unreasonable only loosely, when it rests on a false belief or picks the wrong means.

What we call reason in conduct is often a **calm passion**, such as benevolence, felt so faintly that it is mistaken for reason (T 2.3.3.8). The thesis concerns motivation. Reason keeps its authority over belief (Argument 13).

## Moral sense and sentiment

Book 3 asks "whether 'tis by means of our ideas or impressions we distinguish betwixt vice and virtue" (T 3.1.1.3). The main argument uses Book 2: "Morals excite passions, and produce or prevent actions. Reason of itself is utterly impotent in this particular. The rules of morality, therefore, are not conclusions of our reason" (T 3.1.1.6). Examine a wilful murder and you find passions, motives and thoughts, but not the vice, "till you turn your reflection into your own breast." "Here is a matter of fact; but 'tis the object of feeling, not of reason. It lies in yourself, not in the object" (T 3.1.1.26). The section ends with the **is/ought** paragraph. Moral writers move from propositions joined by *is* to propositions "connected with an ought, or an ought not." Since the ought "expresses some new relation or affirmation," a reason should be given for "what seems altogether inconceivable, how this new relation can be a deduction from others, which are entirely different from it" (T 3.1.1.27; Argument 14).

The positive conclusion follows: "Morality, therefore, is more properly felt than judg'd of" (T 3.1.2.1). "To have the sense of virtue, is nothing but to feel a satisfaction of a particular kind from the contemplation of a character" (T 3.1.2.3). Character is the object of evaluation, and actions count as signs of it (T 3.2.1.2). Hume takes the phrase "moral sense" from Francis Hutcheson but explains the feeling by sympathy and utility. The second *Enquiry* gives reason more room, holding that "reason and sentiment concur in almost all moral determinations" (EPM 1.9). Reason "discovers objects as they really stand in nature"; taste "has a productive faculty, and gilding or staining all natural objects with the colours, borrowed from internal sentiment, raises, in a manner, a new creation" (EPM App. 1.21). See the library entry on the [moral sense school](../../eras/03-early-modern/moral-sense-school.md).

## Sympathy and the general point of view

Moral feeling is shared because of **sympathy**. "No quality of human nature is more remarkable, both in itself and in its consequences, than that propensity we have to sympathize with others, and to receive by communication their inclinations and sentiments" (T 2.1.11.2). Sympathy is a mechanism of communication, not pity. I see the signs of another's feeling and form an idea of it, and "This idea is presently converted into an impression, and acquires such a degree of force and vivacity, as to become the very passion itself" (T 2.1.11.3). The idea draws its force from the ever-present impression of myself, through the resemblance and nearness between us. So sympathy is strong toward those close and like us and weak toward strangers.

Moral judgment would then waver with distance. The correction is the **general point of view**: "In order, therefore, to prevent those continual contradictions, and arrive at a more stable judgment of things, we fix on some steady and general points of view; and always, in our thoughts, place ourselves in them, whatever may be our present situation" (T 3.3.1.15). We judge a character by its effects on those around it. So "Virtue in rags is still virtue" (T 3.3.1.19). The second *Enquiry* ties this to language: one who calls another vicious "must here, therefore, depart from his private and particular situation" (EPM 9.6). Adam Smith's impartial spectator develops the idea; see the library entry on [Smith](../../eras/03-early-modern/adam-smith.md).

## Natural and artificial virtues; justice and property

Benevolence, generosity and clemency are **natural** virtues, approved without any social scheme. Justice, fidelity to promises and allegiance are **artificial**: "the sense of justice and injustice is not deriv'd from nature, but arises artificially, tho' necessarily from education, and human conventions" (T 3.2.1.17). Artificial is not arbitrary. "Mankind is an inventive species; and where an invention is obvious and absolutely necessary, it may as properly be said to be natural as any thing" (T 3.2.1.19).

T 3.2.1 argues that a virtuous act needs a motive other than the sense of its virtue, or the account goes in a circle. No natural motive reliably yields just acts, such as repaying a loan to a rich miser. So the motive is artificial (Argument 15). T 3.2.2 explains the artifice. People need society, and the main threat to it is that goods can be taken. "The remedy, then, is not deriv'd from nature, but from artifice" (T 3.2.2.9). People come by degrees to a **convention** to leave each other's possessions alone, without any promise, since promising is itself a convention. "Two men, who pull the oars of a boat, do it by an agreement or convention, tho' they have never given promises to each other" (T 3.2.2.10). The conditions are specific: "'tis only from the selfishness and confin'd generosity of men, along with the scanty provision nature has made for his wants, that justice derives its origin" (T 3.2.2.18). Self-interest establishes justice; sympathy with the public good makes us approve it (T 3.2.2.24).

Hume names "the three fundamental laws of nature, that of the stability of possession, of its transference by consent, and of the performance of promises" (T 3.2.6.1). Which goods are whose is settled largely by the imagination's associations: occupation, prescription, accession, succession (T 3.2.3). Government comes later, to enforce these rules in large societies (T 3.2.7). Compare [Hobbes](../../eras/03-early-modern/thomas-hobbes.md) and [Rousseau](../../eras/03-early-modern/jean-jacques-rousseau.md).

## Utility

The second *Enquiry* organises ethics around a catalogue. "PERSONAL MERIT consists altogether in the possession of mental qualities, useful or agreeable to the person himself or to others" (EPM 9.1). That gives four classes: useful to others (justice, benevolence), useful to oneself (prudence, industry), agreeable to oneself (cheerfulness), agreeable to others (wit, good manners).

Utility explains approval. EPM 3 argues "That public utility is the sole origin of justice, and that reflections on the beneficial consequences of this virtue are the sole foundation of its merit" (EPM 3.1). In abundance, perfect benevolence or extreme scarcity justice would be useless, and it would lapse (EPM 3.3–19). EPM 5 denies that utility pleases through self-love, since we approve useful qualities in the long dead and in enemies. The source is a sentiment of humanity, "some particle of the dove, kneaded into our frame, along with the elements of the wolf and serpent" (EPM 9.4). Hume uses utility to explain approval, not as a method of decision; [Bentham](../../eras/04-19th-century/jeremy-bentham.md) later made it one. The book closes with the **sensible knave**, who "may think, that an act of iniquity or infidelity will make a considerable addition to his fortune, without causing any considerable breach in the social union and confederacy" (EPM 9.22). If the knave wants an answer, Hume admits, "it will be a little difficult to find any, which will to him appear satisfactory and convincing" (EPM 9.23; Argument 16).

## Taste

"Of the Standard of Taste" (1757) applies the theory of sentiment to beauty. It grants the sceptical premise: "Beauty is no quality in things themselves: It exists merely in the mind which contemplates them" ("Of the Standard of Taste" 7). It still finds a standard, in the general principles shown by works admired across ages, and in the qualified judge. "Strong sense, united to delicate sentiment, improved by practice, perfected by comparison, and cleared of all prejudice, can alone entitle critics to this valuable character; and the joint verdict of such, wherever they are to be found, is the true standard of taste and beauty" ("Of the Standard of Taste" 23). The illustration, from *Don Quixote*, is Sancho's two kinsmen, who taste leather and iron in a hogshead of wine; at the bottom lies a key on a leather thong ("Of the Standard of Taste" 15). Critics still discuss a circle here: good critics are known by their responses to good works, and good works by the responses of good critics.

## Religion

The *Natural History of Religion* (1757) separates two questions about religion, "that concerning its foundation in reason, and that concerning its origin in human nature" (NHR Intro 1). It sets the first aside with a formal nod to design and answers the second. "Polytheism or idolatry was, and necessarily must have been, the first and most ancient religion of mankind" (NHR 1.1), born of hope and fear about unknown causes. Belief then has "a kind of flux and reflux in the human mind," rising to theism and sinking back (NHR 8.1). Hume judges monotheism the less tolerant of the two (NHR 9).

The *Dialogues* takes up the first question. Cleanthes argues from design: "Look round the world: contemplate the whole and every part of it: You will find it to be nothing but one great machine" (DNR 2.5). Philo presses the weakness of the analogy, the rival hypotheses that fit as well, and our lack of experience of worlds being made (Argument 11). Parts 10–11 turn to evil: "Epicurus's old questions are yet unanswered. Is he willing to prevent evil, but not able? then is he impotent. Is he able, but not willing? then is he malevolent. Is he both able and willing? whence then is evil?" (DNR 10.25). Philo grants that evil may be consistent with God but denies that the world gives ground to infer him. It suggests rather "a blind Nature, impregnated by a great vivifying principle" (DNR 11.13; Argument 12). In Part 12 Philo says "no one has a deeper sense of religion impressed on his mind" (DNR 12.2), yet his concession shrinks to the claim "That the cause or causes of order in the universe probably bear some remote analogy to human intelligence," with no consequence for action (DNR 12.33). Who speaks for Hume is disputed; Norman Kemp Smith's edition (1935) argued for Philo.

## Politics

The politics extends the artificial virtues. "Of the First Principles of Government" (1741) asks how the few rule the many. "As Force is always on the side of the governed, the governors have nothing to support them but opinion. It is therefore, on opinion only that government is founded" ("Of the First Principles of Government" 1).

"Of the Original Contract" (1748) attacks the Whig doctrine that government rests on consent. Present governments began mostly in conquest or usurpation, and subjects do not think their allegiance depends on consent. Tacit consent by residence is a fiction: "Can we seriously say, that a poor peasant or artizan has a free choice to leave his country, when he knows no foreign language or manners, and lives from day to day, by the small wages which he acquires?" ("Of the Original Contract" 24). Allegiance and fidelity both rest directly on the interests of society, so "We gain nothing by resolving the one into the other" ("Of the Original Contract" 36). Compare [Locke](../../eras/03-early-modern/john-locke.md) and [Burke](../../eras/03-early-modern/edmund-burke.md).

## How the pieces interlock

The science of man begins with perceptions and the copy principle, which supplies a test of meaning. Association drives the imagination, abstract ideas let particular images serve general thought, and the fork confines demonstration to relations of ideas. Every matter of fact then rests on causation, which only experience teaches. Reason cannot justify induction, so custom does the work and produces belief as an enlivened idea. The idea of necessity, traced to custom's felt transition, yields the two definitions. The same imagination builds the beliefs in body and in a single self, and there it overreaches. Scepticism follows; nature checks it, leaving the title principle and mitigated scepticism. Books 2 and 3 run the same mechanics on feeling. Pride and love work by double relations, reason serves passion, moral distinctions are felt, sympathy spreads the feeling and the general point of view steadies it. Justice is an artifice of interest approved through sympathy, government rests on opinion, and taste and religion get natural histories. A good self-test is to trace any doctrine back to impressions and association.

## Key terms

In the format of the repo [glossary](../../glossary.md), restricted to Hume's usage, with misleading senses flagged.

- **Science of man:** the study of human nature by experience and observation, the foundation of the other sciences (T Intro 4–7).
- **Impression:** a perception (Hume's word for any content of the mind) "with most force and violence": a sensation, passion or emotion as it first appears (T 1.1.1.1). Not a vague feeling.
- **Idea:** a fainter copy of an impression, used in thinking (T 1.1.1.1; EHU 2.3). Narrower than Locke's idea.
- **Impressions of sensation and of reflexion:** sensations arise "from unknown causes"; impressions of reflexion, the passions and desires, arise from ideas (T 1.1.2.1).
- **Copy principle:** all simple ideas derive from simple impressions that they exactly represent (T 1.1.1.7). The name is the scholars'.
- **Missing shade of blue:** Hume's own counter-example to the copy principle (T 1.1.1.10; EHU 2.8).
- **Association of ideas:** the "gentle force" by which one idea introduces another, through resemblance, contiguity and cause or effect (T 1.1.4.1; EHU 3.2).
- **Abstract (general) idea:** a particular idea attached to a word and backed by a custom of calling up resembling ideas (T 1.1.7). There is no indeterminate idea.
- **Philosophical and natural relation:** a respect in which ideas may be compared, versus a relation that carries the mind from one idea to another (T 1.1.5.1; T 1.3.6.16).
- **Relations of ideas and matters of fact (Hume's fork):** truths known by thought alone, whose denial is contradictory, versus truths whose contrary is always possible (EHU 4.1–2). "Fork" is a later name.
- **Constant conjunction:** the repeated pairing of two kinds of object in experience; all that observation shows of causation.
- **Necessary connexion:** the idea of a tie between cause and effect, copied from the felt transition of the mind (EHU 7.28; T 1.3.14.22).
- **Custom (habit):** the principle that, after repetition, carries the mind from an impression to the idea of its usual attendant (EHU 5.5–6). Not a social custom.
- **Belief:** "A lively idea related to or associated with a present impression" (T 1.3.7.5); a manner of conceiving, not an added idea.
- **Force and vivacity:** the felt liveliness that separates impressions from ideas and belief from fancy.
- **Proof:** a causal argument from uniform experience that leaves no doubt (T 1.3.11.2). Not a demonstration.
- **Bundle theory:** the self as "a bundle or collection of different perceptions" (T 1.4.6.4).
- **Mitigated (academical) scepticism:** Pyrrhonian doubt "corrected by common sense and reflection," teaching modesty and limits (EHU 12.24–25).
- **Title principle:** Garrett's name for T 1.4.7.11: reason that is lively and mixed with a propensity "ought to be assented to."
- **Direct and indirect passions:** passions arising straight from pleasure or pain, versus passions such as pride and love that need further relations (T 2.1.1.4).
- **Double relation:** the joint relation of ideas and of impressions that produces the indirect passions (T 2.1.5.5).
- **Sympathy:** the communication by which an idea of another's feeling becomes the feeling itself (T 2.1.11.3). Not pity.
- **General point of view:** the common standpoint adopted to correct the partiality of sympathy (T 3.3.1.15; EPM 9.6).
- **Artificial virtue:** a virtue, such as justice, that depends on human convention (T 3.2.1.17). Not fake or arbitrary.

## Known internal tensions

The system strains at six joints. [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md) gives the history of each.

**(1) The Appendix on personal identity.** Hume writes that "all my hopes vanish, when I come to explain the principles, that unite our successive perceptions in our thought or consciousness" (T App. 20). He names two principles: "there are two principles, which I cannot render consistent; nor is it in my power to renounce either of them, viz. that all our distinct perceptions are distinct existences, and that the mind never perceives any real connexion among distinct existences" (T App. 21). The two are consistent with each other, so readers must supply the problem. Barry Stroud (*Hume*, 1977) suggests it lies in explaining how a bundle can be the subject that feels the easy transition. Garrett (1997) argues that association cannot say why a perception belongs to one bundle and not to another with exactly similar contents. Others point to the richer self of Book 2. Argument 8 sets out the proposals.

**(2) The two definitions of cause.** The first picks out a regularity in objects, the second a disposition of the mind, and they need not apply to the same cases: there are regularities nobody has observed and associations formed from too few instances. The counterfactual gloss in EHU 7.29 says something neither states. J. A. Robinson ("Hume's Two Definitions of 'Cause'," 1962) judged them incompatible. Others read them as one relation seen from two sides, as Hume's phrase "a different view of the same object" suggests (T 1.3.14.31). Garrett (1997) argues that they agree once each is read relative to a suitably placed observer. See Argument 5.

**(3) Scepticism against the science of man.** The Introduction promises a science built on experience. Book 1 concludes that the faculties used in that science are unjustified and at odds with each other. If causal reasoning has no title of its own, neither has the science of man. Hume answers with the title principle and with nature's refusal to let us stop believing. Thomas Reid (*Inquiry into the Human Mind*, 1764) charged that Hume kept trusting faculties his principles condemned; see [Reid](../../eras/03-early-modern/thomas-reid.md).

**(4) The missing shade.** Hume finds a counter-example to his first principle and keeps it. The principle is a generalisation from experience that he uses as a test of meaning. One clear exception makes the generalisation false. It also shows a simple idea formed from resemblances among other ideas, which is what a defender of substance might claim to have done. Replies include that shades are not truly simple, and that the principle is a working rule of the science of man rather than a law. The texts do not settle which Hume held.

**(5) Naturalism against scepticism.** One Hume argues that reason cannot justify induction, body or the self. Another uses induction freely and builds a confident science of passion and morals. Norman Kemp Smith (*The Philosophy of David Hume*, 1941) made the naturalism primary and the scepticism its servant. Readers from Reid to Russell and A. J. Ayer read Hume as a sceptic for whom naturalism is no answer. Recent work (Garrett, Millican, Louis Loeb) sorts Hume's sceptical arguments into epistemic verdicts and observations about human nature. See [Russell](../../eras/05-20th-century/bertrand-russell.md) and [the Vienna Circle](../../eras/05-20th-century/vienna-circle-logical-positivism.md).

**(6) The New Hume.** On the traditional reading, causation for Hume is regularity and necessity a projection of the mind. The New Hume reading holds that he believed in real powers in nature and denied only that we can know them or form a clear idea of them. Its evidence includes his talk of "secret powers" (EHU 4.16) and "ultimate springs and principles" (EHU 4.12). John P. Wright (*The Sceptical Realism of David Hume*, 1983), Edward Craig (*The Mind of God and the Works of Man*, 1987) and Galen Strawson (*The Secret Connexion*, 1989) defend it. Kenneth Winkler ("The New Hume," 1991), Simon Blackburn ("Hume and Thick Connexions," 1990) and Peter Millican ("Hume, Causal Realism, and Causal Science," 2009) reply that Hume's theory of ideas leaves no content for a power beyond regularity and the felt transition, and that the "secret powers" passages borrow his opponents' language. Argument 5 gives the texts on both sides.
