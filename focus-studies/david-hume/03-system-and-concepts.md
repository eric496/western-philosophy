# Hume: The System and Its Concepts
**English** | [简体中文](../../zh/focus-studies/david-hume/03-system-and-concepts.md)

*Focus study · [Dossier home](README.md) · Library entry: [David Hume](../../eras/03-early-modern/david-hume.md) · Study guide: [*Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding*](../../study/hume-enquiry.md) · Arguments in full: [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md)*

## The system in one view

Hume's philosophy is one project stated on the *Treatise*'s title page: *an attempt to introduce the experimental method of reasoning into moral subjects*. The Introduction to the *Treatise* gives the project's architecture. Every science — mathematics, natural philosophy, religion, morals — bears a relation to human nature and depends on the **science of man**, which is therefore "the only solid foundation for the other sciences" and must itself be built the way Newton built natural philosophy: on experience and observation, refusing hypotheses beyond what the phenomena show. What follows in the *Treatise* and the *Enquiries* is that foundation being laid, room by room: the understanding (Book I / the first *Enquiry*), the passions (Book II / the *Dissertation*), morals (Book III / the second *Enquiry*). And the foundation has a distinctive property that drives everything else in this dossier: a science of man is a matter-of-fact science, so its own results are probable, not demonstrative. Hume's system is a natural history of belief, feeling, and valuation that explains why we hold what we hold — and, at the points where its explanations run out, says so. Its skeptical conclusions and its naturalist explanations are not two philosophies stitched together; whether they are fully consistent is the deepest standing question about him (see the tensions below).

## Impressions, ideas, and the copy principle

All perceptions of the mind divide into **impressions** — perceptions in their full force: seeing red, feeling pain, being angry — and **ideas**, the fainter copies of impressions in memory, imagination, and reasoning. Impressions divide into those of **sensation** (from the senses) and those of **reflection** (the passions and emotions, which mostly arise from ideas in turn); both divide into **simple** (admitting no separation) and **complex**. On this inventory Hume rests the **copy principle**: every simple idea is copied from a corresponding simple impression. The imagination can compound, transpose, augment, and diminish what impressions supply — gold plus mountain gives a golden mountain — but it cannot originate a new simple idea: a man blind from birth has no idea of color. Hume then converts the principle from psychology into an instrument of criticism, the system's licensing office: confronted with a disputed term — substance, soul, necessary connection — ask *from what impression is that idea derived?* If none can be produced, the presumption is that the term is noise dressed as thought. He flags his own exception with characteristic candor: a man who has seen every shade of blue but one could, Hume concedes, supply the missing shade from his own imagination. He calls the case "so singular" that it scarcely overturns the principle — and the concession matters, because it shows the principle is offered as an empirical finding, not an axiom.

## Association and the imagination

Ideas do not succeed one another at random. Three principles connect them: **resemblance**, **contiguity** in time or place, and **cause and effect**. Hume calls them, in the *Treatise*'s Introduction, "a kind of attraction, which in the mental world will be found to have as extraordinary effects as in the natural" — his gravitation of the mind. Association is the system's universal mechanism, and most of its doctrines are applications of it. Memory preserves the order and liveliness of impressions; the imagination, moved by association, builds everything from fictions of continued existence to the idea of the self. One of the three principles gets a book's worth of treatment and the others a page, for a principled reason: resemblance and contiguity operate within what is present to sense and memory, but cause and effect is the only relation by which the mind goes *beyond* what is present — from the seen flame to the expected heat, from the observed past to the unobserved future. Everything Hume says about belief, causation, and induction is the theory of that third principle worked out.

## Relations of ideas and matters of fact — the fork

Early in *Enquiry* IV Hume divides "all the objects of human reason or enquiry" in two. **Relations of ideas** — geometry, algebra, arithmetic, and whatever is intuitively or demonstratively certain — are discoverable by the mere operation of thought; their denial implies a contradiction (that the three angles of a triangle equal two right angles cannot coherently be supposed false). **Matters of fact** are not so: their contrary is always conceivable, since "whatever is may not be" — that the sun will not rise tomorrow is a perfectly intelligible supposition. The division is later known as **Hume's fork**; the name is not Hume's, and the dossier flags it because the label can make a framing device sound like a technical theorem. The fork does two jobs. It states what reason unaided can and cannot do; and, applied in *Enquiry* XII's famous final paragraph, it becomes a test for whole libraries — a volume containing neither abstract reasoning about quantity and number nor experimental reasoning about matters of fact is to be committed "to the flames: for it can contain nothing but sophistry and illusion." Whether the fork itself falls under one of its own prongs — it is not demonstrable, and it is not obviously a matter of fact either — is a difficulty critics have pressed since the beginning; Humeans generally treat the fork as a methodological proposal rather than a member of its own classification. The fork's structural role is what matters here: it isolates matters of fact as the domain where a bridge is needed between what experience has shown and what we infer — and Section IV's argument is that reason cannot supply the bridge.

## Belief: a matter of force and vivacity

What distinguishes believing that Caesar died from merely entertaining the proposition? Not the idea's content — the same idea can be believed, doubted, or idly supposed. Hume's answer (Treatise 1.3.7, restated in *Enquiry* V) is that belief is a *manner* of conceiving: a believed idea is felt with greater **force and vivacity**, steadiness, and influence on thought and action than a fiction of the imagination. An idea associated with a present impression borrows the impression's vivacity and becomes belief; this is why testimony, education, and repetition persuade, and why poetry and romance, which do not connect their ideas to present impressions in the customary way, do not. Belief is thus "something felt by the mind" — an operation of the sensitive rather than the cogitative part of our nature. **Probability** is belief in its graduated form: where past experience has been mixed, the vivacity is divided among the contrary outcomes in proportion to their frequencies, and the mind's expectation is a balance of forces. This is the psychology that Section X of the *Enquiry* turns into an epistemology of testimony — a wise man proportions his belief to the evidence — and it explains why Hume's skepticism never touches practice: custom sets the vivacity, and belief follows it whether or not reason can certify the setting.

## Causation, custom, and necessary connection

The analysis runs in stages, and the order is the argument. In a *single* instance — one billiard ball striking another — we perceive contiguity in space, priority in time, and nothing else: no power, force, or tie is given to sense. With *repetition*, experience shows **constant conjunction**: objects of the first kind have always been followed by objects of the second. Repetition adds nothing to the objects, but it changes the mind: **custom** now determines it to pass from the impression of the one to a lively idea of the other, and that felt **determination of the mind** is itself an impression — of reflection — from which, by the copy principle, the idea of **necessary connection** is legitimately derived. Necessity is therefore located where its impression is: "necessity is something, that exists in the mind, not in objects." Hume caps the analysis with **two definitions of cause**: an object followed by another, "and where all the objects similar to the first are followed by objects similar to the second" (the regularity face); and an object followed by another, "and whose appearance always conveys the thought to that other" (the mind's transition face). Whether the two definitions state one relation from two sides, or two different relations, has been debated ever since. The problem of **induction** is the same machinery viewed from the side of justification: the inference from observed to unobserved presupposes that nature's course will continue uniformly, and that presupposition can be established neither demonstratively (its contrary is conceivable) nor from experience (the appeal is circular). Reason does not make the inference; custom does. Note what Hume builds on the far side of the skepticism: *Treatise* 1.3.15 sets out "rules by which to judge of causes and effects" — a working methodology of causal inference. He is the analyst of causal reasoning, not its enemy. Whether he also believed, beyond his analysis, in objective causal powers we can form no adequate idea of — the "New Hume" question — is flagged here and argued as a live dispute in [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md).

## The self: a bundle, feigned identity, and the Appendix

Apply the licensing test to the self. Introspect as long as you like: "I always stumble on some particular perception or other, of heat or cold, light or shade, love or hatred, pain or pleasure. I never can catch *myself* at any time without a perception." There is no impression of a simple, identical, continuing self; therefore there is no such idea. What we call the mind is "a bundle or collection of different perceptions, which succeed each other with an inconceivable rapidity, and are in a perpetual flux and movement" — compared, with explicit caution against pressing the image, to a theatre where perceptions successively appear and vanish. Personal identity is then a construction: memory produces resemblance among past and present perceptions, causation links them in a chain, and the imagination, sliding smoothly along related perceptions as it does along the parts of one enduring object, *feigns* an identity it mistakes for something perceived. The doctrine is the copy principle's most famous application — and then Hume did something almost no philosopher does. In the **Appendix** to the *Treatise*, reviewing the section, he declared himself caught in a labyrinth: two principles — that all our distinct perceptions are distinct existences, and that the mind never perceives any real connection among distinct existences — he could neither renounce nor reconcile, and without reconciling them the bundle has no account of what makes its perceptions *one mind's*. He confessed he could not solve it, and left the confession in print. What exactly the inconsistency is — which doctrine, precisely, collapses — scholars still dispute; that Hume announced a failure at the center of his system is not in dispute, and the dossier takes the Appendix as seriously as the section it corrects.

## Reason and the passions

"Reason is, and ought only to be the slave of the passions, and can never pretend to any other office than to serve and obey them" (Treatise 2.3.3). Read with its context, the slogan is a thesis about motivation, not a license for irrationality. A passion is an impression of reflection — in Hume's phrase, an "original existence" that contains no representative quality and so can be neither true nor false; reason, which deals in truth and falsehood about facts and relations, therefore cannot oppose a passion head-on, only inform it — by showing that its object does not exist, or that the chosen means will not reach it. Abstract reasoning alone never moved anyone; every action needs a passion to supply the end. Reason keeps full authority in its own office: a passion founded on a false supposition dissolves when the supposition is corrected. Two structural details matter for the ethics. **Calm** passions (general benevolence, a steady regard for one's long-term interest) operate so quietly that they are routinely *mistaken for reason* — much of what moralists call reason's victory over passion is a calm passion's victory over a violent one. And the **indirect passions** (pride, humility, love, hatred) are built by a "double relation" of impressions and ideas — association doing systematic work in Book II, which is why the theory of association is the system's engine and not a preliminary.

## Sympathy and the moral sense

How do other people's feelings become morally significant to us? By **sympathy**: we form an idea of another's passion from its outward signs, and because each of us carries an ever-present, maximally lively impression of *himself*, that idea is enlivened — converted, in the degree of vivacity it acquires — into the passion itself, felt more faintly in us. Sympathy varies with resemblance, contiguity, and acquaintance, which is also its moral liability: left uncorrected it approves the near and dear over the distant and strange. Hume's correction is one of his finest devices: for moral judgment we occupy "steady and general points of view," surveying a character with sympathy directed at those who actually deal with its possessor, and it is from that corrected standpoint that moral language becomes common enough to share. **Moral distinctions**, accordingly, are not derived from reason — agree on every fact about an act and the viciousness is still not among the facts; and moral judgments motivate, which inert beliefs cannot — but rest on a **moral sense**: approbation or blame *felt* on contemplating a character from the general point of view. Morality, Hume says, "is more properly felt than judg'd of." In the second *Enquiry* the mechanism is simplified and partly renamed: sympathy recedes and a "principle of humanity" does the work, while **utility** — a quality's tendency to serve its possessor's or the public's interest — carries much more of the explanation of why virtues please. The *Treatise* and the *Enquiry* differ in machinery here, and the dossier keeps both versions visible rather than blending them.

## Justice, the artificial virtues, and convention

Some virtues are **natural**: benevolence and generosity please immediately, by sympathy with their effects, without any system behind them. **Justice** is different, and Hume's analysis of it is the founding text of a whole tradition. The single acts of justice — returning this deposit, honoring that boundary — may serve nobody's interest in the instance; what serves everyone's interest is the *whole scheme* of property, promise, and contract-keeping, and the scheme exists only because human beings, in the **circumstances of justice** (moderate scarcity outside, limited generosity inside), converge on it. The convergence is a **convention**, and Hume is precise about what that word excludes: not a contract, not a promise — a promise itself presupposes the convention that promises bind. His image is two men pulling the oars of a boat, who coordinate "by a common sense of interest" without ever exchanging promises. From the convention flow the rules of property (present possession, occupation, prescription, accession, succession) whose merit is the scheme's utility; allegiance to government is artificial on the same pattern. This is where the moral theory turns political, and where Hume breaks with the contract theorists the learning path reads around him: obligation to justice is grounded in sentiment about a useful convention, not in a founding act of consent.

## Mitigated skepticism: the resting point

Book I of the *Treatise* ends in a famous small drama: the skeptical arguments, followed honestly, produce "philosophical melancholy and delirium" — and then nature cures it unaided. "I dine, I play a game of back-gammon, I converse, and am merry with my friends; and when after three or four hours' amusement, I wou'd return to these speculations, they appear so cold, and strain'd, and ridiculous, that I cannot find in my heart to enter into them any farther." *Enquiry* XII gives the drama its doctrine. **Pyrrhonism** — total, suspended doubt — is psychologically unlivable and refutes nothing, since nature compels belief regardless. **Mitigated skepticism**, the academical philosophy, is skepticism retained as a discipline: doubt dogmatic and abstruse speculation; limit inquiry to subjects proportioned to our faculties; proportion belief to evidence; and in common life, believe and act on custom's authority without pretending the authority is demonstration. That is the system's resting point — skepticism about foundations, naturalism about practice, and modesty as the practical moral of both.

## Known tensions in the system

The template asks for the strains to be named, since they drive the scholarship (see [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md)). Five recur. (1) **The Appendix confession.** The theory of personal identity is the one place Hume himself declared the system inconsistent; locating the exact fault is still contested. (2) **Naturalism versus skepticism.** Is Hume fundamentally a naturalist — his skeptical arguments serving a positive, quasi-scientific theory of human nature — or fundamentally a skeptic whose natural history is offered under skeptical erasure? Norman Kemp Smith's *The Philosophy of David Hume* (1941) made the naturalist reading dominant for a generation: on it, Hume's ruling conviction is that belief is "more properly an act of the sensitive, than of the cogitative part of our natures," and the skepticism is subordinate. Skeptical readings, older and newer (including avowedly Pyrrhonian reconstructions), invert the priority and take the naturalism itself to be advanced as the mitigated skeptic's best available account rather than as doctrine. The dossier presents this as a live dispute about the system's center of gravity, not a question with a settled answer. (3) **The "New Hume" debate.** Did Hume merely deny that we can know or conceive objective necessary connections, while believing they exist (the skeptical-realist reading associated with Galen Strawson and John Wright), or did he reduce causation to regularity plus projection (the traditional reading)? Flagged here; argued in full in [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md). (4) **The two definitions of cause.** One places causation in the objects' regularities, the other in the mind's transitions; whether they are equivalent, complementary, or in tension is unresolved. (5) **The fork's self-application** — noted above: the fork's own epistemic status fits neither prong cleanly, and how much this costs the system is debated.

## Key terms

In the format of the repo [glossary](../../glossary.md), restricted to Hume's own usage, with later textbook senses flagged where they mislead.

- **Impression:** any perception in its liveliest form — sensations, and (impressions of reflection) the passions and emotions. Textbook "sense datum" is narrower than Hume's term: anger and pride are impressions too.
- **Idea:** the fainter copy of an impression in memory, imagination, or reasoning. Not a mental picture by definition — Hume's term covers concepts generally, though his theory explains them on the copy model.
- **Copy principle:** every simple idea copies a corresponding simple impression (Treatise 1.1.1; *Enquiry* II). Hume's licensing test for philosophical terms — and an empirical claim, as his own exception (the missing shade of blue) shows.
- **Force and vivacity:** the felt strength with which a perception strikes the mind — the single dimension along which impressions outrun ideas and beliefs outrun fictions. Textbook "vividness" understates it: vivacity is also influence on action.
- **Association of ideas:** the "gentle force" connecting ideas by resemblance, contiguity, and cause and effect (Treatise 1.1.4; *Enquiry* III). Hume's universal mechanism, not merely a remark about trains of thought.
- **Custom (habit):** the determination, produced by repetition of a conjunction, to expect its usual attendant — "the great guide of human life" (*Enquiry* V). Not mere routine: it is the mechanism that manufactures belief.
- **Belief:** a lively idea associated with a present impression — distinguished from fiction by its manner of conception, its felt force, and its influence on action (Treatise 1.3.7). Hume insists it is "something felt by the mind," not an extra idea added to the content believed.
- **Relations of ideas:** propositions intuitively or demonstratively certain, whose denial implies a contradiction — mathematics and logic. One prong of the (later-named) fork.
- **Matters of fact:** propositions whose contrary is conceivable, known only from experience. The domain of causal inference, probability, and everything the fork says reason cannot settle alone.
- **Constant conjunction:** the repeated sequence of similar objects — all of causation that experience actually shows. Hume's negative point is that nothing further is observed; his positive theory is built on exactly this.
- **Necessary connection:** the idea Hume traces to the mind's felt determination after repeated conjunction, not to any perceived tie in objects (*Enquiry* VII). The traditional reading; the "New Hume" literature contests whether Hume denied such connections exist or only that we can conceive them.
- **Determination of the mind:** custom's felt transition from an impression to the lively idea of its usual attendant. The impression of reflection from which the idea of necessity is copied.
- **Probability:** reasoning from mixed experience, in which an idea's vivacity is divided among contrary outcomes in proportion to their past frequencies (Treatise 1.3.11–13; *Enquiry* VI). Psychological before it is mathematical — degrees of belief as balances of force.
- **Bundle:** the mind or self as "a bundle or collection of different perceptions" in perpetual flux (Treatise 1.4.6). "Bundle theory" is the textbooks' label; Hume's claim is the report of introspection plus the copy principle, and his Appendix withdrew confidence in its completeness.
- **Passion:** an impression of reflection — direct (desire, joy, fear) or indirect (pride, love, built by association's double relation); **calm** passions, operating steadily, are routinely mistaken for reason. "Reason is the slave of the passions" (Treatise 2.3.3) is a thesis about motivation within this taxonomy, not a slogan for unreason.
- **Sympathy:** the mechanism by which an idea of another's feeling is enlivened into the feeling itself in us (Treatise 2.1.11). Not pity — sympathy with joy is equally basic — and not itself a virtue: it is the channel through which moral sentiment receives its objects.
- **Moral sense (moral sentiment):** the approbation or blame felt when a character is surveyed from a general point of view (Treatise 3.1.2). The textbook "moral sense school" label groups Hume with Shaftesbury and Hutcheson, but Hume's version is naturalized: sense corrected by general points of view, with utility doing much of the explaining — in the second *Enquiry*, under the name of humanity.
- **General point of view:** the steady standpoint — surveying a character as it affects those who deal with its possessor — from which moral judgments are corrected for sympathy's partiality (Treatise 3.3.1). Without it, moral language could not be shared.
- **Natural virtue:** a quality approved directly, by sympathy with its usual effects, without reference to any system — benevolence, generosity, natural abilities in part. The contrast class for the artificial virtues.
- **Artificial virtue:** a quality — justice, fidelity to promises, allegiance — whose merit depends on a human convention and the whole scheme it belongs to (Treatise 3.2). Artificial does not mean fake: the obligations are real, but their ground is the scheme's utility, not any single act's tendency.
- **Convention:** a shared sense of common interest expressed in practice, arising without promise or contract — the two men rowing a boat (Treatise 3.2.2). Precisely what Hume denies the social contract can be: promises themselves rest on convention, not the reverse.
- **Mitigated skepticism:** the resting point of *Enquiry* XII (there called academical philosophy): accept the skeptical arguments' force against dogmatic metaphysics, limit inquiry to what our faculties can handle, and let custom conduct common life. Not Pyrrhonism, which Hume treats as unlivable — skepticism retained as modesty, not paralysis.
