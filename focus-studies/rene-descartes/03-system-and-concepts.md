# Descartes: The System and Its Concepts

*Focus study · [Dossier home](README.md) · Library entry: [René Descartes](../../eras/03-early-modern/rene-descartes.md) · Arguments in full: [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md) · Study guide: [*Meditations on First Philosophy*](../../study/descartes-meditations.md)*

## A system built in order

Descartes wanted a system in the strict sense: a body of knowledge in which each truth comes after the truths it depends on. The *Meditations* (1641) lays the foundations. The *Principles of Philosophy* (1644) sets out the metaphysics and the physics in numbered articles. *The Passions of the Soul* (1649) carries the system into physiology and ethics. Several central doctrines appear only in the letters: the creation of the eternal truths, the three primitive notions, and most of the ethics.

Two cautions come with the map. Descartes shifted his emphasis over twenty years. The early *Rules for the Direction of the Mind* (written c. 1628) is about method and barely mentions God, while the late letters to Elisabeth put the union of mind and body at the centre of ordinary life. He also wrote for different readers: the *Discourse on the Method* (1637) is a short autobiography in French, the *Meditations* a first-person exercise in Latin, the *Principles* a textbook meant to replace the Jesuit manuals. This file names the text each claim comes from and flags the disputes.

**Texts and quotation.** English quotations use the Haldane and Ross translation (1911–12) because it is in the public domain and can be checked. The [learning path](../../learning-path.md) does not recommend it for reading; read Cottingham or Cress. Its weak point is the word "thought." Descartes' *cogitatio* (French *pensée*) covers every conscious state, doubting, willing, imagining and sensing as well as reasoning, and Haldane and Ross sometimes use other words for its parts, as when *sentiens* becomes "feels." Where the English misleads, the Latin or French is given in brackets. Their square brackets mark additions from the French versions of 1647. References are to the Adam–Tannery edition (AT), whose pages appear in the margins of Cottingham and Cress.

## The tree of philosophy

In 1647 Descartes added a preface to the French *Principles*, written as a letter to its translator, Claude Picot. It gives his picture of the whole: "Thus philosophy as a whole is like a tree whose roots are metaphysics, whose trunk is physics, and whose branches, which issue from this trunk, are all the other sciences. These reduce themselves to three principal ones, viz. medicine, mechanics and morals" (Preface to the French *Principles*, AT IXB 14). The morals meant are "the highest and most perfect moral science which, presupposing a complete knowledge of the other sciences, is the last degree of wisdom" (same page).

The image makes three claims. Knowledge is one tree, not a set of separate disciplines. Its order is fixed: metaphysics comes first because it contains the principles of knowledge, among them, the Preface says, the attributes of God, the immateriality of the soul and "all the clear and simple notions which are in us." And its point is practical. Fruit is picked from the ends of the branches, so the main use of philosophy lies in the parts learned last. The *Discourse* had promised that the new philosophy could make us "the masters and possessors of nature" (*Discourse* VI, AT VI 62). The French reads *comme maistres & possesseurs*, "as it were masters and possessors," a hedge the English drops.

This file follows the tree: method, then the roots (doubt, ideas, God, the will, substance), the trunk (matter, motion, mechanism) and the branches (the passions and morality).

## Method and the order of reasons

The *Discourse* reduces logic to four rules (*Discourse* II, AT VI 18–19). "The first of these was to accept nothing as true which I did not clearly recognise to be so," admitting into a judgment nothing beyond what is presented "so clearly and distinctly" that there is no occasion to doubt it. The second is to divide each difficulty into as many parts as needed. The third is to think in order, from the simplest objects rising "little by little, or by degrees" to the most complex. The fourth is to make enumerations so complete that nothing is left out. The rules compress the earlier *Rules*, which rest all knowledge on two acts: intuition (*intuitus*), the attentive mind's grasp of something simple, and deduction, a chain of intuitions linked by a continuous movement of thought.

The Second Replies (1641) explain how the *Meditations* applies the method. They separate order from the manner of proof. "The order consists merely in putting forward those things first that should be known without the aid of what comes subsequently, and arranging all other matters so that their proof depends solely on what precedes them" (Second Replies, AT VII 155). That is why the real distinction of mind and body waits for the sixth *Meditation*. The manner of proof is twofold. "Analysis shows the true way by which a thing was methodically discovered and derived" (AT VII 155), so that a reader who follows it makes the result his own. Synthesis derives conclusions from definitions, postulates and axioms, as in Euclid. It forces assent from a hostile reader but hides how the result was found. Descartes states his choice: "I have used in my Meditations only analysis, which is the best and truest method of teaching" (AT VII 156).

His reason is that the first notions of geometry agree with the senses and everyone grants them, while the first notions of metaphysics clash with prejudices held since childhood and need long attention. So the *Meditations* is written as exercises, and the reader is meant to do them. As a concession he appended a synthetic summary to the Second Replies, "drawn up in geometrical fashion," with definitions, postulates and axioms. Several definitions below come from it.

## Doubt and the cogito

The method begins with doubt used as a tool. *Meditation* 1 runs through three grounds: the senses sometimes deceive; there are no sure marks to tell waking from dreaming (Argument 1 in [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md)); and an all-powerful God, or failing that "some evil genius not less powerful than deceitful" (*Med.* 1, AT VII 22), might deceive me even about arithmetic (Argument 2). The doubt concerns knowledge only. Descartes says he is "not considering the question of action, but only of knowledge" (AT VII 22), and the *Discourse* gave him a provisional morality to live by meanwhile. It is also a doubt about the faculties themselves, so only proof that my maker is no deceiver can fully answer it.

The doubt halts at the cogito: "I am, I exist, is necessarily true each time that I pronounce it, or that I mentally conceive it" (*Med.* 2, AT VII 25). The *Discourse* gives the famous form *je pense, donc je suis* (*Discourse* IV, AT VI 32). Descartes denied it is a syllogism: one who says it "does not deduce existence from thought by a syllogism, but, by a simple act of mental vision, recognises it as if it were a thing that is known per se" (Second Replies, AT VII 140). Argument 3 examines it. For the system, the cogito supplies the first item of knowledge and a model of what knowledge is like.

## Thought and clear and distinct perception

The cogito says that I am, not what I am. *Meditation* 2 answers: "A thing which thinks. What is a thing which thinks? It is a thing which doubts, understands, [conceives], affirms, denies, wills, refuses, which also imagines and feels [*sentiens*]" (*Med.* 2, AT VII 28). The geometrical appendix defines the term: "Thought is a word that covers everything that exists in us in such a way that we are immediately conscious of it. Thus all the operations of will, intellect, imagination, and of the senses are thoughts" (Second Replies, Def. I, AT VII 160). "Immediately" excludes bodily motions that follow from thought, such as walking. So Descartes answers Gassendi that "I walk, hence I exist" is no proof "except in so far as our awareness of walking is a thought" (Fifth Replies, AT VII 352). The scholastics had put sensation and imagination in a sensitive soul shared with animals. Descartes makes consciousness the mark of the mental and puts all of it on one side of the line.

He then asks what made the cogito certain. "In this first knowledge there is nothing that assures me of its truth, excepting the clear and distinct perception of that which I state," and so "it seems to me that already I can establish as a general rule that all things which I perceive very clearly and very distinctly are true" (*Med.* 3, AT VII 35). Scholars call this the **truth rule**. The same sentence notes that clarity would not suffice "if it could ever happen that a thing which I conceived so clearly and distinctly could be false," which is why the rule awaits God.

The *Principles* defines the terms. "I term that clear which is present and apparent to an attentive mind, in the same way as we assert that we see objects clearly when, being present to the regarding eye, they operate upon it with sufficient strength. But the distinct is that which is so precise and different from all other objects that it contains within itself nothing but what is clear" (*Principles* I.45, AT VIIIA 22). Pain is his example of a perception that is clear but not distinct (I.46, AT VIIIA 22): the pain is vividly present, but we confuse it with a judgment that something like it exists in the foot. Clarity and distinctness belong to intellectual perception, not to feelings of conviction.

## Ideas: their reality and their origin

In the strict sense ideas are those thoughts that are, "so to speak, images of the things," such as the thought of a man, a chimera, an angel or God (*Med.* 3, AT VII 37). Considered only as modes of thought they cannot be false. Error enters with judgment, when I affirm that an idea resembles something outside me.

**Formal and objective reality.** Every idea can be taken two ways. As a mode of my mind it has **formal reality**, the reality anything has by existing, and in that respect all my ideas are equal. As a representation it has **objective reality**, the reality of what it represents. Ideas of substances "contain so to speak more objective reality within them" than ideas of modes, and the idea of God more than any idea of a finite substance (*Med.* 3, AT VII 40). The causal principle follows: "it is manifest by the natural light that there must at least be as much reality in the efficient and total cause as in its effect" (AT VII 40). It holds for ideas too. An idea needs a cause "in which there is at least as much formal reality as this idea contains of objective reality" (AT VII 41). A cause may contain that reality **formally**, by being the same kind of thing, or **eminently**, by being something higher that could produce it. This drives the trademark argument (Argument 5).

**Innate, adventitious, factitious.** "Among these ideas, some appear to me to be innate, some adventitious, and others to be formed [or invented] by myself" (*Med.* 3, AT VII 37–38; Latin *innatae*, *adventitiae*, *a me ipso factae*). Innate ideas come from my own nature: thing, truth, thought, God. Adventitious ideas seem to come from outside, like a noise or the fire's heat. Factitious ideas are made up: "sirens, hippogryphs, and the like." The sorting is provisional at this stage. Descartes' example of what hangs on it is the sun: small as the senses show it, many times larger than the earth as astronomy, built from innate notions, shows it. Innateness does not mean infants think about geometry. The *Notes against a Programme* (1648) compares innate ideas to family dispositions to certain diseases: a faculty that produces the idea on the right occasion.

## God, conservation and the eternal truths

The proofs come in three forms. The trademark argument reasons from my idea of God to God as its cause (Argument 5). A second argument in *Meditation* 3 reasons from my continued existence (Argument 6). The ontological argument of *Meditation* 5 reasons from God's essence, from which existence can no more be separated than a valley from a mountain (Argument 9).

God then does three jobs. He guarantees the truth rule, which is the step the circle objection targets (Argument 7; tension 1 below). He keeps the world in being: the parts of time are independent, so "the distinction between creation and conservation is solely a distinction of the reason" (*Med.* 3, AT VII 49). And he is the first cause of motion and source of the laws of nature (*Principles* II.36, AT VIIIA 61).

**The creation of the eternal truths.** The most striking doctrine appears in letters to Marin Mersenne of spring 1630, eleven years before the *Meditations*. On 15 April Descartes wrote that the mathematical truths Mersenne called eternal were established by God and depend on him entirely, like all the rest of his creatures (AT I 145). To call them independent of God is to treat him like Jupiter or Saturn, subject to the Styx and the Fates; God set these laws in nature as a king sets laws in his kingdom. On 6 May he added that in God willing and knowing are one, so the truths are true because God knows them, not known because they are true (AT I 149). On 27 May he called God the author of the essences of creatures as well as their existence, as "efficient and total cause" (*efficiens & totalis causa*, AT I 152). In 1648 he told Arnauld he would not dare say that God cannot make a mountain without a valley, or one and two not make three; God has only given him a mind that cannot conceive these things (letter of 29 July 1648, AT V 224). Readers call this **divine voluntarism**. It sits awkwardly with the system's reliance on fixed essences (tension 3).

## The will and judgment

*Meditation* 4 explains error without blaming God. The intellect perceives ideas and neither affirms nor denies. The will affirms, denies, pursues and avoids. The intellect is finite, but "it is free-will alone or liberty of choice which I find to be so great in me that I can conceive no other idea to be more great" (*Med.* 4, AT VII 57), and by it I bear "the image and similitude of God." Error comes from the mismatch: "since the will is much wider in its range and compass than the understanding, I do not restrain it within the same bounds, but extend it also to things which I do not understand" (AT VII 58). The remedy is to suspend judgment where perception is not clear and distinct (Argument 8).

Making judgment an act of will is new. Aquinas and most scholastics treated judgment as an act of the intellect. Descartes makes belief something I do and answer for. The *Principles* draws the moral: acting freely is man's chief perfection and the ground of praise and blame, "for we do not praise automatic machines" for doing exactly what they were built to do (*Principles* I.37, AT VIIIA 18).

**What freedom is.** *Meditation* 4 adds that for freedom "it is not necessary that I should be indifferent as to the choice of one or the other of two contraries." Rather, "the more I lean to the one," whether from clear reasons or because God so disposes my thought, "the more freely do I choose and embrace it." So "this indifference which I feel, when I am not swayed to one side rather than to the other by lack of reason, is the lowest grade of liberty" (AT VII 57–58). Highest freedom is spontaneous assent to what is clearly seen. Letters to the Jesuit Denis Mesland (2 May 1644, AT IV 110–120; and a Latin letter that AT dates, with a query, 9 February 1645, AT IV 173–175) seem to add a two-way power to hold back even from a clear truth, if we think it good to show our freedom. Tension 4 takes this up.

## Substance, principal attribute and mode

*Principles* I.51–56 gives the system its ontology. Everything that exists is a substance or a way a substance is.

**Substance.** "When we conceive of substance, we merely conceive an existent thing which requires nothing but itself in order to exist" (*Principles* I.51, AT VIIIA 24). Strictly only God fits, since every creature needs God to keep it in being, so the word does not apply *univoce*, in the same sense, to God and creatures. Created substances need only God's concurrence (I.52).

**Principal attribute.** We know a substance only through its attributes, since nothing has no attributes (I.52). And "there is always one principal property of substance which constitutes its nature and essence, and on which all the others depend. Thus extension in length, breadth and depth, constitutes the nature of corporeal substance; and thought constitutes the nature of thinking substance" (I.53, AT VIIIA 25). A body's shape or motion is "but a mode of this extended thing"; a mind's imagining or willing is one of "so many diverse forms of thinking." This one-attribute doctrine makes dualism follow quickly: each principal attribute is the whole nature of its substance, so no substance has two.

**Modes.** A mode is a way a substance is and cannot exist or be understood without it (I.56, I.61). God, who does not change, has attributes but no modes. Substantial forms and real qualities are gone. A body's colour is the arrangement of its surface together with a sensation in a mind. Three kinds of distinction complete the doctrine (I.60–62): a **real distinction** between substances, each clearly and distinctly understood without the other; a **modal distinction** between a mode and its substance; and a **distinction of reason** between a substance and its principal attribute, which separate only in thought. A mind that stopped thinking would be no mind.

## Mind and body: distinction, union, primitive notions

**The real distinction.** "It is certain that this I [that is to say, my soul by which I am what I am], is entirely and absolutely distinct from my body, and can exist without it" (*Med.* 6, AT VII 78). The argument runs through God's power: what I clearly and distinctly understand apart can be made to exist apart (Argument 10). A second argument: "body is by nature always divisible, and the mind is entirely indivisible" (AT VII 86; Argument 11). The distinction frees physics from souls, and the 1641 title page presents it as ground for the soul's immortality, though the Synopsis concedes it does not prove survival.

**The union.** Nature teaches "that I am not only lodged in my body as a pilot in a vessel, but that I am very closely united to it, and so to speak so intermingled with it that I seem to compose with it one whole" (*Med.* 6, AT VII 81). A pilot sees damage to his ship; I feel pain. Pain, hunger and thirst are "certain confused modes of thought which are produced by the union and apparent intermingling of mind and body" (AT VII 81). They do not show how bodies are, but they guide the composite toward what helps and away from what harms.

**Elisabeth and the primitive notions.** Princess Elisabeth of Bohemia (1618–1680), living in exile at The Hague, opened the correspondence on 6/16 May 1643. She asked how the soul can determine the bodily spirits to voluntary action, "being only a thinking substance" (*n'estant qu'une substance pensante*, AT III 661, my translation). Every determination of motion seems to need pushing, the manner of the push, or the shape of the mover's surface. The first two need contact, the third extension; Descartes denies the soul extension, and contact seems incompatible with an immaterial thing. She asked for a definition of the soul's substance apart from its action, thought (AT III 660–661). Argument 13 reconstructs the exchange.

Descartes replied on 21 May 1643 that he had said almost nothing about the soul's power to act on the body and be acted on by it, since his aim had been the distinction (AT III 664–665). He then offered **primitive notions** (*notions primitives*), which are *comme des originaux*, "as it were originals," on whose pattern we form all our other knowledge (AT III 665). After the most general, being, number, duration, there is extension for body, from which shape and motion follow; thought for the soul, including perceptions of the understanding and inclinations of the will; and for soul and body together, only their union, on which depends the soul's power to move the body and the body's to cause sensations and passions (AT III 665). Knowledge consists in keeping these apart. Error comes from explaining one by another, as when we model the soul's moving the body on one body moving another (AT III 666).

Elisabeth answered on 10/20 June that she would find it easier to grant matter and extension to the soul than to grant an immaterial being the power to move a body and be moved by it (AT III 685). On 28 June Descartes turned the notions into three ways of knowing. The soul is known by pure intellect, body by intellect aided by imagination, and the union only obscurely by either but *tres-clairement par les sens*, very clearly by the senses (AT III 691–692). One learns to conceive the union by ordinary life and conversation and by abstaining from meditation (AT III 692). He invited her to attribute matter and extension to the soul freely, since that is just to conceive it united to the body, adding that this extension differs from a body's, which excludes other bodies from its place (AT III 694). Whether the union is a third kind of thing is disputed. John Cottingham ("Cartesian Trialism," 1985) treats it as a third category; most readers keep two substances and treat the union notion as a way of knowing.

**The pineal gland.** The *Passions* locates the interaction. The soul, though joined to the whole body, acts most immediately in "a certain very small gland which is situated in the middle of its substance" (*Passions* I.31, AT XI 351–352). Descartes chose it because it is single, while most of the brain is double, so the images from the two eyes can meet there (I.32). The soul moves the gland slightly, redirecting the animal spirits, the fine parts of the blood that flow through the nerves and swell the muscles (I.34, AT XI 354). The gland locates the interaction without explaining it, and Elisabeth's question about contact applies to it as to any body.

## Mechanism: matter, extension and motion

The trunk rests on one thesis. "The nature of matter or of body in its universal aspect, does not consist in its being hard, or heavy, or coloured, or one that affects our senses in some other way, but solely in the fact that it is a substance extended in length, breadth and depth" (*Principles* II.4, AT VIIIA 42). Hardness only means that parts resist our hands; a body that always retreated from the touch would lose nothing of its nature. The wax of *Meditation* 2 makes the same point (Argument 4).

Three consequences follow. There is no vacuum: "As regards a vacuum in the philosophic sense of the word, i.e. a space in which there is no substance, it is evident that such cannot exist, because the extension of space or internal place, is not different from that of body" (*Principles* II.16, AT VIIIA 49; Argument 15). Matter is indefinitely divisible, so there are no atoms (II.20). And the matter of heaven and earth is one, with no edge we can conceive (II.21–22).

**Motion and its laws.** Change is local motion, the transfer of a part of matter from the neighbourhood of the bodies touching it to that of others (II.25). God, being immutable, conserves the same total quantity of motion (II.36, AT VIIIA 61), and from his immutability follow three laws, given here from Haldane and Ross's summary of Part II. "The first law of nature: that each thing as far as in it lies, continues always in the same state; and that which is once moved always continues so to move" (II.37, AT VIIIA 62). "The second law of nature: that all motion is of itself in a straight line; and thus things which move in a circle always tend to recede from the centre of the circle that they describe" (II.39, AT VIIIA 63). The third governs collisions: a body meeting a stronger one loses none of its motion, and one meeting a weaker one loses what it passes on (II.40). Seven rules of impact follow, most of them false. The first two laws come close to the principle of inertia Newton later stated. In a full world, any motion must be circular, since a body can move only if others fill its place, and Part III builds on this the theory of **vortices** carrying the planets round the sun. The library entry describes its fate after Newton.

**Animals as machines.** *Discourse* V summarises the suppressed *Treatise of Man*, including the circulation of the blood, which Descartes takes from William Harvey while rejecting Harvey's account of the heartbeat. It then asks how we could tell a well-made machine from a human being. There are "two very certain tests" (*Discourse* V, AT VI 56). A machine could never "use speech or other signs as we do when placing our thoughts on record for the benefit of others," nor arrange words to "reply appropriately to everything that may be said in its presence, as even the lowest type of man can do." And though it might do some things better than we do, it would fail at others, "for while reason is a universal instrument which can serve for all contingencies, these organs have need of some special adaptation for every particular action" (AT VI 57). Animals fail both tests, so their behaviour comes "only from the disposition of their organs" (Argument 14).

How much this denies animals is disputed. They lack thought, and all consciousness is thought, which seems to deny them feeling. But to Henry More on 5 February 1649 Descartes wrote that he does not deny them sensation in so far as it depends on a bodily organ (*quatenus ab organo corporeo dependet*, AT V 278). Cottingham ("'A Brute to the Brutes?' Descartes' Treatment of Animals," 1978) argues from such passages that he denied animals thought and reflective awareness, not all feeling. The traditional reading takes the denial of thought as a denial of feeling.

## The passions and generosity

*The Passions of the Soul* grew from the letters to Elisabeth and joins physiology to ethics. Passions are "the perceptions, feelings, or emotions of the soul which we relate specially to it, and which are caused, maintained, and fortified by some movement of the spirits" (*Passions* I.27, AT XI 349). Each disposes the soul to will what the composite needs, as fear disposes it to flee. They mislead when they exaggerate goods and evils. There are six primitive passions: wonder, love, hatred, desire, joy and sadness (II.69, AT XI 380). Wonder (*admiration*) comes first, the response to the new before we know whether it is good or bad, and it is the passion of inquiry.

The soul cannot stop a passion directly, but it can redirect it by attention and habit. Since custom can join each motion of the gland to thoughts other than those nature joined it to, "there is no soul so feeble that it cannot, if well directed, acquire an absolute power over its passions" (I.50, heading, AT XI 368). Descartes' example is the training of hunting dogs.

The centre of the ethics is **generosity** (*générosité*), both passion and virtue. The only good ground for self-esteem is "the use of our free will, and the empire which we possess over our wishes" (III.152, AT XI 445). "True generosity which causes a man to esteem himself as highly as he legitimately can" consists partly in knowing that nothing truly belongs to him "but this free disposition of his will," and partly in feeling "a firm and constant resolution to use it well, that is to say, never to fail of his own will to undertake and execute all the things which he judges to be the best—which is to follow perfectly after virtue" (III.153, AT XI 445–446). The generous do not despise others, believing that everyone has the same free will (III.154). Generosity is "so to speak the key of all other virtues, and a general remedy for all the disorders of the passions" (III.161, AT XI 454). The will of *Meditation* 4, where I most resemble God, turns out to be the one thing that is fully mine.

## Morality: provisional and late

Descartes never wrote the "highest and most perfect" morals the Preface promises. He left a provisional morality and a set of late letters.

**The provisional morality.** A man who pulls down his house to rebuild it needs somewhere to live meanwhile. So before doubting Descartes formed "a code of morals for the time being which did not consist of more than three or four maxims" (*Discourse* III, AT VI 22; *une morale par provision*). "The first was to obey the laws and customs of my country, adhering constantly to the religion in which by God's grace I had been instructed since my childhood," following elsewhere the most moderate opinions of the most sensible. The second was to be "as firm and resolute in my actions as I could be," like travellers lost in a forest who should keep walking in one direction (AT VI 24–25). The third was "to try always to conquer myself rather than fortune, and to alter my desires rather than change the order of the world," believing "that there is nothing entirely within our power but our own thoughts" (AT VI 25). The fourth, framed as a conclusion, was to spend his life cultivating his reason. The third maxim is close to the Stoic distinction between what is and is not up to us. Whether the code is temporary is disputed. Donald Rutherford ("Descartes' Ethics," *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*) reads the late ethics as its development, since the maxims return, revised, in the letters.

**The letters to Elisabeth, 1645–46.** In summer 1645 Elisabeth, ill and grieving, proposed reading Seneca's *De vita beata* together. On 4 August 1645 Descartes restated the maxims as three rules for contentment (AT IV 265–266). One must use the mind as well as one can to know what to do. One must have a firm and constant resolution (*vne ferme & constante resolution*, AT IV 265) to carry out what reason recommends, unswayed by passions, and this firmness is virtue. And one must bear in mind that goods not possessed are outside one's power, and so grow used to not desiring them. Virtue so understood suffices for contentment (*béatitude*). Elisabeth pressed the obvious difficulty: one cannot know everything relevant to acting well, and illness itself disturbs the reason one must use. Descartes answered that virtue requires only doing our best with what we can know, the ethical counterpart of the rule for judgment in *Meditation* 4.

## How the pieces interlock

The method requires an order of reasons. Doubt leaves only the thinking self, and the cogito models a clear and distinct perception. The truth rule generalises it, and the doctrine of ideas supplies materials for proving God. God, free creator even of the eternal truths, guarantees the rule. The will explains how a creature of such a God errs and how to stop. Substance and principal attribute make thought and extension two natures with nothing in common, which yields the real distinction. The union, known through the senses, explains why I am no pilot in my body. Extension as the whole of matter yields the plenum, the laws of motion and a mechanism covering animals and the human body. The passions show that mechanism at work inside the union, and the ethics returns to the will of *Meditation* 4 as the one thing we own. A good self-test is to take any doctrine and name the earlier one it depends on.

## Key terms

In the format of the repo [glossary](../../glossary.md), restricted to Descartes' usage, with misleading senses flagged.

- **Thought (*cogitatio*, *pensée*):** everything in us of which we are immediately aware: understanding, willing, imagining, sensing (Second Replies, Def. I, AT VII 160). Far wider than reasoning.
- **Cogito (*ego cogito, ergo sum*; *je pense, donc je suis*):** the first certainty: I am, I exist, whenever I think it. By Descartes' account not a syllogism (AT VII 140).
- **Hyperbolic doubt:** deliberate suspension, once in a life, of every belief open to the slightest doubt, for knowledge and not for action. Not skepticism as a conclusion.
- **Evil genius (*genius malignus*):** the deceiver supposed in *Meditation* 1 (AT VII 22). A hypothesis, not a belief; often "evil demon."
- **Intuition (*intuitus*):** in the *Rules*, the attentive mind's grasp of something simple and evident. Not a hunch.
- **Analysis and synthesis:** the two manners of proof (AT VII 155–156). Analysis shows how a result was found; synthesis derives it from axioms. The *Meditations* uses analysis.
- **Clear and distinct (*clara et distincta*):** present to an attentive mind; so separated from all else that it contains only what is clear (*Principles* I.45). Not a feeling of certainty.
- **Truth rule:** scholars' label for "all things which I perceive very clearly and very distinctly are true" (*Med.* 3, AT VII 35).
- **Natural light (*lumen naturale*):** the mind's power to see self-evident truths such as the causal principle. Distinct from the "teaching of nature," which can mislead.
- **Idea (*idea*):** a thought that is "so to speak" an image of a thing (AT VII 37). Not a mental picture: the idea of God has no image.
- **Formal and objective reality:** the reality a thing has by existing, and the reality an idea has by representing. "Objective" means "as an object of thought," almost the reverse of today's sense.
- **Eminently (*eminenter*):** containing a perfection in a higher form, as God contains extension without being extended.
- **Innate, adventitious, factitious ideas (*innatae*, *adventitiae*, *factae*):** from my nature, apparently from outside, made up by me (AT VII 37–38). Innate means a faculty to form the idea, not an idea present at birth.
- **Eternal truths (*veritates aeternae*):** mathematical truths and essences, freely created by God (letters to Mersenne, 1630, AT I 145–152).
- **Will (*voluntas*):** the power to affirm, deny, pursue and avoid; unlimited in scope and the chief image of God in us (AT VII 57). Judgment is its act.
- **Indifference (*indifferentia*):** being moved to neither side for want of reasons; "the lowest grade of liberty" (AT VII 58). Not freedom itself.
- **Substance (*substantia*):** a thing needing nothing but itself to exist; strictly only God, secondarily what needs only God's concurrence (*Principles* I.51–52).
- **Principal attribute:** the one property constituting a substance's nature: thought for mind, extension for body (*Principles* I.53).
- **Mode (*modus*):** a way a substance is, such as a shape or a volition, which cannot exist without it (I.56).
- **Real distinction (*distinctio realis*):** between substances each clearly and distinctly understood without the other (I.60). A technical term, not just "really different."
- **Primitive notions (*notions primitives*):** extension, thought, and their union, the patterns of all knowledge (to Elisabeth, 21 May 1643, AT III 665).
- **Animal spirits (*esprits animaux*):** the finest, fastest parts of the blood, moving through brain and nerves. Bodies, not souls.
- **Pineal gland (*conarium*):** the small single gland in the middle of the brain where the soul acts most immediately (*Passions* I.31).
- **Generosity (*générosité*):** rightful self-esteem for one's free will and the resolve to use it well; "the key of all other virtues" (*Passions* III.153, 161). Not open-handedness with money.
- **Provisional morality (*morale par provision*):** the maxims Descartes lived by while doubting (*Discourse* III, AT VI 22–25).

## Known internal tensions

The system strains at four joints. [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md) gives the history of each.

**(1) The circle.** Arnauld's Fourth Objections: "we can be sure that God exists, only because we clearly and evidently perceive that; therefore prior to being certain that God exists, we should be certain that whatever we clearly and evidently perceive is true" (AT VII 214). Descartes replied by separating present perception from memory. "We are sure that God exists because we have attended to the proofs that established this fact; but afterwards it is enough for us to remember that we have perceived something clearly, in order to be sure that it is true; but this would not suffice, unless we knew that God existed and that he did not deceive us" (Fourth Replies, AT VII 246). The memory reading takes this at face value; its critics note that the proof of God itself takes time. Harry Frankfurt (*Demons, Dreamers, and Madmen*, 1970) reads the guarantee as answering a doubt about the faculty, and as aiming at a belief system nothing could shake, not at truth beyond it. Argument 7 sets out the positions.

**(2) Interaction.** If the whole nature of mind is thought and of body extension, the union names a relation neither nature can contain. Elisabeth saw that the problem reaches the distinction itself: her remark that it would be easier to give the soul extension (AT III 685) questions the premise, not only the causal mechanism. Descartes' answer makes the union known to the senses while conceding it cannot be explained by the notions that define its parts. Malebranche's occasionalism, Spinoza's single substance and Leibniz's pre-established harmony each remove the problem in a different way; see the entries on [Malebranche](../../eras/03-early-modern/nicolas-malebranche.md), [Spinoza](../../eras/03-early-modern/baruch-spinoza.md) and [Leibniz](../../eras/03-early-modern/gottfried-wilhelm-leibniz.md).

**(3) Divine voluntarism against fixed essences.** The ontological argument needs existence to be inseparable from God's essence, and the physics needs the essence of matter to be extension. Yet the 1630 letters make essences God's free creation, and the 1648 letter declines to say God could not make a mountain without a valley. If necessity depends on God's will, nothing in the doctrine guarantees that what I cannot conceive otherwise could not be otherwise. Descartes' answer is that the truths are immutable because God's will is immutable, and that God made our minds unable to conceive their negation. Readers divide over what remains. Frankfurt ("Descartes on the Creation of the Eternal Truths," 1977) took Descartes to allow that God could have made contradictories true. Edwin Curley (1984) argued for a weaker reading: the eternal truths are necessary, but not necessarily necessary. Jean-Luc Marion (*Sur la théologie blanche de Descartes*, 1981) reads the doctrine as a rejection of the shared scale of being between God and creatures in the Jesuit scholasticism of [Suárez](../../eras/03-early-modern/suarez-and-grotius.md).

**(4) Freedom.** *Meditation* 4 says that the clearer the perception, the freer the assent, and that indifference is freedom's lowest grade. The Mesland letters seem to allow that we can withhold assent even from what we see clearly, to show our freedom. The texts leave open whether freedom requires the ability to do otherwise. Some readers take the Mesland letters to describe only a power to turn our attention away, which leaves *Meditation* 4 intact; Anthony Kenny ("Descartes on the Will," 1972) discusses the problem at length. Others see a change of view, perhaps prompted by the controversies over grace and freedom that followed Jansen's *Augustinus* (1640). The question matters because the theodicy of error and the ethics of generosity both rest on the will being truly ours, and because a will that cannot refuse a clear perception sits uneasily beside a God whose will is not bound even by the eternal truths. For where these positions sit among the repo's nine problems, see [themes.md](../../themes.md).
