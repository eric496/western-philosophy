# Descartes Received: Condemnation, Vortices, the Father of Modern Philosophy, and the Fights Still Open

*Focus study: René Descartes · [Dossier home](README.md) · [Library entry](../../eras/03-early-modern/rene-descartes.md) · [Study guide](../../study/descartes-meditations.md)*

Descartes was condemned by universities and churches within a generation, taught everywhere within two, and named the founder of modern philosophy by the nineteenth century. In the twentieth he became the figure most philosophers defined themselves against. This file tells that story by era, states six live debates with their named positions, and closes with the standard misconceptions in full form and the myths about the life. Quotations from Descartes and his objectors use the Haldane & Ross translation (1911–12) because it is public domain; the learning path's reading editions are Cottingham and Cress. Where Haldane & Ross's English misleads, the Latin or French term follows in brackets. Citations are to Adam–Tannery (AT) volume and page.

## The Cartesian schools and the condemnations, 1640–1700

The first fights started in Descartes' lifetime and in the Dutch universities. At Utrecht, Henricus Regius, professor of medicine, taught a Cartesian physiology and in a disputation of December 1641 called a human being an *ens per accidens*, a union of two things rather than one substance. The rector, the Calvinist theologian Gisbertus Voetius, saw atheism and heresy coming. In March 1642 the university senate condemned the "new philosophy" as contrary to the old and to orthodox theology. Descartes answered in the *Letter to Dinet*, printed with the second edition of the *Meditations* (1642), and in a long open letter to Voetius (1643). Leiden followed in 1647, when theologians accused him of Pelagianism and the curators banned discussion of his philosophy in lectures.

The Catholic condemnations came after his death in 1650 and turned on the Eucharist. The Council of Trent taught that after consecration the bread's substance is gone while its accidents remain. Descartes had no real accidents, only extended matter and its modes, and his attempt to explain transubstantiation through the bread's surface (Fourth Replies) satisfied few theologians. The theology faculty of Leuven censured Cartesian propositions in 1662. On 20 November 1663 the Congregation of the Index placed his works on the Index of Prohibited Books with the clause *donec corrigantur*, "until corrected." When his remains were brought from Stockholm to Paris in 1667, a royal order forbade the funeral oration. In 1671 the Archbishop of Paris, acting for Louis XIV, warned the University against teaching doctrines that departed from Aristotle's.

The bans did not stop the teaching. Claude Clerselier edited the letters (1657–67), and Jacques Rohault's *Traité de physique* (1671) made Cartesian physics a textbook. Louis de La Forge and Géraud de Cordemoy moved toward occasionalism, the view that God is the only true cause and bodily events are occasions for his action. [Nicolas Malebranche](../../eras/03-early-modern/nicolas-malebranche.md) gave occasionalism its classic form. The Port-Royal *Logic* of Arnauld and Nicole (1662) built Cartesian method into the most used logic of the century. The great system-builders treated Descartes as the problem to solve. [Spinoza](../../eras/03-early-modern/baruch-spinoza.md) began with a geometrical exposition of his *Principles* (1663) and ended with one substance. [Leibniz](../../eras/03-early-modern/gottfried-wilhelm-leibniz.md) attacked the Cartesian law of conservation of motion (1686) and answered the interaction problem with pre-established harmony. [Pascal](../../eras/03-early-modern/blaise-pascal.md) found him "useless and uncertain" in a fragment of the *Pensées*, and [Locke](../../eras/03-early-modern/john-locke.md) kept his "way of ideas" while rejecting innate ideas and the claim that the soul always thinks.

## Cartesian physics against Newton

For half a century Cartesian physics was the modern physics. Its central picture was the vortex: a plenum of matter in circular motion, carrying the planets around the sun. Bernard de Fontenelle's *Conversations on the Plurality of Worlds* (1686) made the vortices fashionable reading. Newton's *Principia* (1687) then argued at the end of Book II that a fluid vortex could not reproduce Kepler's laws of planetary motion, and replaced contact mechanics with universal gravitation. Cartesians, and Leibniz and Huygens too, objected that action at a distance was an occult quality of the kind mechanism had banished.

The dispute lasted into the 1730s. Rohault's textbook, translated into Latin by Samuel Clarke with Newtonian footnotes (1697 and later editions), taught Cambridge students Cartesian physics alongside its refutation. Maupertuis argued for Newton in France from 1732, and his expedition to Lapland (1736–37) measured the Earth as flattened at the poles, as Newton had predicted. Voltaire's *Philosophical Letters* (1734) contrasted a Paris full of vortices with a London that had none, and his *Elements of Newton's Philosophy* (1738) was written in close collaboration with [Émilie du Châtelet](../../eras/03-early-modern/emilie-du-chatelet.md), whose translation of the *Principia* followed. By mid-century the vortices were gone. The mechanist program survived them: nature described mathematically, without substantial forms or final causes.

## The Enlightenment image

The Enlightenment made Descartes a liberator with the wrong conclusions. D'Alembert's *Preliminary Discourse* to the *Encyclopédie* (1751) praised him for rising against scholastic authority and called his physics an error that cleared the way for better. Voltaire's verdict was similar: a great geometer and a romancer in physics. In Britain, after [Berkeley](../../eras/03-early-modern/george-berkeley.md) and [Hume](../../eras/03-early-modern/david-hume.md) had drawn skeptical consequences from the "way of ideas," [Thomas Reid](../../eras/03-early-modern/thomas-reid.md) blamed Descartes for putting ideas between the mind and the world. [Kant](../../eras/03-early-modern/immanuel-kant.md) named the "problematic idealism of Descartes" as the target of the Refutation of Idealism in the second edition of the *Critique of Pure Reason* (1787), the view that outer things are doubtful while inner experience is certain.

## The nineteenth century: founder and national hero

[Hegel](../../eras/04-19th-century/g-w-f-hegel.md)'s *Lectures on the History of Philosophy* gave Descartes his modern title. With him, Hegel said, philosophy finally starts from thinking itself, and he compared the arrival to a sailor sighting land after a long voyage. That story of a clean break, with medieval thought as the sea, shaped every textbook that followed. In France Victor Cousin edited the *Œuvres* in eleven volumes (1824–26) and made Descartes the founder of a national spiritualist philosophy, a counterweight to both materialism and German idealism. The tercentenary of 1896 produced the edition every scholar still cites: Charles Adam and Paul Tannery's *Œuvres de Descartes* (1897–1913). Elisabeth's side of the correspondence also appeared in this century, edited by Alexandre Foucher de Careil in 1879. For two centuries readers had seen Descartes' answers without her questions.

## The twentieth century: refounded, attacked, reconstructed

Phenomenology claimed him first. [Husserl](../../eras/05-20th-century/edmund-husserl.md) lectured at the Sorbonne in February 1929 and published the lectures as *Cartesian Meditations* (in French, 1931). He kept the method of starting from consciousness and suspending belief in the world, and rejected Descartes' step from the ego to a thinking substance and to God. [Heidegger](../../eras/05-20th-century/martin-heidegger.md) turned the inheritance into a charge. *Being and Time* (1927, §§19–21) argued that Descartes took the being of the world to be extension and left the being of the *sum*, the "I am," unexamined. "The Age of the World Picture" (lecture 1938) made him the founder of the modern subject who stands over against a world of objects. [Merleau-Ponty](../../eras/05-20th-century/maurice-merleau-ponty.md) set the lived body against the Cartesian split. [Foucault](../../eras/05-20th-century/michel-foucault.md)'s *History of Madness* (1961) read *Meditation* 1's quick dismissal of madness as reason excluding its other, and [Derrida](../../eras/05-20th-century/jacques-derrida.md)'s reply (1963) argued that the dream argument takes up the madness the first paragraph seemed to dismiss.

Analytic philosophy attacked the mind. [Gilbert Ryle](../../eras/05-20th-century/gilbert-ryle.md)'s *The Concept of Mind* (1949) attacked the "official doctrine" of a private inner theater and nicknamed it the ghost in the machine. Ryle called it a category mistake, a misreading of how mental concepts work. The [philosophy of mind that followed](../../eras/05-20th-century/philosophy-of-mind-after-ryle.md) was largely a search for alternatives to dualism.

Scholarship, meanwhile, rebuilt the texts. Martial Gueroult's *Descartes selon l'ordre des raisons* (1953) read the *Meditations* as a chain of reasons in which each step depends only on the steps before it. He took the principle from Descartes himself, who told Mersenne that he followed "the order of reasons" rather than "the order of subjects" (*l'ordre des raisons*, *l'ordre des matières*, letter of 24 December 1640, AT III 266). Ferdinand Alquié (1950) read the same texts as a lived metaphysical discovery, and the two debated at Royaumont in 1955. Étienne Gilson's *Index scolastico-cartésien* (1913) mapped Descartes' debts to the schools. In English, Anthony Kenny's *Descartes* (1968), Harry Frankfurt's *Demons, Dreamers, and Madmen* (1970), Margaret Wilson's *Descartes* (1978) and [Bernard Williams](../../eras/05-20th-century/bernard-williams.md)'s *Descartes: The Project of Pure Enquiry* (1978) set the agenda. Williams read the project as a search for an "absolute conception" of reality, a description of the world as it is independent of any observer's point of view.

## Feminist recovery: Elisabeth and the women of the circle

Feminist scholars reshaped the reception in two directions. One was critique. Genevieve Lloyd's *The Man of Reason* (1984) argued that Descartes meant reason to be equally available to all, and that his separation of pure thought from bodily and practical life nonetheless fitted a division of labor in which men did the thinking. Susan Bordo's *The Flight to Objectivity* (1987) read Cartesian objectivity as a cultural flight from embodiment and the feminine. The other direction was recovery. Eileen O'Neill's "Disappearing Ink" (1998) showed how women philosophers had dropped out of the histories. Elisabeth of Bohemia, once a footnote, is now read as the philosopher who found the weak point of his dualism.

Her letter of 6/16 May 1643 asked how the soul, being only a thinking substance (*n'estant qu'une substance pensante*), can determine the bodily spirits to produce voluntary action, when all determination of motion seems to require contact or extension (AT III 661). On 10/20 June she wrote that she would find it easier to grant matter and extension to the soul than to grant an immaterial being the capacity to move a body and be moved by it (AT III 685). Descartes' replies (21 May and 28 June 1643) called the union a third primitive notion, alongside thought and extension, and said it is learned "by living and ordinary conversation" (*la vie et des conversations ordinaires*, AT III 692, my translation) rather than by metaphysics. The 1645 letters on the passions and the good life led to *The Passions of the Soul* (1649). Lisa Shapiro's edition of the correspondence (2007) made the whole exchange a standard text; the [library entry on Elisabeth and her contemporaries](../../eras/03-early-modern/early-modern-women-philosophers.md) gives her positions.

The wider circle included Queen Christina, who brought him to Stockholm in 1649, and Anna Maria van Schurman, the most learned woman in the Dutch Republic, who belonged to Voetius' Utrecht and took his side. Cartesian method also gave early feminism an argument. François Poullain de la Barre's *De l'égalité des deux sexes* (1673) treated the belief in women's inferiority as a prejudice that methodic doubt should clear away, and Mary Astell's *Serious Proposal to the Ladies* (1694) drew on Descartes and Malebranche to argue that women's minds deserved the same training as men's.

## Damasio's *Descartes' Error*

The best-known recent use of Descartes outside philosophy is the neuroscientist Antonio Damasio's *Descartes' Error: Emotion, Reason, and the Human Brain* (1994). From patients with damage to the ventromedial prefrontal cortex, whose intelligence was intact but whose practical decisions collapsed, Damasio argued that emotion, through bodily "somatic markers," is part of good reasoning. The error he named was the separation of mind from body and of reason from feeling, which he summed up in the cogito. Historians replied that the target is closer to a later "Cartesianism" than to Descartes. The *Passions* treats emotions as states of the mind–body union with bodily causes and practical functions, and the Sixth Meditation says that nature teaches "that I am not only lodged in my body as a pilot in a vessel, but that I am very closely united to it" (*Med.* 6, AT VII 81).

## Live debates

**(1) The Cartesian circle.** Arnauld put it in the Fourth Objections: Descartes says we can be sure that what we clearly and distinctly perceive is true only because God exists, but we can be sure God exists only because we clearly perceive it (AT VII 214). Mersenne's Second Objections had raised a version already. Descartes' answer points back to the Second Replies: "There I distinguished those matters that in actual truth we clearly perceive from those we remember to have formerly perceived" (Fourth Replies, AT VII 245–46). The main readings divide over what God is needed for. The **memory reading** (Willis Doney, 1955) says God guarantees only remembered demonstrations, so present clear perception needs no guarantee; its problem is that the deceiver of *Meditation* 1 seems to threaten present perceptions of arithmetic too. The **general-rule reading** (Kenny, 1968) separates particular clear perceptions, which cannot be doubted while attended to, from the general principle that whatever is so perceived is true, which God secures. The **consistency reading** (Frankfurt, 1970) says Descartes aims to show that reason does not undermine itself, so that no clear perception yields a reason to doubt clear perception. It cites the Second Replies' indifference to any falsity beyond perfect conviction: "What heed do we pay to that absolute falsity, when we by no means believe that it exists or even suspect its existence?" (AT VII 145). Critics answer that Descartes plainly claims truth, not just coherence. **Epistemic-principle readings** (James Van Cleve, 1979) let clear perception confer certainty before God is known, with God needed for the stable knowledge Descartes calls *scientia*. Descartes supports that distinction when he says an atheist geometer has clear knowledge but not true *scientia* (AT VII 141). The current state: few now think the argument is a simple logical blunder, and there is no agreement on which repair Descartes intended.

**(2) The cogito: inference, intuition, or performance?** Descartes denies that it is a syllogism. One who says "I think, hence I am, or exist," he writes, "does not deduce existence from thought by a syllogism, but, by a simple act of mental vision, recognises it as if it were a thing that is known per se" (Second Replies, AT VII 140; the Latin is *simplici mentis intuitu*). Yet the *Discourse* (AT VI 32–33) and the *Principles* (I.7, AT VIIIA) use "therefore," and *Principles* I.10 says the general premise that a thinking thing must exist was known. The **inference reading** holds that some premise is used, if only implicitly. The **intuition reading**, the entry's and the study guide's, takes the denial at face value. The **performance reading** (Hintikka, 1962) says "I do not exist" is existentially inconsistent: saying or thinking it makes it false, which is why the *Meditations* make the certainty hold "each time" it is pronounced or conceived. Critics of Hintikka note that the cogito works from any thought, not only from asserting, a point Descartes makes himself against Gassendi: "you have no right to make the inference: I walk, hence I exist, except in so far as our awareness of walking is a thought" (Fifth Replies, AT VII 352). A further objection asks whether "I" is earned at all. Lichtenberg suggested that we should say "it thinks" as we say "it lightens," and Williams (1978) put the question as whether the evidence shows more than that there is thinking.

**(3) The real distinction.** *Meditation* 6 argues that because I can clearly and distinctly conceive mind without body, God could make them exist apart, so they are really distinct. Arnauld's counterexample in the Fourth Objections (AT VII 201–2) is a man who clearly knows a triangle is right-angled while denying Pythagoras' theorem; clear conception of one feature without another does not show they can be separated. Descartes replied that mind is conceived as a complete thing and the triangle's property is not. Modern readers divide on whether conceivability is any guide to possibility. Kripke and David Chalmers defend versions of the inference; physicalists reject it. Marleen Rozemond's *Descartes's Dualism* (1998) argued that the argument rests on the scholastic premise that each substance has one principal attribute, which makes it stronger in context and less general in reach.

**(4) Trialism or dualism?** John Cottingham's "Cartesian Trialism" (*Mind*, 1985) took the three primitive notions of the letter of 21 May 1643 (AT III 665) as three categories: thought, extension, and the union, to which sensations and passions belong. On this reading Descartes is a dualist about substance but a trialist about attributes. Paul Hoffman ("The Unity of Descartes's Man," 1986) went further and read the soul as the form of the body, on the scholastic model, citing Descartes' advice to Regius in January 1642 to call a human being an *ens per se*. Rozemond and others defend the plain dualist reading, on which the union is a close causal relation between two substances. The live question is whether Descartes' talk of union is metaphysics or a description of experience.

**(5) Did Descartes hold that animals feel?** The standard reading, the *bête-machine*, says animals are automata without thought and therefore without feeling. The *Discourse* argues that animals lack reason and that "it is nature which acts in them according to the disposition of their organs" (*Discourse* V, AT VI 59). Revisionists, beginning with Cottingham's "'A Brute to the Brutes?'" (1978), point to texts that allow animals something. To Henry More, Descartes wrote that he denied animals thought, not life, and did not deny them "sensation, in so far as it depends on a bodily organ" (*nec denego etiam sensum, quatenus ab organo corporeo dependet*, 5 February 1649, AT V 278, my translation). The Sixth Replies place "the first grade of perception" in cerebral motion "which is common to us and to the brutes" (AT VII 437). Defenders of the standard reading, which is how Malebranche and most Cartesians took him, answer that for Descartes pain as felt is a mode of thought, so "sensation" in an animal can only mean its bodily stage. The current state: Descartes clearly denied animals thought and language; whether he denied them any feeling is contested, and the texts may not be consistent.

**(6) Sincerity and the Galileo affair.** In late November 1633 Descartes told Mersenne that Galileo's condemnation had so astonished him that he had almost resolved to burn his papers, or at least to show them to no one (AT I 270–71). He suppressed *Le Monde*. In April 1634 he gave Mersenne his motto, *bene vixit, bene qui latuit*, "he lived well who hid well" (AT I 286). An early notebook has him go forward "masked," *larvatus prodeo* (AT X 213). Maxime Leroy's *Descartes, le philosophe au masque* (1929) and later readers in [Leo Strauss](../../eras/05-20th-century/leo-strauss.md)'s line, Richard Kennington and Hiram Caton among them, read the theology as cover for a secular, even atheist, project. Henri Gouhier (1924), Gilson and Stephen Menn (*Descartes and Augustine*, 1998) read it as sincere and structural, since God's veracity carries the whole system. The library entry judges that the texts support the keystone reading while the pressure was real, and that remains the majority view.

## Misconceptions, in full form

- **"The cogito is a syllogism."** Descartes denied it in the Second Replies: the cogito is known by "a simple act of mental vision" (AT VII 140), and the general premise is learned from the particular case, not the other way round. The denial does not end the debate, since the *Discourse* and *Principles* use "therefore." What is settled is that he did not rest it on a major premise the deceiver could attack.

- **"Descartes was a sceptic."** He used skeptical arguments, more radical than the skeptics' own, as tools to find what survives them. The *Meditations* end with the external world restored.

- **"Dualism means mind and body never interact."** Descartes held that they interact constantly and form a union. Voluntary movement and sensation are his evidence. What he left unexplained is how a non-extended mind moves extended matter, which is Elisabeth's question. Interaction is denied by occasionalists and by Leibniz, his successors.

- **"Descartes said animals feel no pain."** He denied animals thought and language. Whether he denied them all feeling is disputed (see debate 5), and his letter to More expressly allows sensation that depends on bodily organs. The stories of Cartesians kicking dogs and vivisecting animals while dismissing their cries come from later memoirs about Port-Royal and from a tale about Malebranche, not from Descartes.

- **"Cartesian means cold and disembodied."** *The Passions of the Soul* is an account of emotion as a state of the embodied person, with a physiology, a classification of six primitive passions, and an ethics of using them well. The letters to Elisabeth on grief, health and the good life are the same embodied Descartes.

- **"Descartes started philosophy from scratch."** The method presents itself as a fresh start; the materials were old. The skeptical arguments came from the revived Pyrrhonism of [Montaigne](../../eras/03-early-modern/michel-de-montaigne.md) and others. The vocabulary of substance, mode and objective reality came from the schools, as Gilson's *Index* shows, and Descartes learned it from the Jesuits at La Flèche and from [Suárez](../../eras/03-early-modern/suarez-and-grotius.md)'s tradition. The trademark argument reworks scholastic causal principles, and the ontological argument has a predecessor in [Anselm](../../eras/02-medieval/anselm-of-canterbury.md). When a correspondent pointed out [Augustine](../../eras/02-medieval/augustine-of-hippo.md)'s "if I am deceived, I am" (*si fallor, sum*, *City of God* XI.26), Descartes looked it up in the town library. He replied that Augustine used it to prove the certainty of our being and then to find an image of the Trinity in the soul, while he used it to show that the thinking "I" is an immaterial substance (letter of November 1640, AT III 247–48).

- **"'I think, therefore I am' is the argument of the *Meditations*."** The phrase is the *Discourse*'s French *je pense, donc je suis* (AT VI 32–33). The Latin *ego cogito, ergo sum* appears in the *Principles* (I.7), and the *Meditations* say only "I am, I exist," which "is necessarily true each time that I pronounce it, or that I mentally conceive it" (*Med.* 2, AT VII 25).

## Myths about the life

- **The stove.** The *Discourse* says that in the winter of 1619 in Germany he "remained the whole day shut up alone in a stove-heated room" (AT VI 11). The French word is *poêle*, a room heated by a stove. Popular retellings put him inside the stove. The three dreams of the night of 10 November 1619 come from a lost notebook, the *Olympica*, known through Baillet's *Life* (1691).
- **The fly on the ceiling.** The story that he invented coordinate geometry by watching a fly on his ceiling has no source in his writings or Baillet.
- **The poisoning.** He died in Stockholm on 11 February 1650, and the reports of Chanut and Baillet describe a lung fever. Eike Pies (1996) published a letter of the physician Johan van Wullen describing the illness, and Theodor Ebert (2009) argued that the symptoms fit arsenic given in a communion wafer by the chaplain François Viogué, to remove an obstacle to Christina's conversion. Most historians find the case unproven.
- **Francine the automaton.** His daughter Francine, with Helena Jans van der Strom, was real: she was born at Deventer in July 1635 and died at Amersfoort in September 1640. The legend that he later carried a mechanical doll made in her likeness, which a ship's captain threw overboard, has no source from his lifetime. Minsoo Kang's study of it (2017) treats it as an intellectual fable and traces how it grew in print long after his death.
