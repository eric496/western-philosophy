# Works and Texts: The Corpus in Reading Order

*Focus study: Descartes · [Dossier home](README.md) · Library entry: [René Descartes](../../eras/03-early-modern/rene-descartes.md) · Study guide: [*Meditations*](../../study/descartes-meditations.md) · Arguments in full: [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md)*

Descartes' bibliography is small at the centre and large at the edges. In his lifetime he published four books: the *Discourse on the Method* with its three scientific essays (1637), the *Meditations* with the Objections and Replies (1641), the *Principles of Philosophy* (1644) and the *Passions of the Soul* (1649). Around them sit a few polemical pieces, a set of works he wrote and never published, and a correspondence that fills five volumes of the standard edition. Most of what he did not publish reached print through one man, his friend and translator Claude Clerselier, who received the papers after Descartes died.

Two features shape how this corpus is read. The first is language: Descartes wrote in Latin and in French, and the key works exist in both, in versions that differ. The second is the dialogue form of the central work. The *Meditations* was printed with seven sets of objections and Descartes' replies, so the book's defence of itself is part of the book.

## A note on citation before the works

Descartes is cited by **Adam–Tannery (AT)** volume and page. Charles Adam and Paul Tannery edited the *Œuvres de Descartes* for the French Ministry of Public Instruction, published in Paris by Léopold Cerf from 1897 to 1913 in twelve volumes and a supplement. A revised edition, published by Vrin from 1964, keeps the original page numbers, so a citation works in either. Every serious modern translation prints AT pages in its margin, as Aristotle's translations print Bekker numbers.

The volumes: **I–V** the correspondence in date order (I runs to February 1638, III covers January 1640 to June 1643, V ends in February 1650); **VI** the *Discourse* and essays; **VII** the Latin *Meditations* with the Objections and Replies; **VIIIA** the Latin *Principles* (VIIIB the polemical letters); **IX** the French versions, IX-1 the *Méditations* and IX-2 the *Principes*; **X** the early papers, the *Rules* and the *Search after Truth*; **XI** *Le Monde*, the *Treatise on Man* and the *Passions*.

The dossier's form is *work + part or article + AT volume and page*: (*Med.* 2, AT VII 25), (*Disc.* IV, AT VI 32), (*Princ.* I.7, AT VIIIA). Abbreviations: *Disc.*, *Med.*, *Obj.* and *Rep.* (with the set number), *Princ.* (part and article), *Pass.* (part and article), *Rules*, *Search*. Letters are cited by correspondent, date and AT page.

The standard English translation is **Cottingham, Stoothoff and Murdoch**, *The Philosophical Writings of Descartes* (Cambridge, 1984–85), cited as **CSM I** and **CSM II**. Volume III, the letters, added Anthony Kenny and is cited as **CSMK** (1991). Many scholars cite AT and CSM together. This dossier cites AT alone, because AT is in the margins of every edition the reader will use.

## Latin and French, and why the difference matters

Descartes chose his language by audience. He wrote the *Discourse* in French, and said why at its close. In Haldane and Ross's translation: "And if I write in French which is the language of my country, rather than in Latin which is that of my teachers," it is because he hopes readers who use only their natural reason will judge him better than readers who trust only old books (*Disc.* VI, AT VI 77). The *Meditations* and *Principles* he wrote in Latin, for the learned world and the schools.

Each major work then crossed over. The *Discourse* went into Latin in 1644 as the *Specimina philosophiae*, translated by Étienne de Courcelles, without the *Geometry*; Descartes revised it. The *Meditations* and *Principles* went into French in 1647. The two directions differ in authority. Adam's own advice in AT IX-2 is a fair summary: the *Discourse* can be read in either language; for the *Meditations* the Latin keeps priority, though the French was revised with Descartes' knowledge; the *Principles* must be read in both, because the French adds and changes too much to ignore and is also less exact.

Three cases show why the reader should care.

1. **The cogito's wording.** "Je pense, donc je suis" is French, from the *Discourse* (AT VI 32). The Latin "ego cogito, ergo sum" is in the *Principles* (I.7, AT VIIIA). The *Meditations* never says either. It says "ego sum, ego existo" (AT VII 25): in Haldane and Ross, "I am, I exist, is necessarily true each time that I pronounce it, or that I mentally conceive it." The absence of "therefore" there matters to the dispute, set out in [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), over whether the cogito is an inference.
2. **The list of thoughts.** In *Meditation* 2 the Latin calls the thinking thing "dubitans, intelligens, affirmans" and so on (AT VII 28). The French of 1647 has "qui doute, qui conçoit, qui affirme" (AT IX-1 22). Haldane and Ross give both, the French in square brackets: "It is a thing which doubts, understands, [conceives], affirms, denies, wills, refuses, which also imagines and feels." Their preface explains that brackets mark readings from the French.
3. **The word "thought" itself.** The Latin *cogitatio* and the French *pensée* cover every conscious state: doubting, willing, imagining, feeling. The *Principles* says so outright (I.9, AT VIIIA). English "thought" suggests reasoning only. Haldane and Ross sometimes follow the freer French. In *Meditation* 3 they write "I always perceive something as the subject of the action of my mind" (AT VII 37), and a note says that "action de mon esprit" replaces the Latin *mea cogitatio*. A reader of that sentence would not know the Latin word was "my thought". This is the inconsistency the [learning path](../../learning-path.md) holds against their version.

## The published works

### The *Discourse on the Method* and the three essays (1637)

The book appeared at Leiden from the press of Jan Maire in 1637, without the author's name. Its full title presents the *Discourse* as a preface to three essays: the *Dioptrics* (light, lenses and vision), the *Meteors* (weather, rainbows, salt) and the *Geometry*, where Descartes set out the joining of algebra to geometry. The *Discourse* has six parts, taken below in Weeks 1 and 6; Part VI explains why he publishes at all, after holding back his physics. The *Geometry* reached Latin separately, in Frans van Schooten's edition (Leiden, 1649).

### The *Meditations on First Philosophy* (1641, 1642, 1647)

The first edition came out in Paris from Michel Soly; printing was completed on 28 August 1641. Its title promised to demonstrate "the existence of God and the immortality of the soul" (*Dei existentia et animae immortalitas*) and claimed the theologians' approval, which Descartes sought from the Paris Faculty of Theology and never obtained. A dedicatory letter to the Faculty, a Preface to the Reader and the Synopsis come first, then the six Meditations, about a fifth of the volume, then six sets of Objections and Replies.

The second edition (Amsterdam, Louis Elzevir, 1642) made two changes. The title now promised "the distinction of the human soul from the body" (*animae humanae a corpore distinctio*, my translation) in place of immortality, a more accurate description of what *Meditation* 6 proves. And it added the Seventh Objections, by the Jesuit Pierre Bourdin, with Descartes' notes, and his Letter to Father Dinet, Bourdin's superior, complaining of Bourdin. It also printed in full the end of the Fourth Replies, cut in 1641 (see below).

The French version appeared in Paris in 1647. The title page credits two translators by initials: the Duc de Luynes for the Meditations and Clerselier for the Objections and Replies. Descartes saw and approved the translations, so the French counts as a source, and some passages expand what the Latin compresses. The edition omits the Seventh Objections and the Letter to Dinet. Descartes asked Clerselier not to translate Gassendi's objections, because Gassendi had meanwhile republished them with long rejoinders (the *Disquisitio metaphysica*, 1644). Clerselier translated them anyway and printed them at the end of the volume, with a letter of Descartes dated 12 January 1646 answering a summary of Gassendi's rejoinders. Clerselier's revised French edition of 1661 added the Seventh Objections and the Dinet letter, after Descartes' death.

The Preface to the Reader says how the book is to be read. In Haldane and Ross: "I should never advise anyone to read it excepting those who desire to meditate seriously with me" (AT VII 9). The study guide's advice to read the book slowly, first person, as an exercise, comes from here.

### The *Principles of Philosophy* (1644, 1647)

The Latin *Principia philosophiae* appeared at Amsterdam from Elzevir in 1644, in four parts divided into numbered articles, each with a one-line summary. Part I restates the metaphysics; II gives the principles of material things; III the visible universe and the vortices; IV the earth. Descartes meant it as a textbook to replace the scholastic courses, and he dedicated it to Princess Elisabeth of Bohemia.

The French *Principes* followed in Paris in 1647 (Henri Le Gras), translated by "one of his friends", the abbé Claude Picot. Descartes added a letter to the translator to serve as a preface, on what philosophy is and how to read the book. It contains the image of the tree. In Haldane and Ross: "Thus philosophy as a whole is like a tree whose roots are metaphysics, whose trunk is physics, and whose branches, which issue from this trunk, are all the other sciences" (AT IX-2 14). The branches reduce to medicine, mechanics and morals, and the fruit, he adds, is picked from the branches. The image states the order of the system that [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md) lays out.

### The *Passions of the Soul* (1649)

The last book Descartes published, written in French, appeared late in 1649, when he had already left for Sweden. It grew out of the correspondence with Elisabeth, who asked him in 1645 to define the passions. It has three parts and 212 articles. Part I gives the physiology of the union, including the pineal gland; Part II derives the passions from six primitives (wonder, love, hatred, desire, joy, sadness); Part III treats the particular passions and ends with generosity as the key to virtue.

The polemical pieces stay off the reading list: the *Letter to Voetius* (1643), against the Utrecht theologian, and the *Comments on a Certain Broadsheet* (1648), against his former follower Henricus Regius (AT VIIIB).

## The Objections and Replies

Before publication Descartes sent the manuscript of the *Meditations* to Marin Mersenne in Paris, who circulated it and collected responses. The 1641 edition prints six sets, and names only one critic, Gassendi; the others appear as "a theologian", "a philosopher", "a very subtle theologian". Their authors are known:

1. **First:** Johannes Caterus, a Catholic priest at Alkmaar, writing as a Thomist, on the causal and ontological arguments.
2. **Second:** "theologians and philosophers" whose points Mersenne gathered. The Replies end by setting out the main arguments "in the geometrical manner", as definitions, postulates, axioms and propositions.
3. **Third:** Thomas Hobbes, then in Paris, with Descartes' replies after each point. The exchange is short and unsympathetic on both sides.
4. **Fourth:** Antoine Arnauld, a young theologian, later the leading figure of Port-Royal, on the circle and the real distinction. The end of the reply, on how the physics fits the Eucharist, was cut in 1641 with Descartes' consent, in hope of the theologians' approval, and restored in 1642.
5. **Fifth:** Pierre Gassendi, the longest set, from an empiricist and Epicurean standpoint.
6. **Sixth:** further theologians and philosophers, again gathered by Mersenne.
7. **Seventh** (1642 only): the Jesuit Pierre Bourdin, with Descartes' notes interleaved.

The Replies are where Descartes says what he meant: that the cogito is no syllogism (Second), how the circle is avoided (Fourth, with the Second), how God can be understood without being grasped (First).

## The posthumous works

When Descartes died in Stockholm on 11 February 1650, his papers were inventoried there. The French ambassador Pierre Chanut took charge of them and passed them to his brother-in-law Clerselier; Baillet's *Life* (1691) says they reached France only in 1653. Clerselier published what he could. Many of the manuscripts were later lost, so several works survive only in copies or in early printings.

- ***Le Monde*** (*The World*, or *Treatise on Light*), written about 1629–33. Descartes had nearly finished it when he learned in November 1633 of Galileo's condemnation in Rome, and he withheld it, since it assumed the earth's motion. It was published in Paris in 1664. The physics it held went, in revised form, into *Discourse* V and *Principles* II–IV.
- ***Treatise on Man***, the physiological part of the same project: the body as a machine of nerves and animal spirits. It appeared first in Latin translation (Leiden, 1662), then in French in 1664.
- ***Rules for the Direction of the Mind*** (*Regulae*), an unfinished Latin treatise of the late 1620s that breaks off after Rule 21. It survived in copies, appeared in Jan Glazemaker's Dutch translation in 1684 and in Latin in the *Opuscula posthuma* (Amsterdam, 1701). AT X 359ff.
- ***The Search after Truth***, an unfinished French dialogue in which Eudoxus, the voice of natural reason, guides the untaught Polyander while the learned Epistemon objects. Part of the French survives in a copy made for Leibniz; the whole is known from the Latin of 1701. Its date is disputed, between the early 1640s and much earlier.
- ***Conversation with Burman***. On 16 April 1648, at Egmond, the student Frans Burman questioned Descartes passage by passage on the *Meditations*, *Principles* and *Discourse*. Johannes Clauberg wrote the answers out at Amsterdam on 20 April, from Burman's notes or with him. The only text is a copy made at Dordrecht that July, now at Göttingen (AT V 146ff). Adam printed it in the type he kept for documents *about* Descartes, since the words are Burman's. Scholars cite it widely on the circle and the cogito; the dossier treats it as testimony and rests no argument on it alone.

## The correspondence

Mersenne was the hub of Descartes' correspondence, passing letters and queries between him and learned Europe. Clerselier published the first collection, *Lettres de Mr Descartes*, in three volumes (Paris, 1657, 1659 and 1667), often from drafts and sometimes misdated. AT I–V re-sorts everything by date and adds letters found later.

The exchange with **Elisabeth of Bohemia** (1618–80), the exiled Palatine princess at The Hague, runs from 1643 to 1649. Clerselier printed Descartes' side. Hers survived in copies at Rosendael near Arnhem and was first published by Foucher de Careil in 1879. The letters of 1643 are the core text for Week 5:

- Elisabeth to Descartes, 6/16 May 1643 (AT III 660–2). Two dates are given because Elisabeth dated by the old Julian calendar, ten days behind the Gregorian one Descartes used. She asks how the soul of a man can move the bodily spirits to perform voluntary actions, "n'estant qu'vne substance pensante", being only a thinking substance (AT III 661, my translation). Her reason: every motion seems to need contact or extension, and he excludes extension from the soul.
- Descartes to Elisabeth, 21 May 1643 (AT III 663ff): the three primitive notions, of soul, of body, and of their union.
- Elisabeth to Descartes, 10/20 June 1643 (AT III 683ff): she finds it easier to grant the soul matter and extension than to grant an immaterial being the power to move a body.
- Descartes to Elisabeth, 28 June 1643 (AT III 690ff): the union is known best through the senses and ordinary life, not through metaphysics.

The letters of 1645, from his of 4 August (AT IV), turn to Seneca, virtue and the passions. Two other letters recur in the dossier: to Mersenne, late November 1633 (AT I), on Galileo; and to the Marquess of Newcastle, 23 November 1646 (AT IV), on animals.

## The works in the dossier's reading order

### Week 1. *Discourse* I–IV and *Rules* 1–4

The *Discourse* opens with a sentence whose irony is easy to miss, in Haldane and Ross: "Good sense is of all things in the world the most equally distributed" (*Disc.* I, AT VI 1). Everyone has enough reason; what differs is method. Part I is autobiography and the verdict on his schooling; II the four rules; III the provisional morality; IV the cogito, "I think, therefore I am" in Haldane and Ross (AT VI 32), with short proofs of God and the soul. *Rules* 1–4 (AT X 359ff) give the earlier, fuller doctrine of intuition, deduction and one universal science. **Difficulty:** low; the *Rules* moderate. **Place:** the method and the first cogito, before the *Meditations* make them rigorous.

### Weeks 2–4. *Meditations* 1–5, with Objections and Replies

**Week 2:** the Synopsis and *Meditations* 1–2, from the dreaming argument and the demon to the cogito and the wax. **Week 3:** *Meditation* 3, then Arnauld on the circle with the Fourth Replies, and the Second Replies on clear and distinct perception and on why the cogito is no syllogism. **Week 4:** *Meditations* 4–5, then Caterus and Gassendi on the ontological argument, with the replies. The learning path's sequence skips *Meditation* 4; the dossier reads it, as the study guide advises, because its account of the will underlies the theory of error and the truth rule. **Difficulty:** 1–2 moderate; 3 high, for its scholastic vocabulary of formal and objective reality; 4–5 moderate; the Replies high. **Place:** the core. Most of the fifteen arguments in [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md) come from these weeks.

### Week 5. *Meditation* 6, Elisabeth, and *Passions* I

*Meditation* 6 gives the real distinction, the divisibility argument, the proof of the external world and the union. Elisabeth's letters of 1643 put the interaction problem to it. *Passions* I, arts. 30–50, give the late account of the union: the soul joined to the whole body, its principal seat in the pineal gland, the will acting on the passions. **Difficulty:** *Meditation* 6 high; the letters and *Passions* moderate. **Place:** the payoff of the *Meditations* and the objection that set the next generation's agenda; see [early modern women philosophers](../../eras/03-early-modern/early-modern-women-philosophers.md).

### Week 6. *Principles* I and II, and *Discourse* V

The *Principles* I selections restate the metaphysics as theses and define substance, attribute and mode. The Part II selections lay the physics' foundations: body is extension, so there is no vacuum; motion is transfer from one neighbourhood to another; three laws of nature follow from God's constancy. *Discourse* V summarises the physics of *Le Monde* and gives the two tests, language and versatile action, that would tell a human-shaped machine from a human. **Difficulty:** moderate. **Place:** the trunk of the tree.

### Week 7. Hobbes's Third Objections, and the heirs

In the Third Objections and Replies, [Hobbes](../../eras/03-early-modern/thomas-hobbes.md) and Descartes fail to agree on what an idea is and whether a body could think. The week then takes the heirs in summary, through [05-conversations.md](05-conversations.md) and the entries on [Malebranche](../../eras/03-early-modern/nicolas-malebranche.md), [Spinoza](../../eras/03-early-modern/baruch-spinoza.md), [Leibniz](../../eras/03-early-modern/gottfried-wilhelm-leibniz.md) and [Locke](../../eras/03-early-modern/john-locke.md). **Difficulty:** low to moderate. **Place:** the system under a materialist's attack, and the capstone.

## Editions and translations

The rule is the learning path's: Cottingham or Cress for reading, and not Haldane and Ross. Stay with one translation through each work, because Descartes' technical terms vary between translators.

**The reference set** is CSM I–II and CSMK (Cambridge, 1984–91). Volume I has the *Rules*, the *Discourse*, much of the *Principles* and an abridged *Passions*; volume II the *Meditations*, the Objections and Replies and the *Search after Truth*; CSMK the letters in selection and the *Conversation with Burman*.

**The course texts:**

- **Week 1:** the *Discourse* in Donald Cress's translation (Hackett) or Desmond Clarke's *Discourse on Method and Related Writings* (Penguin, 1999); the *Rules* in CSM I.
- **Weeks 2–4:** the *Meditations* in John Cottingham's *Meditations on First Philosophy, with Selections from the Objections and Replies* (Cambridge), or in Cress. Use CSM II for anything Cottingham's selection omits.
- **Week 5:** the Elisabeth letters in **Lisa Shapiro (ed. and trans.), *The Correspondence between Princess Elisabeth of Bohemia and René Descartes*** (Chicago, 2007), which prints both sides; the *Passions* in CSM I, or complete in Stephen Voss's translation (Hackett, 1989).
- **Week 6:** *Principles* I–II in CSM I, with V. R. and R. P. Miller's complete translation (Reidel, 1983) for omitted articles; *Discourse* V in Cress or Clarke.
- **Week 7:** the Third Objections in CSM II or Cottingham's selection; the Newcastle letter in CSMK.

**Avoid, as a reading text,** Elizabeth Haldane and G. R. T. Ross, *The Philosophical Works of Descartes* (Cambridge, 1911–12), as the learning path rules, for its inconsistent *cogitatio* and its mixing of Latin and French readings. Avoid John Veitch's older translations of the *Discourse* (1850) and *Meditations* (1853), the text of many free reprints. Avoid any edition without AT numbers in the margin.

**A note on quotation.** The dossier quotes Descartes in English from Haldane and Ross, the version it tells you not to read. The reason is checking: Haldane and Ross is in the public domain and free online, so every quotation can be verified word for word, while Cottingham and Cress are in copyright. Each quotation carries its AT page, so you can find it in your own edition. Where the English misleads, the Latin or French follows in brackets, above all for *cogitatio* and *pensée*. Elisabeth's letters have no public-domain translation: her arguments are paraphrased, and her few French words carry the dossier's own translation, marked as such.

## The reading order

1. **Week 1, method:** *Discourse* I–IV; *Rules* 1–4.
2. **Week 2, doubt and the cogito:** Synopsis; *Meditations* 1–2.
3. **Week 3, God and the circle:** *Meditation* 3; Fourth Objections and Replies; Second Replies.
4. **Week 4, error and essence:** *Meditations* 4–5; First and Fifth Objections and Replies on the ontological argument.
5. **Week 5, mind and body:** *Meditation* 6; Elisabeth correspondence of 1643, with 1645 selections; *Passions* I, arts. 30–50.
6. **Week 6, matter and mechanism:** *Principles* I (arts. 1–13, 51–54, 63) and II (arts. 1–4, 10–18, 23–25, 36–40); *Discourse* V.
7. **Week 7, critics and heirs:** Third Objections and Replies (Hobbes); the heirs in summary; the capstone.
