## The figure in brief

Descartes was born in 1596 at La Haye in Touraine, into a family of lawyers and minor nobility, and was schooled by the Jesuits at La Flèche in the Aristotelian philosophy he would spend his life replacing. After a law degree at Poitiers he travelled and soldiered, and in 1618 at Breda he met Isaac Beeckman, who turned him toward mathematical physics. In November 1619, by his own account, a winter day alone in a stove-heated room in Germany gave him the plan of rebuilding knowledge on foundations of his own. From 1628 he lived in the Dutch Republic, moving often, writing to the learned world through Marin Mersenne in Paris. He suppressed his physics, *Le Monde*, when Galileo was condemned in 1633, and published instead the *Discourse on the Method* (1637), the *Meditations on First Philosophy* (1641), printed with seven sets of objections and his replies, the *Principles of Philosophy* (1644), and the *Passions of the Soul* (1649). From 1643 he corresponded with Princess Elisabeth of Bohemia, his sharpest critic. He went to Stockholm at Queen Christina's invitation in 1649 and died there on 11 February 1650.

The ideas this library keeps meeting begin with him: doubt used as a method, the cogito as the first certainty, clear and distinct perception as the mark of truth, God proved from the idea of God, the real distinction between a thinking mind and an extended body, matter as pure extension governed by laws of motion, and animals as machines. The problems he left are as famous as the doctrines: the Cartesian circle, the interaction of mind and body, and the gap between ideas and the world they represent.

## Why he rewards weeks of study

First, *the order of reasons*: Descartes builds his system so that each step depends only on what came before it, and the *Meditations* lets you watch that order being made and tested. Few philosophical books are so short and so tightly argued, which is why a week per two Meditations is time well spent. Second, *the objections*: Descartes published his book with its critics. Hobbes, Gassendi, Arnauld, Caterus and Mersenne raised in 1641 most of the objections still taught today, and his replies show which steps he thought carried weight. Elisabeth's letters of 1643 add the interaction problem in its classic form. Third, *the reach*: the way of ideas, the mind–body problem, foundationalism, and the mechanical picture of nature all come from him. Spinoza, Malebranche and Leibniz built on him, Locke, Berkeley and Hume argued with him, and Kant's attacks on the ontological argument and on idealism are aimed at him. Reading him closely is reading the starting point of Stage 3.

## The study plan at a glance

The library's [learning path](../../learning-path.md) meets Descartes first in **Stage 3** (Early Modern to Kant): the *Discourse* I–IV as a warm-up, then *Meditations* 1, 2, 3, 5 and 6 in order, with Elisabeth's objections in selection. This dossier keeps the path's editions (Cottingham or Cress) and its order, adds *Meditation* 4, the Objections and Replies, the *Principles* and the *Passions*, and gives the correspondence with Elisabeth a week of its own. [07-study-plan.md](07-study-plan.md) lays out a **7-week plan** at the path's weekly rhythm:

| Week | Reading | Theme |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | *Discourse* I–IV; *Rules* 1–4 | Method |
| 2 | Synopsis; *Meditations* 1–2 | Doubt, the cogito, the wax |
| 3 | *Meditation* 3; Second and Fourth Objections and Replies on the circle | God and the circle |
| 4 | *Meditations* 4–5; Caterus and Gassendi | Error and the ontological argument |
| 5 | *Meditation* 6; the Elisabeth letters; *Passions* I | Mind, body, and the union |
| 6 | *Principles* I–II selections; *Discourse* V | Matter, motion, and animal machines |
| 7 | Hobbes's Third Objections; the heirs in summary; capstone essay | Critics, heirs, and synthesis |

Weeks 2–5 also use the library's [study guide to the *Meditations*](../../study/descartes-meditations.md), Meditation by Meditation.

The honest total is about **45 hours: 33 at the desk, about 5.7 of assigned media, and about 6.5 of daily card review**, with weekly loads of about 5.4 to 7.8 hours. These hours sit on top of Stage 3's own budget rather than inside it; the plan file states the net addition to the stage and the combined cost if you also run the [Kant dossier](../immanuel-kant/README.md).

## On the quotations

The dossier quotes Descartes and his objectors in the 1911 Haldane & Ross translation, because it is in the public domain and every quotation could be checked word for word. The learning path rejects it as a reading text, and so does this dossier: read Cottingham or Cress, both of which print the Adam–Tannery page numbers the dossier cites. Where Haldane & Ross's English misleads, above all on *cogitatio* ("thought"), the Latin or French is given in brackets. Elisabeth's letters have no public-domain English translation, so the dossier paraphrases them and quotes only a few words of her French, in its own translation.
