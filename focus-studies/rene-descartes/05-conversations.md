# Descartes in Conversation — Backward, Sideways, Forward

*Focus study: René Descartes · [Dossier home](README.md) · [Library entry](../../eras/03-early-modern/rene-descartes.md) · [Study guide](../../study/descartes-meditations.md)*

Descartes presented himself as a man who stopped reading and started thinking. The record shows a philosopher who worked in constant argument. He tested his physics on a Dutch schoolmaster, routed his metaphysics through a Paris friar who sent it to the sharpest critics in Europe, and printed their objections beside his book. A princess made him rethink the union of mind and body, and a former disciple made him say in print what he did not mean. After his death most of the great systems of the next century began as repairs to his. This file traces those exchanges; the scholarly controversies and the reception by era are in [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md).

Descartes is quoted here from the Haldane and Ross translation (1911–12) because it is in the public domain and can be checked; read him in Cottingham or Cress, as the [learning path](../../learning-path.md) advises. Haldane and Ross sometimes narrow *cogitatio* (French *pensée*), which for Descartes covers doubting, willing, imagining and sensing, all of it "thought." Citations give the Adam–Tannery (AT) volume and page printed in the margins of both recommended editions.

## Backward: what he inherited and what he refused

### Augustine: *si fallor, sum*

The cogito has an ancestor, and Descartes' friends kept telling him so. Augustine had answered the skeptic that being deceived requires being: *si fallor, sum*, "if I am mistaken, I am" (*City of God* XI.26, my translation). In November 1640 a correspondent pointed out the passage. Descartes read it in the Leiden town library and replied that Augustine uses the point to prove the certainty of our being and to find an image of the Trinity in us, while he uses it to show that the thinking self is an immaterial substance. The inference is so simple, he added, that anyone might have made it (AT III 247–248; the addressee is unnamed, often taken to be Andreas Colvius). A month later he gave Mersenne the reference and promised to look at Anselm too (AT III 261).

Antoine Arnauld made the parallel public. His Fourth Objections open with Augustine's *On Free Choice of the Will* II.3, where Alypius' question ends: "if you did not exist you could never be in error?" (AT VII 197–198). Descartes' reply was a polite brush-off: "I shall not take up time here by thanking my distinguished critic for bringing to my aid the authority of St Augustine" (Fourth Replies, AT VII 219). The difference he insisted on is real. In [Augustine](../../eras/02-medieval/augustine-of-hippo.md) the certainty turns the mind toward God; in Descartes it becomes the first link in a chain meant to secure physics.

### The schools: La Flèche, Eustachius, Suárez

Descartes learned the Aristotelian textbooks at the Jesuit college of La Flèche, and he kept their vocabulary while changing its use. Substance, "formal" and "objective" reality, causes that contain their effects "eminently": the trademark argument of week 3 cannot be read without them. In 1640 he asked Mersenne which scholastics were current (AT III 185), then bought the *Summa philosophica* of Eustachius a Sancto Paulo, called it the best book ever written on the subject, and planned to reprint it with his own notes and a comparison of the two philosophies (AT III 232–233). Eustachius died that December, and the plan became the *Principles* (week 6), a textbook meant to replace the textbooks.

[Francisco Suárez](../../eras/03-early-modern/suarez-and-grotius.md) appears on both sides of the *Meditations*. Caterus, the Dutch priest who wrote the First Objections, quotes him (AT VII 95) and follows [Aquinas](../../eras/02-medieval/thomas-aquinas.md) against the ontological argument: grant that the concept of a supremely perfect being includes existence, and you have shown only that the concept includes it (AT VII 99). Descartes, defending his term "material falsity" against Arnauld, cited Suárez's *Metaphysical Disputations* IX.2.4, excusing himself for "not having spent much time in reading the writings of philosophers" (AT VII 235). Caterus begins the line of objection that ends in Kant ([04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 9).

### Montaigne and the Pyrrhonian revival

The doubt of week 2 had been in the air for decades. Sextus Empiricus was printed in Latin in 1562, [Montaigne](../../eras/03-early-modern/michel-de-montaigne.md)'s "Apology for Raymond Sebond" (1580) turned his arguments on human reason, Pierre Charron systematized them, and Mersenne wrote a long book against the Pyrrhonists in 1625. Descartes did not claim the doubt as new: "I had long ago seen several books written by the Academics and Sceptics about this subject and felt some disgust in serving up again this stale dish" (Second Replies, AT VII 130). He claimed instead to have answered it, in "the Arguments by which I, first of all men, upset the doubt of the Sceptics" (Seventh Replies, AT VII 549).

Two other debts are plain. The provisional morality of *Discourse* III (AT VI 22ff.) is skeptical practical advice: follow the laws and customs of one's country, act firmly on the best opinion available, master oneself rather than fortune. And *Discourse* V answers Montaigne's suggestion that animals differ from us less than we think. A machine built like us would fail two tests, the first being that "they could never use speech or other signs as we do when placing our thoughts on record for the benefit of others" (AT VI 56). Descartes names Montaigne on this point, refusing the thought that Montaigne and others grant to animals, in a letter to the Marquess of Newcastle of 23 November 1646 (AT IV 573). Richard Popkin made the skeptical crisis the key to the whole project; other historians, among them Daniel Garber, give more weight to the aim of replacing Aristotelian physics.

### Galileo and the suppressed *World*

Descartes never met Galileo. In late November 1633 he wrote to Mersenne from Deventer that the *Dialogue* had been burned in Rome and its author condemned, and that he was almost resolved to burn his own papers or show them to no one (AT I 270–271). *The World*, with its moving earth, stayed in a drawer. *Discourse* VI tells the story without the name: persons he deferred to "had disapproved of a physical theory published a little while before by another" (AT VI 60). His view of Galileo's science was mixed. Reading the *Two New Sciences*, he complained to Mersenne that Galileo explained particular effects without first finding their causes (11 October 1638, AT II 380). The caution this taught him is traced in [01-life-and-times.md](01-life-and-times.md).

## Sideways: the network, the Objections, the letters

### Beeckman

On 10 November 1618, in the garrison town of Breda, a young French volunteer tried to prove to Isaac Beeckman that angles do not exist, and Beeckman noted it in his journal (AT X). The Dutch schoolmaster taught him to treat physical problems mathematically, and they worked together on falling bodies and music. Descartes dedicated his first work, the *Compendium of Music*, to Beeckman, dated at Breda on the last day of 1618 (AT X 141), and wrote in April 1619 that Beeckman alone had roused him from idleness (AT X 162–163). In 1630 Beeckman let it be known that he had taught Descartes much of what he knew, and Descartes broke with him in letters of real harshness (AT I).

### Mersenne and the Objections

Marin Mersenne (1588–1648), a Minim friar in Paris and an older alumnus of La Flèche, ran the correspondence network of French science and was for twenty years Descartes' address, news service and post office. In 1640–41 he circulated the *Meditations*, gathered the Second and Sixth Objections, and sent the text to Hobbes, Gassendi and Arnauld. The book appeared in Paris in 1641 with six sets of Objections and Replies; the Amsterdam edition of 1642 added the Jesuit Pierre Bourdin's Seventh. To Mersenne alone Descartes confided, on 28 January 1641, that the six Meditations contained all the foundations of his physics, and that he hoped readers would accept his principles before noticing that they destroyed Aristotle's (AT III 297–298).

The Second Objections drew a key clarification. Whoever says "I think, hence I am" "does not deduce existence from thought by a syllogism, but, by a simple act of mental vision, recognises it as if it were a thing that is known per se" (Second Replies, AT VII 140). This is the source for reading the cogito as an intuition ([04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 3).

### Hobbes: thinking matter

[Thomas Hobbes](../../eras/03-early-modern/thomas-hobbes.md), then in Paris and already at odds with Descartes over optics, wrote the Third Objections (week 7). He granted the cogito and attacked the next step: from thinking it does not follow that I am a thought, any more than "I am walking; hence I am the walking" (AT VII 172). Every act needs a subject, and we conceive subjects only as bodies, so "the proper inference seems to be that that which thinks is material rather than immaterial" (AT VII 174). Descartes replied that he had assumed nothing about the thinker's nature: "I did not assume the opposite, neither did I use it as a basis for my argument; I left it wholly undetermined until Meditation VI" (AT VII 175).

### Gassendi: "flesh"

Pierre Gassendi, priest, astronomer and reviver of Epicurean atomism, wrote the longest objections, the Fifth, addressing Descartes throughout as "Mind" (*o Mens*, *o Anima*). On the cogito he was brisk: "You might have inferred that from any other activity, since our natural light informs us that whatever acts also exists" (AT VII 259). Descartes replied that only thinking is certain: "you have no right to make the inference: I walk, hence I exist, except in so far as our awareness of walking is a thought" (AT VII 352). And he returned the address: "I ask you therefore, flesh, or whatever the name be by which you prefer to be known" (AT VII 352). Gassendi also argued that existence is not a perfection but the condition of having any, an early form of Kant's objection to the ontological argument (week 4).

### Arnauld: the circle and the triangle

Arnauld, a young theologian who would become the leading mind of Port-Royal, wrote the objections Descartes valued most, and they still organize the literature. Against the real distinction: one can clearly conceive a right triangle without knowing that the square on its hypotenuse equals the squares on the other sides, so conceiving a thing without a feature does not show it lacks the feature (AT VII 201–202). On method: "The only remaining scruple I have is an uncertainty as to how a circular reasoning is to be avoided," if God is proved by clear perception and clear perception is guaranteed by God (AT VII 214). On the Eucharist: without real accidents, how do the appearances of bread remain? Descartes answered the circle by pointing to the Second Replies' line between present perception and remembered conclusions, insisting "I have not argued in a circle" (AT VII 245). These are readings for weeks 3 and 5 ([04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Arguments 7 and 10). Arnauld stayed a Cartesian; the *Port-Royal Logic* (1662), written with Pierre Nicole, carried clear and distinct ideas into the schools.

### Elisabeth: the union

[Elisabeth of Bohemia](../../eras/03-early-modern/early-modern-women-philosophers.md) (1618–1680), the exiled daughter of the Winter King, opened the most important correspondence of Descartes' life on 6/16 May 1643. She asked how the human soul, "n'estant qu'vne substance pensante" ("being only a thinking substance," my translation), can move the bodily spirits to produce voluntary action. Motion is caused by impulsion, which needs contact, or by the mover's surface and shape, which need extension; Descartes denies the soul extension, and contact seems impossible for an immaterial thing (AT III 661). She added a second question: how can thought depend on the body, as it seems to in a deep faint?

Descartes replied on 21 May that we have three primitive notions, of extension for body, of thought for the soul, and of their union, and that confusion comes from explaining one by another. He compared the soul's action on the body to the way heaviness was once conceived as a quality moving bodies (AT III 665–667). Elisabeth found the comparison worse than the problem, since on his own physics that idea of heaviness is a fiction. She would find it easier, she wrote, to concede matter and extension to the soul than to grant an immaterial being the power to move a body and be moved by it (10/20 June, AT III 684–685). His answer of 28 June is the famous one. The union is known through the senses, in ordinary life and conversation, not by metaphysics, which one should work through once in a lifetime and then leave alone (AT III 692, 695). She may attribute matter and extension to the soul if she likes, he added, since that is only to conceive it as united to the body (AT III 694).

This is the core of week 5 ([04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 13). The letters continued until his death. In 1645 they read Seneca's *On the Happy Life* at his suggestion (AT IV 253), and her questions about the passions led to *The Passions of the Soul* (1649), which places the union's work in "a certain very small gland which is situated in the middle of its substance" (art. 31, AT XI 352). He had dedicated the *Principles* to her in 1644. She would not allow her letters to be printed, so for two centuries readers knew only his side.

### Regius and Utrecht

Henricus Regius, professor of medicine at Utrecht, was Descartes' first academic disciple. In 1641 he called a human being an *ens per accidens*, an accidental unity of two substances. The rector, the theologian Gisbertus Voetius, treated this as heresy, and Descartes advised Regius to say instead that a human is an *ens per se*, a true unity (AT III 460, 491–492). The Utrecht senate condemned the new philosophy in 1642. Later Regius went his own way, and a broadsheet of 1647 declared that the mind "may be either a substance, or a mode of a corporeal substance." Descartes answered with *Notes against a Programme* (1648, AT VIIIB), complaining that "our author seems to assume that man is body alone." Regius had reached Hobbes's conclusion through Descartes' own physiology.

### The Huygens and Christina

Constantijn Huygens (1596–1687), poet and secretary to the Prince of Orange, was a friend and protector from the early 1630s and read the *Dioptrics* in draft. His son Christiaan (1629–1695) grew up on Cartesian physics and became its ablest critic. He showed that most of the collision rules of *Principles* II.46–52 are false, and in notes on Baillet's 1691 biography called the physics a fine romance, plausible stories offered as truths ([06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md)).

Queen Christina of Sweden reached Descartes through the French resident Pierre Chanut. Asked for his view of the supreme good, he answered on 20 November 1647 that it lies in a firm will to do well and the contentment that follows (AT V 81–86). Her invitation followed. He reached Stockholm early in October 1649 (AT V 429–430), wrote in January that men's thoughts seemed to freeze there in winter like the water (AT V 467), and died on 11 February 1650.

## Forward: the heirs and the critics

### Malebranche: one true cause

[Nicolas Malebranche](../../eras/03-early-modern/nicolas-malebranche.md) became a Cartesian in 1664, when, as his biographer Yves André tells it, Descartes' *Treatise on Man* made his heart pound at a Paris bookstall. His *Search after Truth* (1674–75) generalized Elisabeth's problem. No creature has a necessary connection with its effects, so God is the only true cause, and minds and bodies are occasions for his action. The seed is in Descartes, for whom "the distinction between creation and conservation is solely a distinction of the reason" (*Med.* 3, AT VII 49): if God recreates the world at each moment, little work is left for bodies. Hume's verdict was that Descartes "insinuated that doctrine of the universal and sole efficacy of the Deity, without insisting on it," and Malebranche built a philosophy on it (*Enquiry* VII, note).

### Spinoza: one substance

[Baruch Spinoza](../../eras/03-early-modern/baruch-spinoza.md)'s only book under his own name expounded Descartes' *Principles* in geometrical order (1663). The *Ethics* (1677) keeps Descartes' definition of substance and draws the consequence he avoided: only God meets it, so there is one substance, with thought and extension as two of its attributes. Mind and body are one thing under two descriptions, and the interaction problem disappears. The preface to *Ethics* V attacks the pineal gland: a philosopher who blamed the scholastics for occult qualities "could maintain a hypothesis, beside which occult qualities are commonplace." Spinoza asks, "What does he understand, I ask, by the union of the mind and the body?" (Elwes translation). He also denied that will and intellect differ (*Ethics* II, prop. 49), which removes Descartes' theory of error. The [Spinoza dossier](../baruch-spinoza/05-conversations.md) tells this from the other side.

### Leibniz: force and harmony

[Leibniz](../../eras/03-early-modern/gottfried-wilhelm-leibniz.md) studied Descartes' papers in Paris in 1675–76; the early notes he copied survive only through him (AT X). In "Meditations on Knowledge, Truth and Ideas" (1684) he argued that clear and distinct perception is no criterion until we say what makes an idea clear and distinct, and that the ontological argument needs a proof that a perfect being is possible. His "Brief Demonstration of a Notable Error of Descartes" (1686) showed that collisions conserve not quantity of motion, size times speed (*Principles* II.36), but mass times the square of speed. His "New System" (1695) replaced interaction with a pre-established harmony.

### Locke and Berkeley: the way of ideas

[John Locke](../../eras/03-early-modern/john-locke.md) took from Descartes the view that the mind's immediate objects are its own ideas; Damaris Masham reported that Descartes first gave him a taste for philosophy. The *Essay* (1689) then attacks innate ideas, denies that the soul always thinks (II.i.10–19), and allows that God could give matter the power to think (IV.iii.6), Hobbes's possibility made respectable. [George Berkeley](../../eras/03-early-modern/george-berkeley.md) drew the conclusion Descartes feared. If we perceive only ideas, and the proof of a material world rests on a divine guarantee few accepted, matter is an idle hypothesis; the *Principles of Human Knowledge* (1710) keeps minds and God and drops extended substance.

### Hume: incurable doubt

[David Hume](../../eras/03-early-modern/david-hume.md) made Descartes his example of "antecedent" skepticism, a doubt of all our faculties before inquiry. No principle could survive it to begin the rebuilding: "The Cartesian doubt, therefore, were it ever possible to be attained by any human creature (as it plainly is not) would be entirely incurable" (*Enquiry* XII, 1748). See the [Hume *Enquiry* study guide](../../study/hume-enquiry.md).

### Kant: the refutation of idealism

[Kant](../../eras/03-early-modern/immanuel-kant.md) called Descartes' position "problematic idealism": my own existence is certain, the existence of things in space doubtful. The Refutation of Idealism, added in 1787 (B274–279), reverses the order. I am conscious of my existence as determined in time, which requires something permanent in perception distinct from my representations, so inner experience presupposes outer. The Paralogisms argue that the "I think" accompanying my representations gives no knowledge of a simple, enduring thinking substance, and the Ideal denies that existence is a real predicate ([04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 9; [Kant dossier](../immanuel-kant/04-arguments.md)).

### Husserl and Ryle

[Edmund Husserl](../../eras/05-20th-century/edmund-husserl.md) lectured at the Sorbonne in February 1929 and published the lectures, expanded, as *Cartesian Meditations* (French, 1931). He praised Descartes for starting from the knowing subject and faulted him for making the ego a thinking substance, one more piece of the world and the first premise of a chain of proofs. He closed with Augustine's advice to turn inward, where truth dwells (*On True Religion* 39.72), bringing the conversation back to *si fallor, sum*.

[Gilbert Ryle](../../eras/05-20th-century/gilbert-ryle.md)'s *The Concept of Mind* (1949) called the picture of mind as a private inner theater steering a public body "Descartes' Myth," the dogma of the ghost in the machine. Its error is a category mistake: mental words describe abilities and ways of acting, not hidden episodes. How fairly this fits the historical Descartes, who insisted on the union and wrote a book on the embodied passions, is debated ([06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md); [Philosophy of Mind after Ryle](../../eras/05-20th-century/philosophy-of-mind-after-ryle.md)).

## The themes trace

The library's [problem index](../../themes.md) places Descartes in six of its nine threads.

- [§1 Knowledge & Skepticism](../../themes.md#1-knowledge--skepticism--what-can-we-know-and-how-do-we-know-it) — Methodic doubt answered by the cogito and God's guarantee of clear and distinct perception. Weeks 2–3; Montaigne behind, Hume and Kant ahead.
- [§2 Mind & Body](../../themes.md#2-mind--body--what-is-mind-and-how-does-it-relate-to-the-body) — Mind and body as really distinct substances, united in a human being. Week 5; Hobbes, Elisabeth, Regius, Spinoza, Malebranche, Leibniz, Ryle.
- [§3 God & Religion](../../themes.md#3-god--religion--does-god-exist-and-what-follows-for-faith-and-reason) — The trademark and ontological arguments. Weeks 3–4; Caterus, Gassendi, Arnauld, Leibniz, Kant.
- [§4 Ethics & the Good Life](../../themes.md#4-ethics--the-good-life--how-should-i-live-and-what-is-right) — The provisional morality and the late ethics of the passions. Weeks 1 and 5; Elisabeth and Christina.
- [§6 Self & Identity](../../themes.md#6-self--identity--what-am-i-and-what-makes-me-the-same-person-over-time) — The self as a thinking thing, known before any body. Week 2; Augustine, Hume, Kant, Husserl.
- [§8 Science & Method](../../themes.md#8-science--method--how-should-inquiry-proceed-and-what-does-it-prove) — One method for all the sciences, and a physics of extension and motion. Weeks 1 and 6; Beeckman, Galileo, Leibniz, Christiaan Huygens.

He is absent from §5 (Politics & Justice), §7 (Language & Meaning) and §9 (History & Progress). The first absence is real: *Discourse* VI leaves the reform of morals to those God has set over peoples (AT VI 60–61). The second is partly a filing decision, since the language test of *Discourse* V is a claim about what speech shows about minds. The third fits a philosophy that begins by setting aside everything inherited.
