# Descartes: The Arguments
**English** | [简体中文](../../zh/focus-studies/rene-descartes/04-arguments.md)

*Focus study · [Dossier home](README.md) · Library entry: [René Descartes](../../eras/03-early-modern/rene-descartes.md) · The system these arguments build: [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md) · Study guide: [*Meditations on First Philosophy*](../../study/descartes-meditations.md)*

Each argument below is reconstructed in numbered premises in the house style: what the argument proves, the reconstruction, its textual basis, the strongest standard objection, and the best reply, Descartes' own where he gives one and the literature's where he does not, labeled as such. Citations are by **Adam–Tannery (AT)** volume and page. Charles Adam and Paul Tannery edited the complete works in Paris between 1897 and 1913, and their page numbers are printed in the margins of the Cottingham and Cress translations. So (*Med.* 2, AT VII 25) means volume VII, the Latin *Meditations* with the Objections and Replies, page 25. The *Discourse* is in AT VI. The Latin *Principles* are in AT VIIIA and are also cited by part and article, which every edition prints. Quotations come from the Haldane and Ross translation of 1911–12, because it is in the public domain and each quotation can be checked word for word; it is for checking, and the reading editions remain Cottingham and Cress. Where its English misleads, the Latin or French term follows in brackets. The most important case is *cogitatio* (French *pensée*), "thought." Descartes uses it for every conscious state, doubting, willing, imagining and seeming to see as much as reasoning. A reader who hears "thought" as "reasoning" will misread the cogito.

The *Meditations* is written as a narrator's private reflection, starting from no settled beliefs, so a step that looks question-begging may be one the narrator has not yet earned. The Objections and Replies, published with the book in 1641, show which steps carry weight, and each argument here is read with them beside it.

**Argument 1. The dreaming argument (*Meditations* 1)**

*What it proves:* that the senses cannot certify any particular judgment about the bodies around me, not even that I am sitting by the fire, because nothing in present experience marks it as waking rather than dreaming. It does not yet touch arithmetic and geometry.

The First Meditation has already set aside judgments about things small or far away. The narrator then takes the hardest case for doubt: his own hands and the paper in front of him. Madmen doubt such things, and he declines to copy them. Dreams are another matter. He has often dreamt he was dressed and seated by the fire while lying undressed in bed.

1. I have often had experiences in sleep that seemed exactly like the experience I am having now.
2. If no mark tells me, from within an experience, that it is waking rather than dreaming, then for any present experience it is possible that I am dreaming.
3. There is no such mark: "there are no certain indications by which we may clearly distinguish wakefulness from sleep" (*Med.* 1, AT VII 19).
4. If I may be dreaming, my present sensory judgment (that I am seated here, that these hands exist) may be false.
Therefore no sensory judgment about particular bodies is certain.

The narrator then limits the result. Dream images are "like painted representations which can only have been formed as the counterparts of something real and true" (AT VII 19). A painter can assemble a siren from parts of animals, but the colours must be real. In the same way the simplest elements, extension, shape, quantity, place and time, survive. Physics, astronomy and medicine are doubtful; arithmetic and geometry are not, "for whether I am awake or asleep, two and three together always form five, and the square can never have more than four sides" (AT VII 20). That limit is why a second argument is needed.

**Textual basis.** *Med.* 1, AT VII 18–20; *Discourse* IV, AT VI 32; the Synopsis, AT VII 12, which says the doubt frees the mind from prejudice and leads it away from the senses. The answer comes in *Med.* 6, AT VII 89–90.

**Objection.** (i) Thomas Hobbes, in the first of the Third Objections (AT VII 171), granted the argument and called it stale: Plato and other ancients had discussed the uncertainty of the senses, and the difficulty of telling dreams from waking is common knowledge. (ii) The stronger modern objection is that the argument undermines itself. To know that dreams have deceived me before, I must have told dreams from waking on those occasions, which premise 3 says I cannot do. J. L. Austin pressed a version in *Sense and Sensibilia* (1962). Norman Malcolm, in *Dreaming* (1959), argued that dreams are not experiences had during sleep at all, so premise 1 compares unlike things. See [Thomas Hobbes](../../eras/03-early-modern/thomas-hobbes.md).

**Reply.** To Hobbes, Descartes' own (Third Replies, AT VII 171–172): he used the old arguments not as news but to prepare readers to separate intellectual matters from corporeal ones, and in order to answer them later. To the self-undermining objection, the literature's: the argument needs only the possibility that this experience is a dream, which can be granted without claiming to know of any particular past dream. Bernard Williams (*Descartes: The Project of Pure Enquiry*, 1978) and Barry Stroud (*The Significance of Philosophical Scepticism*, 1984) read it this way, and Stroud argues that the challenge stands. Descartes' own answer to the doubt comes in the Sixth Meditation, after God has been shown to be no deceiver. He now finds "a very notable difference" between the states, since "our memory can never connect our dreams one with the other, or with the whole course of our lives" as it connects waking events (*Med.* 6, AT VII 89). Coherence is the test, and God's veracity makes it reliable, so he can "set aside all the doubts of these past days as hyperbolical and ridiculous" (AT VII 89). That answer depends on the proof of God, so it inherits whatever trouble Argument 7 finds there.

**Argument 2. The deceiving God and the evil demon (*Meditations* 1)**

*What it proves:* that even the simplest truths of arithmetic and geometry can be doubted, because nothing yet rules out that my mind was made to err even in what seems most evident. The demon is then a device for keeping that doubt in force.

1. I believe I was made by an all-powerful God (AT VII 21).
2. An all-powerful being could have made me so that I go wrong even in what seems most evident: "how do I know that I am not deceived every time that I add two and three, or count the sides of a square" (*Med.* 1, AT VII 21)?
3. God's goodness is no answer, since it already permits me to be deceived sometimes, and I cannot rule out that it permits more.
4. If instead I owe my existence to fate, chance or a chain of natural causes, my origin is less perfect, and "since to err and deceive oneself is a defect" (AT VII 21), the less powerful my author, the likelier it is that I always err.
5. So on every hypothesis about my origin I have a reason to doubt even the simplest judgments.
Therefore nothing I formerly believed is beyond doubt, and I must withhold assent from it as carefully as from what is plainly false.

Habit keeps pulling the old opinions back, so the narrator adopts a working fiction. He will suppose "some evil genius not less powerful than deceitful, has employed his whole energies in deceiving me" (AT VII 22). The Latin is *genius malignus*, a malicious spirit; "genius" here means spirit, not talent. The demon adds no new reason for doubt. The reason is premise 5, and the demon keeps it in view without the narrator having to suppose that God deceives.

**Textual basis.** *Med.* 1, AT VII 21–23; *Med.* 2, AT VII 24–25; *Med.* 3, AT VII 35–36, where the doubt is restated and graded.

**Objection.** David Hume put the standard objection in the *Enquiry concerning Human Understanding* (1748), section XII. A doubt that reaches our very faculties cannot be answered, since any answer must use those faculties: "The Cartesian doubt, therefore, were it ever possible to be attained by any human creature (as it plainly is not) would be entirely incurable." That is two charges. The doubt cannot really be held, and if it were held it would close every exit, including the proofs of God that Descartes intends as the way out. See [David Hume](../../eras/03-early-modern/david-hume.md).

**Reply.** Descartes' own, in part. He grades the doubt. Since he has no reason yet to think God a deceiver, "the reason for doubt which depends on this opinion alone is very slight, and so to speak metaphysical" (*Med.* 3, AT VII 36). It is not a belief that I am deceived, only the lack of a reason to exclude it. And it does not hold while I attend to something I perceive clearly: then, he says, "Let who will deceive me," no one can make it false that I exist while I think, or that two and three make five (AT VII 36). This answers Hume's first charge. The doubt is not a state anyone lives in, and Descartes says so: "I am not considering the question of action, but only of knowledge" (AT VII 22). Whether it answers the second charge is the question of Argument 7.

**Argument 3. The cogito (*Meditations* 2; *Discourse* IV; *Principles* I.7; Second Replies)**

*What it proves:* that I exist, whenever I think, with a certainty no deceiver can touch; and, in a second step, that what I am known to be, so far, is a thinking thing.

The narrator wants one fixed point, as Archimedes wanted one point to move the earth (AT VII 24). He has persuaded himself that there is no sky and no bodies. Was he then persuaded that he did not exist? No: "of a surety I myself did exist since I persuaded myself of something" (AT VII 25). The deceiver gives the same result: "he can never cause me to be nothing so long as I think that I am something" (AT VII 25). So the proposition "I am, I exist, is necessarily true each time that I pronounce it, or that I mentally conceive it" (*Med.* 2, AT VII 25).

1. I am now thinking: doubting, being persuaded, or being deceived. Each of these is *cogitatio*.
2. That I am thinking cannot be doubted, because doubting it is itself thinking.
3. Thinking cannot occur unless something exists that thinks.
Therefore I exist, as long as I think.

The second step asks what this "I" is. Body, nutrition, walking and sensing, taken as bodily acts, may all be illusions. Thinking alone cannot be removed: "I am, I exist, that is certain. But how often? Just when I think" (AT VII 27). So I am, strictly, "a thing which thinks" (AT VII 27). Sensing survives in a reduced form. I may be dreaming, but "it is at least quite certain that it seems to me that I see light," and this seeming [*sentire*; Haldane and Ross write "feeling"], "used in this precise sense," is "no other thing than thinking" (AT VII 29).

The famous formula is not in the *Meditations*. It is in the *Discourse* (1637), in French, *je pense, donc je suis*, a truth "so certain and so assured that all the most extravagant suppositions brought forward by the sceptics were incapable of shaking it" (*Discourse* IV, AT VI 32). The Latin *ego cogito, ergo sum* appears in the *Principles* (1644), with a reason: "there is a contradiction in conceiving that what thinks does not at the same time as it thinks, exist" (*Principles* I.7, AT VIIIA 7).

**Textual basis.** *Med.* 2, AT VII 24–29; *Discourse* IV, AT VI 32–33; *Principles* I.7–10; Second Replies, AT VII 140; Third Objections and Replies II, AT VII 172–174; Fifth Objections, AT VII 258–259, and Fifth Replies, AT VII 352. Antoine Arnauld noted in the Fourth Objections that [Augustine](../../eras/02-medieval/augustine-of-hippo.md) had made a similar move in *On Free Choice of the Will* II.3 (AT VII 197).

**Is it an inference?** The "therefore" suggests that premise 3 is a general major premise from which the conclusion follows by syllogism. Descartes denied this in the Second Replies. One who says "I think, hence I am, or exist" "does not deduce existence from thought by a syllogism, but, by a simple act of mental vision, recognises it as if it were a thing that is known per se" (AT VII 140; Latin *simplici mentis intuitu*). Were it a syllogism, "the major premise, that everything that thinks is, or exists, would have to be known previously" (AT VII 140), whereas we learn the general truth from the particular case. Yet the *Principles* say the cogito presupposes knowing "what is knowledge, what is existence, and what is certainty, and that in order to think we must be" (*Principles* I.10, AT VIIIA 8). How the passages fit is disputed. One reading takes I.10 to list background notions that are understood, not premises inferred from. Jaakko Hintikka ("Cogito, ergo sum: Inference or Performance?", *Philosophical Review*, 1962) held that the certainty comes from the act: "I do not exist" defeats itself whenever I think it.

**Objection.** The conclusion claims more than the evidence gives. Hobbes, in the second of the Third Objections, argued that "I am exercising thought" does not make me a mind, any more than one may say "I am walking; hence I am the walking" (AT VII 172). "Hence it is possible for a thing that thinks to be the subject of the mind, reason, or understanding, and hence to be something corporeal" (AT VII 173). Pierre Gassendi, in the Fifth Objections, asked why so much machinery was needed: "You might have inferred that from any other activity, since our natural light informs us that whatever acts also exists" (AT VII 259). Later, Georg Christoph Lichtenberg wrote in his notebooks that we should say "it thinks," as we say "it thunders": all that is given is that thinking goes on.

**Reply.** To Gassendi, Descartes' own (Fifth Replies, AT VII 352): no other activity is certain in the same way. "I walk, hence I exist" holds only "in so far as our awareness of walking is a thought"; the body's motion may not exist, as in dreams. To Hobbes, also his own (Third Replies, AT VII 174): "thinking" can name the thing that thinks as well as the act, and whether the thinker is a body is left open here and settled only in the Sixth Meditation (Argument 10). To Lichtenberg, the literature's: Williams (1978) locates the "I" in Descartes' principle that every thought is a property of some substance, and says that principle, not the cogito, is where to press. Others reply that a thought belonging to no one is not something we can conceive. The dispute is open. The cogito secures at least that thinking is occurring and cannot be doubted while it occurs; what more it secures is contested.

**Argument 4. The wax (*Meditations* 2)**

*What it proves:* that bodies are known by the intellect, not by the senses or the imagination; and, as a consequence, that the mind is known more clearly than any body.

The narrator cannot shake the feeling that bodies, which he sees and touches, are known better than the "I" he cannot picture. So he takes a piece of wax fresh from the hive, with the taste of honey and the scent of flowers, a definite colour, shape and size, hard, cold, and giving a sound when struck. He puts it by the fire. The taste and smell go, the colour changes, the shape is lost, it melts and grows hot, and it gives no sound. "Does the same wax remain after this change? We must confess that it remains" (*Med.* 2, AT VII 30).

1. The wax before and after heating is the same wax.
2. Every quality given by the senses has changed.
3. So what remains the same, the wax itself, is not known by any of the senses.
4. What remains is "a certain extended thing which is flexible and movable" (AT VII 31).
5. Flexible and movable means capable of more changes of shape and size than I can ever picture; so the imagination cannot grasp it either.
6. The only faculty left is the intellect. The perception of the wax "is neither an act of vision, nor of touch, nor of imagination," but "an intuition" [*inspectio*, an inspection] "of the mind," confused at first and clear and distinct when attended to (AT VII 31).
Therefore bodies are perceived by the intellect alone.

Ordinary speech hides this. From a window I say I see men in the street, but "what do I see from the window but hats and coats which may cover automatic machines? Yet I judge these to be men" (AT VII 32). Then comes the consequence for the mind. If I judge that the wax exists because I see it, "it certainly follows much more clearly that I am or that I exist myself from the fact that I see it" (AT VII 33), since the seeing may be illusory but the thinking is not.

**Textual basis.** *Med.* 2, AT VII 29–34; Third Replies, AT VII 175; Fifth Objections, AT VII 271–273, and Fifth Replies, AT VII 359. The Fifth Meditation develops the result: the essence of body is extension, known through the clear ideas of geometry (AT VII 63); see Argument 15.

**Objection.** Gassendi made the standard objection (Fifth Objections, AT VII 271–273). Everyone grants that the concept of wax can be abstracted from its qualities, but that gives no distinct perception of what the wax is. We conceive some subject of the changing qualities, but its nature eludes us, and "it is only by conjecture that we think that there must be some substratum." When Descartes does seem to conceive the wax, he conceives it as extended, shaped and even coloured, so his intellect is "imagination of a kind." A later objection is that the argument shows at most that the wax is known by judgment on sensory evidence, which an empiricist can accept. [John Locke](../../eras/03-early-modern/john-locke.md)'s "substance in general" is a version of Gassendi's unknown substratum.

**Reply.** Descartes' own (Fifth Replies, AT VII 359): "neither have I abstracted the concept of wax from that of its accidents; rather have I tried to show how its substance was manifested by means of accidents." The sensory qualities could change completely while the wax remained. What stays is extension, and extension is not an unknown substratum but the clearly understood nature of body. The Third Replies (AT VII 175) add that the example was meant only to show that colour, hardness and shape do not belong to the wax's formal nature. On the empiricist objection, the literature's reply is that the weight falls on premise 5. The wax can take more shapes than any finite series of images, and knowing that is not itself an image. Whether such knowledge needs a faculty beyond judgment on sense remains debated, from Locke through [Berkeley](../../eras/03-early-modern/george-berkeley.md) to Kant.

**Argument 5. The trademark argument: objective reality and the causal adequacy principle (*Meditations* 3)**

*What it proves:* that God exists, from the fact that I have an idea of God and from a principle about what can cause an idea. It is the first proof of God in the *Meditations*, and the rest of the book leans on it.

Three pieces of scholastic vocabulary are needed. An idea's **formal reality** is the reality it has as a mode of my mind; on that score all my ideas are equal. Its **objective reality** is the reality of what it represents. The idea of a substance has more objective reality than the idea of a mode, and the idea of an infinite substance more than that of a finite one. A cause contains something **formally** if it has it as the effect has it, and **eminently** if it has it in a higher form.

1. I have an idea of God, a substance "infinite [eternal, immutable], independent, allknowing, all-powerful," by whom I and everything else were created (*Med.* 3, AT VII 45).
2. "It is manifest by the natural light that there must at least be as much reality in the efficient and total cause as in its effect" (AT VII 40).
3. This holds for the objective reality of ideas as well as for things. One idea may cause another, but not without end: "in the end we must reach an idea whose cause shall be so to speak an archetype" (AT VII 42), containing formally what the idea contains objectively.
4. The objective reality of my idea of God is infinite.
5. I am finite, and so is everything I know besides God; nothing finite contains infinite reality, formally or eminently.
Therefore an infinite being, God, exists as the cause of my idea of God.

Premise 4 is defended against an obvious reply, that I form the idea of the infinite by negating the finite, as I form the idea of rest by negating motion. Descartes says the order runs the other way: "I have in me the notion of the infinite earlier than the finite—to wit, the notion of God before that of myself" (AT VII 45). I could not know that I doubt and lack things unless I had the idea of a more perfect being to compare myself with. The idea is innate, and God placed it in me "to be like the mark of the workman imprinted on his work" (AT VII 51), which gives the argument its name. The Synopsis (AT VII 14) and the First Replies (AT VII 103–104) offer an analogy: the idea of a very skilful machine needs a cause, a real machine seen before or great mechanical knowledge.

**Textual basis.** *Med.* 3, AT VII 40–47 and 51; Synopsis, AT VII 14; First Objections, AT VII 92, and First Replies, AT VII 102–104; Second Replies, AT VII 139; Third Objections and Replies V, AT VII 179–181; Fifth Objections, AT VII 295, and Fifth Replies, AT VII 365; *Discourse* IV, AT VI 33–34.

**Objection.** Three are standard, and the third is the strongest. (i) Johannes Caterus, a Dutch priest, in the First Objections, denied that objective reality needs a cause. Being "objectively in the understanding" is "a merely external attribute of the thing and nothing belonging to its reality," so "why should I ask for a cause of that which is nothing actual" (AT VII 92)? (ii) Hobbes, in the Third Objections, denied that we have an idea of God: "we have no image, no idea corresponding to it" (AT VII 180). We infer an eternal cause, as a man born blind infers a fire from its warmth, and give it a name. (iii) Gassendi (AT VII 295) and later Hume argued that the idea can be built from finite materials. In Hume's words, the idea of God "arises from reflecting on the operations of our own mind, and augmenting, without limit, those qualities of goodness and wisdom" (*Enquiry* II, §14). If so, a finite mind with a power of amplifying is an adequate cause, and premise 5 fails.

**Reply.** Descartes' own to each. (i) Caterus is talking about the thing, for which being thought of is indeed an extrinsic label; Descartes means the idea. The idea of the sun is the sun existing in the mind objectively, and "this mode of being is truly much less perfect than that in which things exist outside the mind, but it is not on that account mere nothing" (First Replies, AT VII 102–103). (ii) Hobbes takes "idea" to mean an image in the corporeal imagination; Descartes uses it for "whatever the mind directly perceives" (Third Replies, AT VII 181). (iii) To amplification he answers with number. In counting I can always go higher, which shows "I have received the power of conceiving that a number is thinkable, that is higher than any that can ever be thought by me" (Second Replies, AT VII 139), and received it from something more perfect than myself. The Fifth Replies generalize (AT VII 365): the power of amplifying created perfections must itself have a source, and it is God. Critics answer that this restates premise 2 rather than defending it, and most modern readers reject the causal adequacy principle as applied to the content of ideas. The literature's defence, for example in Williams (1978), is historical: the principle was common ground for the scholastic audience, as the degrees-of-being framework of [Thomas Aquinas](../../eras/02-medieval/thomas-aquinas.md) shows.

**Argument 6. The argument from my continued existence (*Meditations* 3)**

*What it proves:* that God exists, as the cause who keeps me in existence from moment to moment. Descartes presents it not as a new proof but "a more thorough-going explanation" of Argument 5 (First Replies, AT VII 106), because the first proof is hard to keep in view when attention slackens (AT VII 47). It asks about the cause of me, the thing that has the idea of God.

1. I exist, as a thinking thing that has the idea of God.
2. If I were the author of my own being, I would have given myself every perfection I have an idea of: "were I myself the author of my being, I should doubt nothing and I should desire nothing" (AT VII 48). I do doubt and desire, so I am not.
3. Supposing that I have always existed does not help: "all the course of my life may be divided into an infinite number of parts, none of which is in any way dependent on the other" (AT VII 48–49). That I existed a moment ago does not entail that I exist now.
4. So at each moment some cause must, so to speak, create me again, "that is to say, conserves me," and "the distinction between creation and conservation is solely a distinction of the reason" (AT VII 49).
5. I have no power to keep myself in existence, for as a thinking thing, "if such a power did reside in me, I should certainly be conscious of it" (AT VII 49), and I am conscious of none.
6. By the causal principle of Argument 5, my conserver must be a thinking thing with at least the perfections I have ideas of.
7. If it exists through itself, it is God. If through another, the question repeats, and there can be no infinite regress, since the question is what conserves me now. My parents do not conserve me (AT VII 50).
Therefore God exists and conserves me.

The regress step differs from the medieval proofs Caterus compared it with. Descartes did not argue from a series of past causes, which he could not show must have a first member. "I prefer to use as the foundation of my proof my own existence, which is not dependent on any series of causes" (First Replies, AT VII 107). A series of causes sustaining me at this moment must end.

**Textual basis.** *Med.* 3, AT VII 47–51; First Objections, AT VII 93–95, and First Replies, AT VII 106–111; Fifth Objections, AT VII 300–301, and Fifth Replies, AT VII 369–370; the geometrical exposition appended to the Second Replies, Proposition III (AT VII 168); *Principles* I.21.

**Objection.** Gassendi attacked premises 3 and 4 (Fifth Objections, AT VII 300–301). Some effects need their cause present to go on existing, as sunlight needs the sun, but many, everything procreated or manufactured, outlast their cause, and you are one of these. He asked "what difference this dependence or independence of the parts of time, which are external, successive and non-active, makes to your production or reproduction." A second objection: premise 5 assumes that any power of self-conservation would be conscious, which needs an argument; Anthony Kenny (*Descartes*, 1968) presses this. A third concerns cost. If creatures are recreated at every instant, it is hard to see what causal work they do. Hume remarked that Descartes "insinuated that doctrine of the universal and sole efficacy of the Deity, without insisting on it," and that [Malebranche](../../eras/03-early-modern/nicolas-malebranche.md) made it a foundation (*Enquiry* VII, note).

**Reply.** Descartes' own to Gassendi (Fifth Replies, AT VII 369–370). Gassendi confuses causes of becoming with causes of being: "an architect is the cause of a house and a father of his son in respect of coming into being merely," so their effects outlast them, but God, like the sun with its light, causes things to go on existing. The point concerns not abstract time but the duration of an enduring thing, which may cease at any moment. A creature able to persist unaided would have a perfection of the Creator. On the cost, the literature has no single reply. Daniel Garber (*Descartes' Metaphysical Physics*, 1992) argues that Descartes drew occasionalist consequences for bodies but not for minds; others resist that reading. The argument's own weakness is that premise 6 uses the causal adequacy principle, so it stands or falls with Argument 5.

**Argument 7. The Cartesian circle and Descartes' reply (Fourth Objections and Replies; Second Replies)**

*What it proves:* the objection claims that Descartes' route from the cogito to God and back to knowledge is circular. The reply claims that it is not, because clear and distinct perception needs no guarantee while it is present, and God's veracity is needed only for remembered conclusions.

The structure under attack runs as follows. From the cogito the narrator draws a rule: "all things which I perceive very clearly and very distinctly are true" (*Med.* 3, AT VII 35). At once he doubts it, since a God could make him err "even in matters in which I believe myself to have the best evidence," and "without a knowledge of these two truths," that God exists and is no deceiver, "I do not see that I can ever be certain of anything" (AT VII 36). He proves both (Arguments 5 and 6; AT VII 52) and concludes that "the certainty and truth of all knowledge depends alone on the knowledge of the true God" (*Med.* 5, AT VII 71).

Marin Mersenne's Second Objections raised the problem first, about the cogito itself: "since you are not yet certain of the aforesaid existence of God," and claim to be certain of nothing until you are, you cannot know that you are a thinking thing (AT VII 124–125). They added the atheist who knows clearly that a triangle's angles equal two right angles (AT VII 125). Arnauld's Fourth Objections gave the classic form (AT VII 214):

1. Descartes holds that "the only secure reason we have for believing that what we clearly and distinctly perceive is true, is the fact that God exists."
2. But we can be sure that God exists only because we clearly and evidently perceive it.
3. So before being certain that God exists, we must already be certain that whatever we clearly perceive is true.
Therefore the proof of God presupposes what it was meant to establish.

**Textual basis.** *Med.* 3, AT VII 35–36; *Med.* 5, AT VII 69–71; Second Objections, AT VII 124–125, and Second Replies, AT VII 140–141 and 144–146; Fourth Objections, AT VII 214, and Fourth Replies, AT VII 245–246. Gassendi pressed a version against the Fifth Meditation (Fifth Objections, AT VII 327–328).

**Descartes' reply.** The memory reply. In the Fourth Replies he points Arnauld back to the Second Replies: "There I distinguished those matters that in actual truth we clearly perceive from those we remember to have formerly perceived" (AT VII 246). We are sure God exists while we attend to the proofs; afterwards it is enough to remember having perceived something clearly, but only if we know that God exists and does not deceive. The Second Replies had told Mersenne that the claim concerns only "such conclusions as can recur in memory without attending further to the proofs" (AT VII 140); the cogito is a primitive act of knowledge, not such a conclusion. The Fifth Meditation states the problem the guarantee solves. While I attend to a geometrical proof I cannot help believing it, but when I turn away, "although I still recollect having clearly comprehended it, it may easily occur that I come to doubt its truth, if I am ignorant of there being a God" (*Med.* 5, AT VII 70). So the atheist's knowledge "cannot constitute true science, because no knowledge that can be rendered doubtful should be called science" (Second Replies, AT VII 141). "Science" translates *scientia*, stable knowledge. The Second Replies also set the standard. A conviction so firm that no reason for doubt remains is "all the certainty that can reasonably be desired," and if someone supposes that it looks false to God or an angel: "What heed do we pay to that absolute falsity, when we by no means believe that it exists or even suspect its existence?" (AT VII 145).

**Objection to the reply.** The memory reply leaves present perception unguarded, yet the Third Meditation doubts present perception. The doubt about adding two and three (AT VII 21, 36) concerns something grasped in one glance. If present clear perception can be doubted, so can the proofs of God, which are made of it, and the circle returns. A further problem: the proofs themselves take time, so they too rely on memory, and God guarantees clear perception, not memory. Willis Doney ("The Cartesian Circle," *Journal of the History of Ideas*, 1955) defended the memory reading; Harry Frankfurt (*Demons, Dreamers, and Madmen*, 1970) argued against it on these grounds.

**Reply.** The literature's, and there is no consensus. (i) The memory reading, refined: while I attend, I cannot doubt ("Let who will deceive me," AT VII 36). The doubt arises only when I think of God's power and turn away from the perception, so the proofs are safe as they are made. (ii) The coherence reading: Alan Gewirth ("The Cartesian Circle," *Philosophical Review*, 1941) and Frankfurt (1970) take the aim to be showing that reason, properly used, gives no reason to doubt itself. The proof of a non-deceiving God removes the last such reason, and the passage on "absolute falsity" shows Descartes wanted nothing more. (iii) The rule-free reading: James Van Cleve ("Foundationalism, Epistemic Principles, and the Cartesian Circle," *Philosophical Review*, 1979) separates knowing particular clear propositions from knowing the general rule. Particular clear perceptions yield knowledge before the rule is proved, and the proof of God uses them, not the rule; God's veracity is needed only to know the rule. Each reading fits some texts and strains others. Descartes' own answer is the memory distinction; whether it saves the structure is still disputed. Later critics, [Kant](../../eras/03-early-modern/immanuel-kant.md) above all, attacked the proofs of God on which the whole structure rests.

**Argument 8. Error from a will that outruns the intellect (*Meditations* 4)**

*What it proves:* that a God who is no deceiver is compatible with my making mistakes. Error comes from no faculty God gave me, but from my use of two good faculties together: a finite intellect and a will that can affirm or deny anything. The argument also yields a rule: if I assent only to what I perceive clearly and distinctly, I will not err.

Meditation 3 left a problem. If a perfect God made me, how can I ever be wrong? Meditation 4 divides judgment between two faculties. The intellect (*intellectus*, "understanding" in Haldane and Ross) only perceives ideas, and by itself neither affirms nor denies. The will (*voluntas*) affirms or denies what the intellect puts before it.

1. My intellect is finite. Of many things I have no idea, or only a confused one.
2. My will is not limited in the same way. My freedom of choice is so great that I can conceive no greater, and it is chiefly in this that I bear "the image and similitude of God" (*Med.* 4, AT VII 57).
3. Each faculty is good of its kind. What I understand, I understand correctly, and the will is "very ample and very perfect of its kind" (AT VII 58).
4. So error comes from a misuse: "since the will is much wider in its range and compass than the understanding, I do not restrain it within the same bounds, but extend it also to things which I do not understand" (AT VII 58).
5. The misuse is mine. The privation that makes an error is in the act "in so far as it proceeds from me," not in the faculty or in the act as it depends on God (AT VII 60).
Therefore God is not the author of my errors, and I avoid them by withholding judgment wherever I lack clear and distinct perception.

Two side claims matter later. Freedom does not need indifference: when the intellect sees a truth clearly, "from great clearness in my mind there followed a great inclination of my will" (AT VII 59), and indifference is "the lowest grade of liberty" (AT VII 58). And God could have made me unable to err, but the universe may be more perfect with some parts liable to error than with all alike (AT VII 61).

**Textual basis.** *Meditations* 4, AT VII 52–62. The general rule announced in Meditation 3 (AT VII 35), that what I perceive very clearly and distinctly is true, here becomes a duty of the will. *Principles* I.29–44 restates the account.

**Objection.** (i) The theodicy fails. Gassendi pressed this in the Fifth Objections: Descartes explains why God gave him no larger intellect, but "the difficulty is not so much, why God has not given you a greater faculty of knowing, as why He has given you one that falls into error" (AT VII 312). A good prince wants all his citizens good. (ii) The psychology fails. Gassendi doubted that the will reaches further than the intellect, since we cannot will what we do not in some way understand. The modern form attacks **doxastic voluntarism**, the view that belief is under the will's control. Spinoza held that will and intellect are one and the same (*Ethics* II, prop. 49, scholium, 1677); Bernard Williams ("Deciding to Believe," 1973) argued that no one can believe something just by deciding to.

**Reply.** Descartes' own, in the Fifth Replies. On (i), liability to err is not a positive defect but the lack of a greater perfection. A bad citizen is something positive relative to the state; a creature's finitude is nothing positive relative to the universe. Wanting every creature immune to error is like wanting "the whole of the human body covered with eyes" because the eye is its most beautiful part (AT VII 376). On (ii), asked where the will reaches beyond the intellect, he answers in a sentence: "You desire me here briefly to state to what the will may extend, which escapes the understanding. Precisely to everything in which we happen to err" (AT VII 376). On voluntarism the literature divides. Edwin Curley (*Descartes Against the Skeptics*, 1978) and others argue that the method needs only a weak claim, that I can suspend assent when perception is unclear, as Meditation 1 shows, and that this survives the objection to believing at will. Whether Descartes also held that I could assent against clear evidence is disputed; the letter to Mesland that AT dates, with a query, to 9 February 1645 (AT IV) is cited on that side.

**Argument 9. The ontological argument (*Meditations* 5; Caterus; Gassendi)**

*What it proves:* that God exists, from the idea of God alone, without the causal premises of Meditation 3. Existence belongs to the nature of a supremely perfect being as necessarily as angles equal to two right angles belong to the nature of a triangle.

The name is Kant's. Descartes' version does not run through Anselm's formula of a being than which none greater can be conceived. It runs through the **true and immutable natures** that open Meditation 5. When I think of a triangle I find properties I did not put there and cannot remove, and they belong to its nature whether or not any triangle exists.

1. Whatever I clearly and distinctly perceive to belong to the true and immutable nature of a thing can truly be affirmed of it.
2. My idea of God, a supremely perfect being, is the idea of such a nature, not a fiction I assembled.
3. Existence is a perfection.
4. So existence belongs to that nature: "existence can no more be separated from the essence of God than can its having its three angles equal to two right angles be separated from the essence of a [rectilinear] triangle, or the idea of a mountain from the idea of a valley" (*Med.* 5, AT VII 66).
Therefore God exists.

Descartes raises the obvious objection himself. A mountain cannot be thought without a valley, yet no mountain need exist. He answers that the mountain case shows only that the two go together if either exists, while in God's case existence itself is what cannot be separated. This is "not that my thought can bring this to pass, or impose any necessity on things, but, on the contrary, because the necessity which lies in the thing itself, i.e. the necessity of the existence of God determines me to think in this way" (AT VII 67).

**Textual basis.** *Meditations* 5, AT VII 63–71; First Objections and Replies, AT VII 99 and following; Fifth Objections, AT VII 323, and Fifth Replies, AT VII 382; *Principles* I.14–16.

**Objection.** (i) Johannes Caterus, a Catholic priest at Alkmaar who wrote the First Objections, gave the reply Aquinas had given Anselm: "Though it be conceded that an entity of the highest perfection implies its existence by its very name, yet it does not follow that that very existence is anything actual in the real world, but merely that the concept of existence is inseparably united with the concept of highest being" (AT VII 99). (ii) Gassendi denied premise 3: "existence is a perfection neither in God nor in anything else; it is rather that in the absence of which there is no perfection" (Fifth Objections, AT VII 323). A thing that does not exist has no perfections, so existence is the condition of having them, not one of them. Kant's thesis that being is not a real predicate (*Critique of Pure Reason*, A598/B626, 1781) develops this point; see the [Kant entry](../../eras/03-early-modern/immanuel-kant.md).

**Reply.** Descartes' own. To Caterus he distinguishes possible from necessary existence: "in the concept or idea of everything that is clearly and distinctly conceived, possible existence is contained, but necessary existence never, except in the idea of God alone" (First Replies, AT VII). Caterus' rule holds for ordinary concepts; the argument moves from the one concept whose content is necessary existence. To Gassendi he refuses the restriction on "property": "I do not see to what class of reality you wish to assign existence, nor do I see why it may not be said to be a property as well as omnipotence" (Fifth Replies, AT VII 382). Necessary existence, he adds, is a property in the strictest sense, since it belongs to God's essence alone. Leibniz held that Descartes must first show the idea of God to be consistent (*Meditations on Knowledge, Truth and Ideas*, 1684; see the [Leibniz entry](../../eras/03-early-modern/gottfried-wilhelm-leibniz.md)). Modal versions such as Alvin Plantinga's (*The Nature of Necessity*, 1974) keep necessary existence and drop "existence is a perfection," conceding Gassendi's point while keeping Descartes' reply to Caterus.

**Argument 10. The real distinction from clear and distinct conception (*Meditations* 6; Second and Fourth Replies)**

*What it proves:* that mind and body are **really distinct**, two substances each able to exist without the other, so that the mind could survive the body's destruction. The second edition of 1642 names this distinction in its subtitle as one of the book's two demonstrations.

In the scholastic vocabulary Descartes keeps, a real distinction holds between substances. A modal distinction holds between a substance and its mode, as between wax and its shape; a distinction of reason holds between aspects one thing has only in thought. The argument draws on three earlier results: what I am (Meditation 2), what body is (the wax and Meditation 5), and God's guarantee of clear and distinct perception (Meditations 3–4).

1. Whatever I clearly and distinctly understand can be made by God just as I understand it.
2. So "it suffices that I am able to apprehend one thing apart from another clearly and distinctly in order to be certain that the one is different from the other, since they may be made to exist in separation at least by the omnipotence of God" (*Med.* 6, AT VII 78).
3. I have "a clear and distinct idea of myself inasmuch as I am only a thinking and unextended thing," and "a distinct idea of body, inasmuch as it is only an extended and unthinking thing" (AT VII 78).
4. So God can make each exist without the other, and things that can exist apart are really distinct.
Therefore my mind is really distinct from my body and can exist without it.

The Second Replies set this out in geometrical order as Proposition IV (AT VII 169–170), and note that God's power is named only as a measure of what is possible; no extraordinary power is needed for the separation.

**Textual basis.** *Meditations* 6, AT VII 78; Second Replies, AT VII 169–170; Fourth Objections, AT VII 200–204; Fourth Replies, AT VII 221–225; *Principles* I.60.

**Objection.** Antoine Arnauld, then a young Sorbonne theologian, gave the classic counterexample in the Fourth Objections. Someone may know that the triangle in a semicircle is right-angled while doubting, or by a fallacy denying, Pythagoras' theorem. By Descartes' rule he could argue: "But I clearly and distinctly understand that this triangle is right-angled, without comprehending that the square on its base is equal to the squares on its sides" (AT VII 202). So God could make a right triangle without the property, which is absurd. Conceiving one thing without another shows only that I can think of them apart. Arnauld adds that my knowledge of myself as a thinking thing may be incomplete, an abstraction, as a geometer conceives a line without breadth. Modern discussion of whether conceivability entails possibility, in Saul Kripke (*Naming and Necessity*, 1980) and David Chalmers (*The Conscious Mind*, 1996), descends from this exchange.

**Reply.** Descartes' own, in the Fourth Replies. The rule needs each thing to be understood as a **complete thing**: "by a complete thing I mean merely a substance endowed with those forms or attributes which suffice to let me recognise that it is a substance" (AT VII 222). Complete understanding is not adequate understanding, which would grasp every property and belongs to God alone. Arnauld's case fails because "the ratio between the square on the base and those on the sides is not a complete thing," because that ratio is understood only in a right triangle anyway, and because the triangle cannot be distinctly understood if the ratio is denied (AT VII 225). Mind, by contrast, is understood as complete while everything bodily is denied of it. Whether this works is disputed. Margaret Wilson (*Descartes*, 1978) argued that the reply needs the claim that I know thought to be my whole essence, which is what the argument was to prove. On John Cottingham's reading, the argument stands or falls with the step from *nothing else is known to belong to my essence* to *nothing else belongs*, the step Arnauld had already flagged.

**Argument 11. The divisibility argument (*Meditations* 6)**

*What it proves:* that the mind is not the body, because the body is divisible and the mind is not. It is an independent route to the real distinction, by **Leibniz's law**: if one thing has a property the other lacks, they are not the same.

1. Every body is extended, and whatever is extended can be divided, at least in thought.
2. When I consider my mind, "that is to say, myself inasmuch as I am only a thinking thing," I cannot distinguish parts in it; I apprehend myself as one and entire (*Med.* 6, AT VII 86).
3. Its faculties are not parts: "the faculties of willing, feeling, conceiving, etc. cannot be properly speaking said to be its parts, for it is one and the same mind which employs itself in willing and in feeling and understanding" (AT VII 86).
4. If a foot or an arm is cut off, nothing is taken from the mind.
5. So "there is a great difference between mind and body, inasmuch as body is by nature always divisible, and the mind is entirely indivisible" (AT VII 85–86).
Therefore mind and body are distinct.

Descartes rates it modestly. It "would be sufficient to teach me that the mind or soul of man is entirely different from the body, if I had not already learned it from other sources" (AT VII 86). The Synopsis gives it a further use: since the body's corruption is a division of its parts, it does not by itself imply the mind's destruction (AT VII 13). That is the nearest the *Meditations* comes to arguing for immortality.

**Textual basis.** *Meditations* 6, AT VII 85–86; Synopsis, AT VII 13; *Passions* I.30 (AT XI 351), on why we cannot conceive half or a third of a soul.

**Objection.** (i) Premise 2 begs the question. It reports how the mind seems when I attend only to thinking. A materialist says the mind is divisible because the brain is, and that introspection does not show its parts because it does not show its structure. (ii) Kant's second paralogism (*Critique of Pure Reason*, A351–361, 1781) gives the deeper form: the unity of the "I think" is a formal condition of thought, not a perception of a simple substance, and nothing follows from it about what does the thinking. (iii) After surgery severing the corpus callosum, some patients act in tests as if two streams of awareness were running. Thomas Nagel ("Brain Bisection and the Unity of Consciousness," 1971) argued that such cases leave no clear answer to how many minds there are.

**Reply.** The literature's; Descartes left only premise 3 as a defence. The usual line is that the argument rests on Argument 10's result that the essence of mind is thought, plus the claim that a thought has no spatial parts. Half a doubt makes no sense, though half a brain does. On this reading the two arguments stand or fall together, as Descartes' remark about "other sources" suggests. On split-brain cases a Cartesian can say that what divides is the bodily machinery the mind uses. Many find this ad hoc.

**Argument 12. The proof of the external world (*Meditations* 6)**

*What it proves:* that bodies exist and cause my sensory ideas, and that they have at least the properties I clearly and distinctly perceive in them, the properties of geometry. It does not prove that bodies resemble my sensations of colour, heat or taste.

Meditation 6 first argues that bodies probably exist, because imagining a figure takes an effort that pure understanding does not, which a closely joined body would explain (AT VII 72–73). Descartes calls this only probable. The proof proper follows the real distinction.

1. I have "a certain passive faculty of perception, that is, of receiving and recognising the ideas of sensible things" (*Med.* 6, AT VII 79).
2. A passive faculty would be useless unless some active faculty produced the ideas.
3. That active faculty is not in me as a thinking thing. It presupposes no understanding (Latin *intellectio*; H&R has "thought"), and "those ideas are often produced in me without my contributing in any way to the same, and often even against my will" (AT VII 79).
4. So it is in some other substance containing, formally or eminently, the reality the ideas contain objectively (the causal principle of Argument 5): a body, God, or a creature nobler than body.
5. God has given me no faculty for recognising any cause but bodies, and instead "a very great inclination to believe [that they are sent to me or] that they are conveyed to me by corporeal objects" (AT VII 79–80).
6. If the cause were anything but bodies, God would be a deceiver. He is not.
Therefore "we must allow that corporeal things exist" (AT VII 80).

The conclusion is limited at once: bodies "are perhaps not exactly what we perceive by the senses" (AT VII 80). The rest of the Meditation treats the senses as reliable guides to what helps or harms the mind-body union, not to the nature of bodies.

**Textual basis.** *Meditations* 6, AT VII 72–80; *Principles* II.1, where the proof opens the physics.

**Objection.** (i) The proof is only as good as the proofs of God, so it inherits the Cartesian circle (Argument 7). (ii) Premise 5 rests on a natural inclination, and Meditation 3 told us to distrust natural impulses (AT VII 38–39). Why does this one count? (iii) George Berkeley accepted premises 1–4 and denied the rest. In *A Treatise Concerning the Principles of Human Knowledge* (1710, §§18–20) he argued that matter is idle: our ideas would be as they are if no bodies existed, and their order is best explained by God producing them directly. Malebranche, within Cartesianism, had already held that the existence of bodies cannot be strictly demonstrated and is known in the end by faith (*Search after Truth*, Elucidation 6, 1678). See the [Berkeley](../../eras/03-early-modern/george-berkeley.md) and [Malebranche](../../eras/03-early-modern/nicolas-malebranche.md) entries.

**Reply.** On (ii), Descartes' text marks the difference. The impulses of Meditation 3 could be corrected by the natural light. Here God has given "no faculty to recognise" another cause (AT VII 79). An inclination that no faculty I have could ever correct would make its author a deceiver if it were false. On (iii), the reply usually made for Descartes is that Berkeley's God produces ideas as if bodies caused them while there are none, which is the deception premise 6 rules out. Berkeley answers that the inclination is a philosophers' prejudice, and that ordinary people believe only in the things they see and touch, which he keeps.

**Argument 13. Elisabeth's interaction objection and Descartes' replies (letters of 1643)**

*What it proves:* on Elisabeth's side, that Descartes' account cannot explain how an unextended mind and an extended body act on each other. On Descartes' side, that their union is a primitive notion, known through ordinary life and the senses, and that the question applies notions proper to bodies to the soul.

Elisabeth of Bohemia (1618–1680), daughter of the exiled Frederick V, lived at The Hague. She opened the correspondence on 6/16 May 1643; the double date gives the Julian and Gregorian calendars. No public-domain English translation of her letters exists, so her arguments are paraphrased and her French quoted in a few words in my translation. The same constraint governs this dossier's English quotations of Descartes, which use Haldane and Ross (1911) because it is public domain, not because it is the edition to read. See the [early modern women philosophers entry](../../eras/03-early-modern/early-modern-women-philosophers.md).

Her first letter (AT III 661) argues:

1. Every determination of motion comes from the impulsion of the thing moved, from the manner in which the mover pushes it, or from the quality and shape of the mover's surface.
2. The first two require contact; the third requires extension.
3. Descartes excludes extension from the soul, and contact seems incompatible with an immaterial thing.
Therefore it is unclear how the soul, "n'estant qu'vne substance pensante" ("being only a thinking substance," my translation), can move the bodily spirits to produce voluntary action.

Descartes replied on 21 May 1643 (AT III 663–668). We have a few **primitive notions**, patterns for all our other knowledge: being, number and duration, which apply to everything; extension, for body; thought, for the soul; and the union of soul and body, on which the notion of the soul's power to move the body depends (AT III 665). Confusion comes from explaining one by another, and Elisabeth's question applies notions of impact to the soul. As an analogy he offered heaviness as the scholastics conceived it, a quality moving a body toward the earth without contact (AT III 667–668).

Elisabeth was not satisfied (10/20 June 1643, AT III 683–685). A power falsely ascribed to a quality could not persuade her that something immaterial moves a body. She could conceive the immaterial only as the negation of matter, which can have no communication with it, and it would be easier for her to "concéder la matière & l'extension a l'ame" ("grant matter and extension to the soul," my translation) than to grant an immaterial being the power to move a body and be moved by it (AT III 685).

Descartes' second reply (28 June 1643, AT III 690–695) shifted ground. Metaphysics makes the notion of the soul familiar and mathematics that of body, but it is "en vsant seulement de la vie & des conuersations ordinaires" ("only by living and through ordinary conversation," my translation) that one learns to conceive the union (AT III 692). The mind cannot conceive distinction and union at once, since that means conceiving soul and body "comme vne seule chose, & ensemble les conceuoir comme deux, ce qui se contrarie" ("as one single thing, and at the same time as two, which is contradictory," my translation; AT III 693). So she should freely attribute matter and extension to the soul, for that is just to conceive it as united to the body; she will then see that this extension differs from body's, since it excludes no other extension from its place (AT III 694).

**Textual basis.** The four letters above (AT III 660–695); *Meditations* 6, where I am "not only lodged in my body as a pilot in a vessel, but that I am very closely united to it" (AT VII 81); *Passions of the Soul* (1649) I.31–41 (AT XI 351–360) on the pineal gland. Article 41 (AT XI 359–360) states the mechanism: "the whole action of the soul consists in this, that solely because it desires something, it causes the little gland to which it is closely united to move in the way requisite to produce the effect which relates to this desire."

**Objection.** Elisabeth's rejoinder is the strongest form. The heaviness analogy compares the mind to a quality Descartes says does not exist. "Primitive notion" names the problem without solving it. And if the union is known only by the senses, the metaphysics that proved the distinction is silent on the most familiar fact of life, that I move my arm. The pineal gland moves the contact problem to a small place without removing it, as Spinoza complained in the preface to *Ethics* V (1677). See the [Spinoza](../../eras/03-early-modern/baruch-spinoza.md) entry.

**Reply.** Descartes' two replies are above: the union is a primitive notion with its own evidence, and the soul's kind of extension does not compete with body's. The literature divides on whether this is an answer. John Cottingham ("Cartesian Trialism," 1985) takes the three primitive notions at face value: the human being is a third category, and asking for a mechanism of the union is like asking what pushes a falling stone. Others hold that Descartes never answered and that Elisabeth saw it. Leibniz claimed that Descartes let the mind change the direction of the spirits' motion but not its quantity (*Theodicy* §60, 1710; *Monadology* §80); Daniel Garber ("Mind, Body, and the Laws of Nature in Descartes and Leibniz," 1983) argued that no text of Descartes says so, and the attribution is now usually treated as Leibniz's reconstruction.

**Argument 14. Animals as machines: the language and action tests (*Discourse* V; letter to More, 1649)**

*What it proves:* that two signs, language and flexible general-purpose action, reliably mark a thinking soul, and that since no animal shows them, animal behaviour can be explained mechanically, without thought.

*Discourse* V (1637) summarises the mechanical physiology of the unpublished *Treatise on Man*, then asks how we would tell a machine shaped like us from a human being.

1. Such machines would fail two tests. The first is language: they "could never use speech or other signs as we do when placing our thoughts on record for the benefit of others" (*Discourse* V, AT VI 56). A machine might cry out when touched, but "it never happens that it arranges its speech in various ways, in order to reply appropriately to everything that may be said in its presence, as even the lowest type of man can do" (AT VI 56–57).
2. The second is action: machines "infallibly fall short in others, by the which means we may discover that they did not act from knowledge, but only from the disposition of their organs. For while reason is a universal instrument which can serve for all contingencies, these organs have need of some special adaptation for every particular action" (AT VI 57).
3. The same tests separate humans from animals. Every human being arranges words to make thoughts known, and those born deaf and mute invent signs. No animal does, and not for lack of organs: "magpies and parrots are able to utter words just like ourselves, and yet they cannot speak as we do, that is, so as to give evidence that they think of what they say" (AT VI 57).
4. Where animals do one thing better than we do, they act like "a clock, which is only composed of wheels and weights" (AT VI 59).
Therefore animals have no reason, and their behaviour is the working of bodily machines.

To Henry More, on 5 February 1649, Descartes restated and limited the claim. Language is "vnicum ... cogitationis in corpore latentis signum certum" ("the one certain sign of thought hidden in a body," my translation; AT V 278). It cannot be proved that animals think, but neither can it be demonstrated that they do not, since the human mind does not reach into their hearts (AT V 276–277). He denies them thought, not life or sensation: "vitam enim nulli animali denego" ("for I deny life to no animal," my translation), nor sensation in so far as it depends on a bodily organ (AT V 278). The letter to the Marquess of Newcastle of 23 November 1646 makes the same case, with the parrot at AT IV 575, and is optional.

**Textual basis.** *Discourse* V, AT VI 55–59; to More, 5 February 1649, AT V 275–279; *Passions* I.16 (AT XI 341).

**Objection.** (i) Montaigne's "Apology for Raymond Sebond" (*Essays* II.12, 1580) had argued that animals communicate and reason in ways we fail to understand, and that our failure is no proof they lack minds; see the [Montaigne entry](../../eras/03-early-modern/michel-de-montaigne.md). More pressed the point in his letter of 11 December 1648 (AT V 243–245): the signs of fear and cunning in dogs and horses are too like ours to be mechanical. (ii) The tests are tests for human-like reason, so they cannot show that animals lack all thought or feeling. (iii) The action test is a seventeenth-century bet about how flexible a machine can be.

**Reply.** Descartes' own, to More. On (i), the belief in animal thought rests on one analogy, that their organs and movements resemble ours. Stronger reasons point the other way: their behaviour can be explained mechanically, as convulsions show it can in us, and it is less probable that worms and gnats have immortal souls than that they move like machines (AT V 277). On (ii), he concedes the logical point and claims only probability, while keeping sensation in the bodily sense. On (iii) the literature takes over. Alan Turing ("Computing Machinery and Intelligence," 1950) turned the language test into a criterion and bet that machines could pass it. Whether current language models do, and whether passing would show thought, is disputed in terms Descartes set: whether replies fitted "to everything that may be said" can come from "the disposition of their organs" alone. On animals, Locke (*Essay* II.xi.11, 1690) and Hume (*Treatise* I.iii.16, 1739) rejected the denial of animal thought while keeping language as distinctively human.

**Argument 15. Matter is extension, so there is no vacuum (*Principles* II.4, 11, 16)**

*What it proves:* that the whole nature of body is extension in length, breadth and depth, that space and body are one in reality, and so that a space empty of all body is a contradiction.

*Principles of Philosophy* (Latin 1644, French 1647) builds the physics on the *Meditations*. Part II opens with the proof of the external world (Argument 12) and asks what bodies are.

1. The nature of body consists "not in weight, nor in hardness, nor colour and so on, but in extension alone" (*Principles* II.4, title, AT VIIIA 42).
2. We know hardness only as resistance to our hands. But "if, whenever we moved our hands in some direction, all the bodies in that part retreated as soon as our hands approached them, we should never feel hardness" (II.4, AT VIIIA 42), and the bodies would still be bodies. The same reasoning removes weight, colour and every sensible quality.
3. What remains is the same as space: "there is nothing remaining in the idea of body excepting that it is extended in length, breadth, and depth; and this is comprised in our idea of space" (II.11, AT VIIIA 46). Space and body differ only in how we conceive them (II.10, 12).
4. Nothing has no properties, and "it is absolutely inconceivable that nothing should possess extension" (II.16, AT VIIIA 49).
Therefore wherever there is extension there is extended substance, and a vacuum in the philosophical sense is impossible (II.16).

If God removed all the body from a vessel and let nothing replace it, "the sides of the vessel will thereby come into immediate contiguity with one another," because "distance is a mode of extension, and without extended substance it cannot therefore exist" (II.18, AT VIIIA 50). An "empty" pitcher lacks only what we expected, such as water; it is still full of air (II.17). Descartes repeated the vessel point to More (AT V 272). The consequences are large: no atoms and no edge to the world (II.20–22), and all change is motion in a full world, a **plenum**, which leads to the vortex physics of Parts III and IV.

**Textual basis.** *Principles* II.4–22 (AT VIIIA 42–52); *The World* (written 1629–33, published 1664); *Meditations* 5 on extension as the essence of material things; to More, 5 February 1649, AT V 272–275.

**Objection.** (i) Experiment. Torricelli's mercury tube (1643–44) left an apparently empty space above the mercury. Pascal's *New Experiments Concerning the Vacuum* (1647) and the Puy-de-Dôme experiment, made for him by his brother-in-law Florin Périer on 19 September 1648, showed the column falling with altitude, as air pressure explains, and Pascal argued that the space contained no detectable matter. Descartes wrote to Mersenne on 13 December 1647 that he had advised Pascal "d'expérimenter si le vif-argent montoit aussi haut" ("to test whether the quicksilver rose as high," my translation) on a mountain as at its foot (AT V 99); who first proposed the test is disputed. See the [Pascal entry](../../eras/03-early-modern/blaise-pascal.md). (ii) Metaphysics. Henry More argued that spirits and God are extended without being bodies, so extension cannot be what makes a body; in his letter of 11 December 1648 he concluded that God is extended in his own way (AT V 239). (iii) Dynamics. Leibniz argued that extension is passive and presupposes something extended, and that resistance and inertia need an active principle, force (*Brief Demonstration of a Notable Error of Descartes*, 1686; *Specimen Dynamicum*, 1695).

**Reply.** Descartes' own on (i) and (ii); the literature's on (iii). On (i), the experiments show only that the space holds no air or mercury, the ordinary emptiness II.17 allows; a subtler matter passing through the glass fills it, and the column's fall shows the weight of the air. Pascal answered that nothing detects this matter, and later physics agreed with him. On (ii), Descartes told More that the mind is extended only by reason of its power, which it can apply to larger or smaller bodies, not by reason of its substance (15 April 1649, AT V 347). On (iii), Malebranche accepted that bodies have no force of their own and placed all force in God, which is occasionalism; Leibniz put force back into substance. Descartes' identification of space with matter lost to Newton. His rule that physics deals only in what can be described mathematically, size, shape and motion, outlived it.
