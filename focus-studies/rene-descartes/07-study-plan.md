# Study Plan — Descartes in Seven Weeks

## What this plan is, and where it plugs in

The [learning path](../../learning-path.md) meets Descartes at the opening of **Stage 3 (Early Modern to Kant)**, as the stage's first core figure (●) at row 1. Its block is short by design: the *Discourse on the Method* Parts I–IV as an optional warm-up, then *Meditations* 1, 2, 3, 5 and 6 **in order**, plus Princess Elisabeth's objections in selection, which the path calls the sharpest contemporary pushback on dualism. Its editions are Cress (Hackett) or Cottingham (Cambridge, with the Objections and Replies), and its warning is plain: not Haldane & Ross. This dossier keeps those editions and texts and expands the block to seven weeks, with three additions. It reads *Meditation* 4, as the [study guide](../../study/descartes-meditations.md) advises, because the account of error is what makes the truth rule a rule. It reads the Objections and Replies beside the meditations they answer, since Descartes published them as part of the book. And it follows the system into the *Principles*, *Discourse* V and the *Passions*, so that the mind–body problem is met with the mechanism it was built for.

Run it as one block in place of Stage 3's Descartes row, before Pascal (row 2) and Spinoza (row 3). Pascal's vacuum experiments are aimed at Week 6's plenum, and Spinoza, Malebranche and Leibniz each take up Elisabeth's question from Week 5. [02-works-and-texts.md](02-works-and-texts.md), "The works in the dossier's reading order," gives the reasons for the order work by work.

**Total cost, stated honestly: ≈45 hours all-in across seven weeks: 33 hours at the desk, ≈5.7 hours of assigned media, and ≈6.5 hours of the daily [Anki](https://apps.ankiweb.net) [FSRS](https://docs.ankiweb.net/deck-options.html) queue (about 8 minutes a day for 49 days, 392 minutes).** The table below rounds each week's 56 minutes of cards to 0.9 hours, so its columns sum to 33 + 5.7 + 6.3 = 45.0; with the cards unrounded the total is 45.2. Weekly totals run from about 5.4 to 7.8 hours. Optional items are not in the total. Media hours are partly estimates. [08-bibliography.md](08-bibliography.md) gives measured lengths for the *In Our Time* programme and Millican's parts, but none for Adamson's episodes, which this plan prices at the path's own Stage 3 rate (≈14 hours for eps. 462–502, about 20 minutes each). Wi-Phi (20 minutes) and Millican's Hobbes part (15) are estimates too.

**The accounting against the path, stated plainly: these hours sit on top of Stage 3's budget (16 weeks, 72 desk hours, ≈111 all-in), they are not hidden inside it.** The path fixes no week count for its Descartes row. At Stage 3's rate of about 4.5 desk hours a week, the row reads as roughly two weeks, about 9 desk hours; that is this dossier's estimate, not the path's figure. Most of the media is already in the path: Adamson's Descartes run (eps. 466–475) lies inside its Stage 3 audio spine (eps. 462–502), Millican's Descartes parts inside its in-stage *General Philosophy* subset, and Wi-Phi is its own watch-before. About 4.9 of this plan's 5.7 media hours are therefore not new cost; the 45-minute *In Our Time* programme is. Count a net addition of roughly 30 hours to Stage 3, again an estimate. The [Kant dossier](../immanuel-kant/README.md) sits at the stage's other end at ≈47 hours all-in by [its own plan](../immanuel-kant/07-study-plan.md). If its net addition is of the same order, running both adds roughly 60 hours to the path's longest stage, an estimate. They fit together well, since Kant's attacks on the ontological argument and on Cartesian idealism are aimed at Weeks 4 and 5 here, but choose deliberately. The [Spinoza dossier](../baruch-spinoza/README.md) is a third Stage 3 option of similar size. The path's 12-month trim (§10) refuses to cut Stage 3, so the path's Descartes row survives it; this dossier's extra hours do not fit inside the trim's ≈335.

**Reading texts.** Follow the path and [08-bibliography.md](08-bibliography.md), tier 1. The *Meditations* in Cottingham's Cambridge selection or in Cress: pick one and do not alternate. With Cottingham, use CSM II for objections his selection omits; with Cress, add Ariew and Cress's *Meditations, Objections, and Replies* (Hackett). The *Discourse* in Cress or Clarke. The *Rules*, *Principles* and *Passions* in CSM I, or Ariew's one-volume Hackett reader. The Elisabeth letters in Lisa Shapiro's Chicago edition, which prints both sides. [02-works-and-texts.md](02-works-and-texts.md), "Editions and translations," maps volumes to weeks. **Not Haldane & Ross as your reading copy:** the dossier quotes it only because it is public domain and can be checked word for word, and this plan quotes nothing.

**Cite by Adam–Tannery (AT), and budget by part and article.** Every edition above prints AT volume and page in the margin: AT VI for the *Discourse*, AT VII for the Latin *Meditations* with the Objections and Replies, AT VIIIA for the *Principles*, AT X for the *Rules*, AT XI for the *Passions*, AT III–V for the letters. The *Principles* and *Passions* are also cited by part and article. Editions paginate differently, so each week's target is given as parts, articles and AT ranges plus a session length, not as a page count. [02-works-and-texts.md](02-works-and-texts.md), "A note on citation before the works" and "Latin and French, and why the difference matters," explain the system and why *cogitatio* (French *pensée*), "thought," covers every conscious state and not just reasoning.

**The guide is your companion, by design.** The library's [study guide to the *Meditations*](../../study/descartes-meditations.md), with its walkthroughs, three reconstructions and fourteen checkpoints with answers, is not duplicated here. Weeks 2–5 assign its sections, reconstructions (a)–(c) and checkpoints by number; the checkpoints printed in those weeks are the dossier's own additions.

## Week 1 — Method: *Discourse* I–IV, *Rules* 1–4 (~5.4 hrs: 4 desk + 0.5 media + 0.9 cards)

**Primary.** The *Discourse on the Method*, Parts I–IV, in Cress or Clarke (AT VI 1–40): short and easy, about two hours. The path calls it optional; here it comes first because every later week assumes its method. Part I (AT VI 1–10) is the verdict on his schooling. Part II (AT VI 11–22) is the method, with the four rules at AT VI 18–19; write them down from memory. Part III (AT VI 22–31) is the provisional morality. Part IV (AT VI 31–40) gives the first published cogito (AT VI 32) and short proofs of the soul and of God, a sketch the *Meditations* will redo with full rigor. Then the *Rules for the Direction of the Mind*, Rules 1–4, in CSM I (AT X 359–379), the unfinished Latin treatise of the late 1620s. Read Rule 3 for its definitions of intuition and deduction and Rule 4 for the idea of method, and let the mathematics pass.

**Alongside.** [01-life-and-times.md](01-life-and-times.md), from "La Flèche" through "The *Discourse* and the *Meditations*," including "The stove-heated room and the three dreams"; [02-works-and-texts.md](02-works-and-texts.md), its two opening sections and the *Discourse* entry; [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md), "A system built in order," "The tree of philosophy," "Method and the order of reasons" and "Morality: provisional and late"; [05-conversations.md](05-conversations.md), "The schools: La Flèche, Eustachius, Suárez" and "Montaigne and the Pyrrhonian revival." In Session B, the SEP's ["Descartes' Life and Works"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/descartes-works/), and the IEP's ["Scientific Method"](https://iep.utm.edu/descartes-scientific-method/) if you want more on the method.

**Media.** *Preview:* Peter Adamson, [*History of Philosophy Without Any Gaps*](https://historyofphilosophy.net/podcast/all-episodes), ep. 466, ["Well Hidden: Descartes' Life and Works"](https://historyofphilosophy.net/podcast/early-modern/france-and-netherlands/descartes-life-works), before the week's reading. Peter Millican, [*General Philosophy*](https://podcasts.ox.ac.uk/series/general-philosophy), part [1.4, "From Galileo to Descartes"](https://podcasts.ox.ac.uk/14-galileo-descartes) (about 11 minutes), also before.

**Checkpoints (book closed).**
1. *State the four rules of Discourse II and say which one the Meditations build on.* Accept nothing as true that you do not evidently know to be so, judging only what is presented so clearly and distinctly that there is no occasion to doubt it; divide each difficulty into parts; proceed in order from the simplest; make enumerations so complete that nothing is left out (AT VI 18–19). The first becomes the method of doubt and later the truth rule (*Med.* 3, AT VII 35). [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md), "Method and the order of reasons."
2. *What are the maxims of the provisional morality, and why does a doubter need them?* Obey the laws and customs of his country, keeping his religion and the most moderate opinions; be firm in action once decided, like a traveller lost in a forest who walks straight on; master himself rather than fortune, changing his desires rather than the world. A closing resolution chooses the cultivation of reason (AT VI 22–28). Doubt is for knowledge, not for action, and life cannot wait for certainty. The same rules return in the letter to Elisabeth of 4 August 1645 (Week 5). [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md), "Morality: provisional and late."
3. *How do the Rules define intuition and deduction, and where is the seed of the circle?* Intuition (*intuitus*) is an attentive mind's grasp so easy and distinct that no doubt remains; deduction is a continuous movement of thought linking intuitions (Rule 3). A long deduction cannot be seen in one glance, so it depends on memory, and Week 3's reply to the circle turns on just this difference between the present and the remembered. [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 7.
4. *What does Discourse IV leave out that the Meditations put in?* The deceiving God and the evil genius. The *Discourse* doubts demonstrations only because people make mistakes in reasoning, not because a maker might have built me to err in what seems most evident. The structure that invites the circle is already there, since Part IV says the rule of clear and distinct conception is assured only because God exists (AT VI 38); the *Meditations* add the doubt that makes the circle bite. Part IV also states the cogito with a "therefore" (AT VI 32), which the *Meditations* drop ([06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md), "Misconceptions, in full form").

**Reconstructions.** No numbered argument yet. As a warm-up, write the route of *Discourse* IV in ten numbered lines, book closed, and keep it for Week 2. It is not in the cadence below.

**Cards.** The first 2 cards of the dossier's deck, [cards.tsv](cards.tsv), both concept cards: the tree of philosophy and the provisional morality. Import them after the week's reading, never before. Deck at 2.

## Week 2 — Doubt, the cogito and the wax: the Synopsis, *Meditations* 1–2 (~7.5 hrs: 4.5 desk + 2.1 media + 0.9 cards)

**Primary.** The Preface to the Reader (AT VII 7–11), the Synopsis (AT VII 12–16), then *Meditation* 1 (AT VII 17–23) and *Meditation* 2 (AT VII 23–34), in Cottingham or Cress. The Preface says the book is to be read as a meditation you perform with him. Read the Synopsis first and again after *Meditation* 2. *Meditation* 1 has three stages: the senses, the dreaming argument (AT VII 18–20), and the deceiving God, with the evil genius (*genius malignus*) at AT VII 22. Try each doubt on your own beliefs before reading on. *Meditation* 2 finds the cogito (AT VII 24–25), asks what the "I" is (AT VII 25–29), and ends with the wax (AT VII 29–34), which is worth two readings, one for the story and one for the argument.

**The guide.** Its Meditation I and II sections, before each meditation and after; its reconstruction **(a)** (the cogito), written book-closed before you open it; its **checkpoints 1–5** as the week's extended set in Session C. Its answer to checkpoint 4 quotes the cogito sentence in Cottingham's wording, while the dossier's files quote it in Haldane & Ross (AT VII 25); learn whichever your edition prints.

**Alongside.** [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md), "Doubt and the cogito" and "Thought and clear and distinct perception"; [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Arguments 1–4; [05-conversations.md](05-conversations.md), "Augustine: *si fallor, sum*"; [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md), live debate (2). In Session B, the SEP's ["Descartes' Epistemology"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/descartes-epistemology/), which also serves Week 3.

**Media.** *Watch-before:* one or two of [Wi-Phi](https://www.wi-phi.com/)'s short epistemology videos, the path's watch-before for this week (about 20 minutes, an estimate). *Review:* Adamson ep. 468, ["Perchance to Dream: Descartes' Skeptical Method"](https://historyofphilosophy.net/podcast/early-modern/france-and-netherlands/descartes-skeptical-method), after *Meditation* 1; ep. 470, ["Gary Hatfield on Descartes' Meditations"](https://historyofphilosophy.net/podcast/early-modern/france-and-netherlands/descartes-meditations-hatfield), after *Meditation* 2. BBC Radio 4, *In Our Time*, ["Cogito Ergo Sum"](https://www.bbc.co.uk/programmes/b010mvcp) (28 April 2011, 45 minutes), after your reconstruction of the cogito. Millican, [4.1, "Scepticism about the External World"](https://podcasts.ox.ac.uk/41-scepticism-about-external-world) and [4.2, "Possible Answers to External World Scepticism"](https://podcasts.ox.ac.uk/42-possible-answers-external-world-scepticism) (about 9 minutes each), at the end of the week.

**Checkpoints (the dossier's own).**
1. *What does the dreaming argument leave standing, and why does that force a second argument?* It removes every sensory judgment about particular bodies, since nothing inside an experience marks it as waking (AT VII 19). It leaves the simple elements dream images are built from, as a painter's siren is built from real colours, and so it leaves arithmetic and geometry (AT VII 20). Then give the self-undermining objection (Austin, Malcolm) and the reply that the argument needs only the possibility that this experience is a dream. [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 1.
2. *Which hypothesis threatens arithmetic, the deceiving God or the evil genius?* The deceiving God (AT VII 21): an all-powerful maker could have made me err even in adding two and three, and a lesser origin makes error likelier still. The evil genius (AT VII 22) is a working fiction that keeps the doubt in force against habit and adds no new reason. Then state Hume's charge that such a doubt is incurable, and Descartes' grading of it as slight, held for knowledge and not for action (AT VII 22, 36). [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 2.
3. *Why does Descartes deny that the cogito is a syllogism, and what replaces it?* A syllogism would need the major premise that whatever thinks exists, known in advance; he says that general truth is learned from the particular case, and the thinker sees his existence by a simple act of mental vision (*simplici mentis intuitu*, AT VII 140). Then name the inference, intuition and performance (Hintikka) readings and one objection to each. [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 3; [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md), live debate (2).
4. *What does the wax show, and what is Gassendi's objection?* That bodies are perceived by an inspection of the mind (*inspectio*), not by sense or imagination: every sensory quality changed while the wax remained, and what remains, something extended, flexible and changeable, can take more shapes than I can picture (AT VII 30–31). So the mind is known better than any body (AT VII 33). Gassendi: abstraction leaves only an unknown substratum (AT VII 271–273). Descartes: what remains is extension, the clearly understood nature of body (AT VII 359). [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 4.

**Reconstructions.** Dreaming and the deceiving God as one (Arguments 1–2), and the cogito (Argument 3; guide (a) is the check, not the source), written before the *In Our Time* programme. Set your Week 1 sketch beside it and note in two sentences what the *Meditations* add. The wax (Argument 4) is carried by its card.

**Cards.** +5 from [cards.tsv](cards.tsv): the argument cards for Arguments 1, 2, 3 and 4, and the concept card on *cogitatio* (thought). If you imported the library's shipped Descartes cards from [`study/cards/`](../../study/cards/README.md), suspend any that duplicate these. Deck at 7.

## Week 3 — God and the circle: *Meditation* 3, the Second and Fourth Replies (~6.2 hrs: 5 desk + 0.3 media + 0.9 cards)

**Primary.** *Meditation* 3 (AT VII 34–52), the hardest meditation, in two passes as the guide advises. First the machinery: the truth rule (AT VII 35), the graded doubt (AT VII 36), the three kinds of ideas (AT VII 37–38), formal and objective reality and the causal principle (AT VII 40–42). Then the trademark argument (AT VII 45–47, 51) and the argument from continued existence (AT VII 47–51). Keep a glossary of the scholastic terms; they return in Weeks 4 and 6. Then about 30 pages of Objections and Replies: the Second Objections, where Mersenne first raises the circle (AT VII 124–125), and the Second Replies on the cogito, remembered conclusions, the atheist geometer and the standard of certainty (AT VII 140–146); Arnauld on the circle (AT VII 214) and the Fourth Replies (AT VII 245–246); and the First Objections and Replies on objective reality and conservation (AT VII 92–95, 102–111). If time is short, leave Caterus for Week 4.

**The guide.** Its Meditation III section and "The Cartesian circle — a debate, not a verdict"; its reconstruction **(b)**, written book-closed before checking; its **checkpoints 6–8 and 10**, with the correction to 10 noted above.

**Alongside.** [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md), "Ideas: their reality and their origin," "God, conservation and the eternal truths," and tension (1); [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Arguments 5–7; [05-conversations.md](05-conversations.md), "Mersenne and the Objections" and "Arnauld: the circle and the triangle"; [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md), live debate (1). In Session B, the SEP's ["Descartes' Theory of Ideas"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/descartes-ideas/), and the entry on [Suárez and Grotius](../../eras/03-early-modern/suarez-and-grotius.md) if the scholastic vocabulary stalls you.

**Media.** *Review:* Adamson ep. 471, ["Unclear and Indistinct Ideas: Debating the Meditations"](https://historyofphilosophy.net/podcast/early-modern/france-and-netherlands/descartes-debating-meditations), after your reconstruction of the circle.

**Checkpoints (the dossier's own).**
1. *Caterus says objective reality is nothing actual and needs no cause. What does Descartes reply, and why does the argument need the reply?* Caterus: being thought of is an external label on a thing (AT VII 92). Descartes: he means the idea, and the sun existing objectively in the mind is a lesser mode of being but not nothing (AT VII 102–103). Without that, the causal principle has nothing to apply to. [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 5.
2. *Hume says the idea of God is built by amplifying our own qualities without limit. How does Descartes answer this kind of objection?* By number: I can always count higher, so I have received a power to conceive a number beyond any I can think, from something more perfect than myself (AT VII 139); the power of amplifying itself needs a source (AT VII 365). And the infinite is known before the finite, since I know my lack only by comparison (AT VII 45). Critics say this restates the causal principle instead of defending it. [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 5.
3. *Why does the argument from continued existence need creation and conservation to differ only in reason, and what is Gassendi's objection?* Because it asks what keeps me in existence now: if the moments of my life are independent, some cause must, in effect, create me again at each (AT VII 48–49). Gassendi: effects outlast causes, as a house its builder (AT VII 300–301). Descartes: builders cause becoming, God causes being, as the sun its light (AT VII 369–370). Then name the cost Hume saw and Malebranche built on: creatures seem to do no causal work. [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 6.
4. *State the Cartesian circle, who raised it first, Descartes' reply, and two readings of it.* The truth rule rests on God, and God is known only by clear and distinct perception. Mersenne raised it first (AT VII 124–125), Arnauld gave the classic form (AT VII 214). Descartes: only remembered conclusions need God's guarantee (AT VII 140, 246), and the atheist's knowledge is real but not stable *scientia* (AT VII 141). Readings: memory (Doney), strained because *Meditation* 1 doubts present arithmetic; consistency (Gewirth, Frankfurt), on which reason is shown not to undermine itself; rule-free (Van Cleve), on which particular clear perceptions are knowledge before the rule is proved. This is the path's Stage 3 exit checkpoint; answer it aloud. [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 7; [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md), live debate (1).

**Reconstructions.** The trademark argument (Argument 5; guide (b) is the check) and the circle with Descartes' reply (Argument 7). Continued existence (Argument 6) is carried by its card. Arguments 1–3 get their +1-week touches.

**Cards.** +8: the argument cards for Arguments 5, 6 and 7, and the concept cards on clear and distinct perception, formal and objective reality, the causal adequacy principle, innate, adventitious and factitious ideas, and the Cartesian circle as a problem for the system. Deck at 15.

## Week 4 — Error and the ontological argument: *Meditations* 4–5, Caterus and Gassendi (~5.7 hrs: 4.5 desk + 0.3 media + 0.9 cards)

**Primary.** *Meditation* 4 (AT VII 52–62), short and often skipped; the path omits it, and the guide's reading plan (session 4) says why not to. It answers the obvious objection to *Meditation* 3, that a perfect God would not let me err, by dividing judgment between a finite intellect and an unlimited will (AT VII 56–58). Read AT VII 57–59 slowly, on freedom and indifference. *Meditation* 5 (AT VII 63–71): true and immutable natures, the ontological argument (AT VII 65–68), and the claim that all knowledge depends on knowing God (AT VII 69–71). Then Caterus on the ontological argument (AT VII 98–100) with the First Replies, and Gassendi's Fifth Objections in two selections: his objections to *Meditation* 4 (AT VII 307–318) and to *Meditation* 5 (AT VII 318–328, with existence as a perfection at AT VII 323), and the Fifth Replies to each (AT VII 374–379 and 379–384). Gassendi is long and repetitive; read the selections, not the whole set.

**The guide.** Its Meditation IV and Meditation V sections, and **checkpoints 9 and 11** as the extended set.

**Alongside.** [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md), "The will and judgment," "God, conservation and the eternal truths," and tensions (3) and (4); [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Arguments 8 and 9; [05-conversations.md](05-conversations.md), "Gassendi: 'flesh'"; the entry on [Anselm](../../eras/02-medieval/anselm-of-canterbury.md) for the argument's first form. In Session B, the SEP's ["Descartes' Ontological Argument"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/descartes-ontological/) and ["Descartes' Modal Metaphysics"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/descartes-modal/).

**Media.** *Review:* Adamson ep. 472, ["Less Cheer, More Knowledge: Descartes' Ethics"](https://historyofphilosophy.net/podcast/early-modern/france-and-netherlands/descartes-ethics), on the provisional morality, free will and virtue, after *Meditation* 4. *Optional, not counted:* *In Our Time*, ["The Ontological Argument"](https://www.bbc.co.uk/programmes/b01mwx64) (27 September 2012, 42 minutes), after Argument 9.

**Checkpoints (the dossier's own).**
1. *Why does error come from neither faculty, and what rule follows?* The intellect only perceives, and correctly; the will affirms or denies, and is so ample that in it I most bear God's image (AT VII 57). Error comes from their joint misuse: the will reaches further than the intellect, and I extend it to what I do not understand (AT VII 58). The defect is in the act as it comes from me (AT VII 60). The rule: assent only to what I perceive clearly and distinctly. [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 8.
2. *Gassendi asks why God gave you a faculty that errs at all. What does Descartes answer, and what is the modern objection?* Liability to error is the lack of a greater perfection, and wanting every creature immune to it is like wanting the body covered with eyes; asked where the will outruns the intellect, he answers, in every case where we err (AT VII 376). The modern objection targets doxastic voluntarism: Spinoza made will and intellect one (*Ethics* II, prop. 49, scholium), and Williams argued that no one believes at will. Curley's reply: the method needs only the power to suspend assent. [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 8.
3. *Run the ontological argument, then give Caterus's objection and the reply.* What I clearly perceive in a true and immutable nature can be affirmed of it; a supremely perfect being is such a nature; existence is a perfection; so existence belongs to God as the angle sum to the triangle (AT VII 65–66). The mountain and valley go together only if either exists; in God, existence itself cannot be separated (AT VII 66–67). Caterus, with Aquinas: the concepts are joined, not the things (AT VII 99). Descartes: only the idea of God contains necessary existence, not merely possible existence. [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 9.
4. *Gassendi denies that existence is a perfection. What is the point, and who develops it?* What does not exist has no perfections, so existence is their condition, not one of them (AT VII 323); Descartes asks why it may not be a property as well as omnipotence (AT VII 382). Kant makes it the thesis that being is not a real predicate; Leibniz asks first for proof that the idea is consistent; Plantinga's modal version drops "existence is a perfection." Then say how this proof differs from *Meditation* 3's (guide checkpoint 11). [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 9.

**Reconstructions.** Error from the will (Argument 8) and the ontological argument with Caterus and Gassendi (Argument 9). The trademark argument (Argument 5) gets its +1-week touch and the circle (Argument 7) its +2-day and +1-week touches; keep each to a short recall.

**Cards.** +5: the argument cards for Arguments 8 and 9, and the concept cards on the will and judgment, the freedom of indifference, and the creation of the eternal truths. Deck at 20.

## Week 5 — Mind, body and the union: *Meditation* 6, Elisabeth, *Passions* I (~7.8 hrs: 5 desk + 1.9 media + 0.9 cards)

**Primary.** *Meditation* 6 (AT VII 71–90), the book's payoff and the plan's longest reading, in three parts: imagining against understanding and the merely probable case for body (AT VII 71–73); the real distinction (AT VII 78) and the proof of the external world (AT VII 79–80); then the union, from the pilot in a ship (AT VII 81) through the divisibility argument (AT VII 85–86) to the end of dreaming doubt (AT VII 89–90). Then the four letters of 1643 in Shapiro's edition, about fifteen pages: Elisabeth's objection of 6/16 May (AT III 660–662), Descartes' reply of 21 May (AT III 663–668), her rejoinder of 10/20 June (AT III 683–685), and his reply of 28 June (AT III 690–695). Read Elisabeth before anyone's summary of her. Then the 1645 selection on the good life: Descartes' letter of 4 August, which restates the three rules of morality while reading Seneca's *De vita beata* (AT IV 263–268); Elisabeth's reply of 16 August, asking whether happiness survives an illness that takes away the power to reason; and his answer of 1 September (AT IV 281–287). Finally *Passions* I.30–50 (AT XI 351–370): the soul joined to the whole body, its seat in the pineal gland (I.31–32), how it moves the gland (I.41), and the will's power over the passions (I.45–50).

**The guide.** Its Meditation VI section; its reconstruction **(c)**, written book-closed before checking; its **checkpoints 12–14**.

**Alongside.** [01-life-and-times.md](01-life-and-times.md), "Elisabeth, the *Principles* and the *Passions*"; [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md), "Mind and body: distinction, union, primitive notions," "The passions and generosity," and tension (2); [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Arguments 10–13; [05-conversations.md](05-conversations.md), "Elisabeth: the union"; [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md), live debates (3) and (4) and "Feminist recovery: Elisabeth and the women of the circle"; the entry on [early modern women philosophers](../../eras/03-early-modern/early-modern-women-philosophers.md). In Session B, the SEP's ["Elisabeth, Princess of Bohemia"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/elisabeth-bohemia/) and ["Dualism"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/dualism/), or the IEP's ["Mind-Body Distinction"](https://iep.utm.edu/descartes-mind-body-distinction-dualism/).

**Media.** *Review:* Adamson ep. 469, ["Ghost in the Machine: Cartesian Dualism"](https://historyofphilosophy.net/podcast/early-modern/france-and-netherlands/descartes-dualism), after *Meditation* 6; ep. 473, ["As Rational As You: Elisabeth of Bohemia"](https://historyofphilosophy.net/podcast/early-modern/france-and-netherlands/elisabeth-bohemia), and ep. 475, ["Ariane Schneck on Elisabeth and Descartes"](https://historyofphilosophy.net/podcast/early-modern/france-and-netherlands/descartes-elisabeth-schneck), after the letters and your reconstruction of Argument 13; ep. 474, ["States of the Union: Descartes on the Passions"](https://historyofphilosophy.net/podcast/early-modern/france-and-netherlands/descartes-passions), after *Passions* I. Millican, [4.3, "Cartesian Dualism"](https://podcasts.ox.ac.uk/43-cartesian-dualism) and [4.4, "The Mind-Body Problem"](https://podcasts.ox.ac.uk/44-mind-body-problem) (about 15 and 18 minutes), at the end of the week. *Optional, not counted:* *In Our Time*, ["The Mind/Body Problem"](https://www.bbc.co.uk/programmes/p003k9b8) (13 January 2005, 45 minutes). If the listening crowds the reading, move ep. 474 into Week 6.

**Checkpoints (the dossier's own).**
1. *Run the real distinction, give Arnauld's triangle, and say what "complete thing" does in the reply.* What I clearly understand, God can make as I understand it; I clearly understand mind as thinking and unextended and body as extended and unthinking; so each can exist without the other (AT VII 78). Arnauld: one can clearly know a triangle is right-angled while doubting Pythagoras, so conceiving apart does not show separability (AT VII 202). Descartes: each must be understood as a complete thing, a substance, and the theorem's ratio is not one (AT VII 222, 225). Wilson: the reply assumes that thought is my whole essence, the very conclusion. [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 10.
2. *Why does Descartes rate the divisibility argument modestly, and what tells against it?* He says it would suffice only if he had not already learned the distinction elsewhere (AT VII 86): it rests on Argument 10. Against it, Kant's second paralogism (the unity of the "I think" is formal, not a perceived simple substance) and Nagel on split-brain cases. [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 11.
3. *How does Meditation 6 prove that bodies exist, and what does it not prove?* I have a passive faculty of receiving sensory ideas; an active faculty must produce them; it is not in me, since they come without my cooperation; God has given me no way to recognize any cause but bodies and a strong inclination to believe they come from bodies; God is no deceiver; so bodies exist (AT VII 79–80). It does not prove that bodies resemble my sensations (AT VII 80). Then give Berkeley's reply. [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 12.
4. *State Elisabeth's objection, Descartes' two replies, and her rejoinder.* All determination of motion needs pushing, contact or extension, and the soul has neither extension nor, it seems, contact (AT III 661). First reply: the union is a primitive notion beside thought and extension, and heaviness as the scholastics conceived it moves without contact (AT III 665–668). Her rejoinder: an unreal quality explains nothing, and it would be easier to grant the soul matter and extension (AT III 685). Second reply: the union is known by the senses and ordinary life, and distinction and union cannot be conceived at once (AT III 692–694). Then state Cottingham's trialism and the view that Descartes never answered. [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 13; [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md), live debate (4).

**Reconstructions.** Three first reconstructions: the real distinction (Argument 10; guide (c) is the check), the external world (Argument 12), and Elisabeth with both replies (Argument 13). Divisibility (Argument 11) is carried by its card. Arguments 1–3 get their +3-week touches and Arguments 8–9 their +1-week touches, one short recall each.

**Cards.** +9: the argument cards for Arguments 10, 11, 12 and 13, and the concept cards on the real distinction, the three primitive notions, the union of mind and body, the animal spirits and the pineal gland, and generosity (*générosité*), which [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md), "The passions and generosity," covers this week. Deck at 29.

## Week 6 — Matter and mechanism: *Principles* I–II, *Discourse* V (~5.7 hrs: 4.5 desk + 0.3 media + 0.9 cards)

**Primary.** The *Principles of Philosophy* in CSM I, by part and article. Part I: articles 1–13, the doubt and the cogito restated as numbered theses, a check on how much of Weeks 2–3 you recognize; 51–54, substance, principal attribute, and thought and extension as the attributes of created substance; and 63. Part II, the foundations of the physics (AT VIIIA 40–65 for the articles assigned): 1–4, bodies exist and their nature is extension alone, with the hardness thought experiment (II.4, AT VIIIA 42); 10–18, space and body one in reality and no vacuum, down to the vessel whose sides would touch (II.16–18, AT VIIIA 49–50); 23–25, motion as transfer from the neighbourhood of contiguous bodies; 36–40, God's conservation of motion and the three laws of nature. Then *Discourse* V (AT VI 40–60): the body as a machine and the two tests (AT VI 56–59). *Optional:* the letter to Henry More of 5 February 1649 (AT V 267–279), in CSMK, which [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md) uses for both of this week's arguments; or the letter to the Marquess of Newcastle of 23 November 1646 (AT IV 568ff.) on the animals.

**Alongside.** [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md), "Substance, principal attribute and mode" and "Mechanism: matter, extension and motion"; [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Arguments 14 and 15; [01-life-and-times.md](01-life-and-times.md), "*Le Monde* and Galileo"; [05-conversations.md](05-conversations.md), "Leibniz: force and harmony"; [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md), "Cartesian physics against Newton" and live debate (5). The entry on [Pascal](../../eras/03-early-modern/blaise-pascal.md), for the vacuum experiments, bridges to Stage 3's next row. In Session B, the SEP's ["Descartes' Physics"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/descartes-physics/), and ["Descartes' Mathematics"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/descartes-mathematics/) if you want the *Geometry*.

**Media.** *Review:* Adamson ep. 467, ["Written in Mathematics: Descartes' Physics"](https://historyofphilosophy.net/podcast/early-modern/france-and-netherlands/descartes-physics), after *Principles* II.

**Checkpoints (book closed).**
1. *What is a substance, strictly and loosely, and what is a principal attribute?* Strictly, what needs nothing but itself to exist, God alone; loosely, what needs only God's concurrence (*Principles* I.51–52). Each has one principal attribute constituting its nature, thought or extension, and its other properties are modes of it (I.53). Rozemond finds this premise under the real distinction. [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md), "Substance, principal attribute and mode"; [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md), live debate (3).
2. *Run the argument that matter is extension and there is no vacuum, then give the experimental objection and the reply.* Hardness, weight and colour can each be removed in thought while the body remains; what remains is extension, which is also our idea of space; nothing has no extension; so an empty space is a contradiction (II.4, 11, 16). Torricelli's tube and Pascal's experiments, with Périer's ascent of the Puy-de-Dôme in 1648, left a space that seemed empty. Descartes: a subtle matter passing through the glass fills it. Pascal: nothing detects that matter. [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 15.
3. *What are the three laws of nature, where do they come from, and what did Leibniz correct?* From God's constancy, conserving the same quantity of motion (II.36): a thing keeps its state unless changed (II.37); motion is of itself rectilinear (II.39); a rule for collisions (II.40). Leibniz showed that collisions conserve mass times the square of speed, not size times speed. The identification of space with matter lost to Newton; the rule that physics deals only in size, shape and motion outlived it. [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md), "Mechanism: matter, extension and motion."
4. *State the two tests of Discourse V, and say what the letter to More concedes.* Language: a machine could not arrange words to reply to whatever is said, as even the dullest human can (AT VI 56–57). Action: reason is a universal instrument, while organs need special arrangement for each act (AT VI 57). Animals fail both, though parrots can utter words. To More (1649), he grants it cannot be demonstrated that animals do not think, and denies them thought, not life or bodily sensation (AT V 276–278). Then give Montaigne's objection and Turing's use of the test. [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 14; [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md), live debate (5).

**Reconstructions.** Animals as machines (Argument 14) and matter as extension with no vacuum (Argument 15). Arguments 10, 12 and 13 get their +2-day and +1-week touches, and Arguments 5 and 7 their +3-week touches: the plan's fullest Session C, so keep every touch to a short recall.

**Cards.** +5: the argument cards for Arguments 14 and 15, and the concept cards on principal attribute and mode, matter as extension, and the laws of motion. Deck complete at 34.

## Week 7 — Hobbes, the heirs and the capstone (~6.7 hrs: 5.5 desk incl. the essay + 0.3 media + 0.9 cards)

**Primary.** Hobbes's Third Objections with Descartes' replies, all sixteen (AT VII 171–196), in CSM II or Ariew and Cress: about an hour and a half, two philosophers failing to agree on what an idea is. The second objection (AT VII 172–174) proposes that what thinks may be a body; the fifth (AT VII 179–181) denies we have any idea of God. The replies are terse; read them as carefully as the objections. Then the heirs in summary, through the dossier and the entries on [Malebranche](../../eras/03-early-modern/nicolas-malebranche.md), [Spinoza](../../eras/03-early-modern/baruch-spinoza.md), [Leibniz](../../eras/03-early-modern/gottfried-wilhelm-leibniz.md) and [Locke](../../eras/03-early-modern/john-locke.md), with [Berkeley](../../eras/03-early-modern/george-berkeley.md) and [Hume](../../eras/03-early-modern/david-hume.md) as the next step: about an hour and a half. Then the capstone essay (about 2.5 hours).

**Alongside.** [05-conversations.md](05-conversations.md), "Hobbes: thinking matter," "Forward: the heirs and the critics" and "The themes trace"; [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md), "How the pieces interlock" as the week's self-test; [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md) in full, as capstone raw material; [08-bibliography.md](08-bibliography.md) for what to read next. The entry on [Hobbes](../../eras/03-early-modern/thomas-hobbes.md); the path meets him again at Stage 3 row 6.

**Media.** *Review:* Millican, [*General Philosophy*](https://podcasts.ox.ac.uk/series/general-philosophy), part 2.2, "Thomas Hobbes: The Monster of Malmesbury" (about 15 minutes, an estimate). *Optional, after the capstone, not counted:* Adamson ep. 476, ["What He Should Have Said: the Early Cartesians"](https://historyofphilosophy.net/podcast/early-modern/france-and-netherlands/early-cartesians); ep. 477, ["The Mind Has No Sex: Cartesianism and Gender"](https://historyofphilosophy.net/podcast/early-modern/france-and-netherlands/cartesianism-gender); ep. 478, ["This Gland Is Your Gland: Cartesian Science"](https://historyofphilosophy.net/podcast/early-modern/france-and-netherlands/cartesian-science); ep. 481, ["True Fool's Gold: Pierre Gassendi"](https://historyofphilosophy.net/podcast/early-modern/france-and-netherlands/gassendi); and ep. 489, ["All Power to Him: Malebranche and Occasionalism"](https://historyofphilosophy.net/podcast/early-modern/france-and-netherlands/malebranche-occasionalism). All are in the path's Stage 3 spine anyway.

**Checkpoints (book closed).**
1. *Hobbes grants the cogito. What step does he attack, and how does Descartes reply?* The step from thinking to being a mind. Hobbes: that is like inferring from my walking that I am the walking; acts need subjects, we conceive subjects only as bodies, so what thinks is more probably material (AT VII 172–174). Descartes: "thinking" can name the thinker as well as the act, and he left the thinker's nature open until *Meditation* 6 (AT VII 174–175). Hobbes's real target is Argument 10. [05-conversations.md](05-conversations.md), "Hobbes: thinking matter."
2. *Why does Hobbes say we have no idea of God, and why does the answer decide the trademark argument?* For Hobbes an idea is an image, and there is none of God; we infer an eternal cause and give it a name (AT VII 180). Descartes: an idea is whatever the mind directly perceives (AT VII 181). If Hobbes is right, the trademark argument fails at its first premise. [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 5.
3. *How do Malebranche, Spinoza and Leibniz each remove Elisabeth's problem, and at what price?* Malebranche: God is the only true cause, and minds and bodies are occasions, at the price of creaturely causation. Spinoza: one substance with thought and extension as attributes, at the price of Descartes' plurality of substances and his theory of error. Leibniz: a pre-established harmony, at the price of any real interaction. [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md), tension (2); [05-conversations.md](05-conversations.md), "Forward: the heirs and the critics."
4. *What does Locke take from Descartes and what does he refuse?* He takes ideas as the mind's immediate objects. He refuses innate ideas, denies that the soul always thinks (*Essay* II.i.10–19), and allows that God could make matter think (IV.iii.6). Then say how Berkeley turns the way of ideas against Argument 12. [05-conversations.md](05-conversations.md), "Locke and Berkeley: the way of ideas."

**Reconstructions.** No new numbered argument: the capstone essay is this week's reconstruction. Error and the ontological argument (Arguments 8 and 9) get their +3-week touches; animals as machines (Argument 14) gets its +2-day touch, and its +1-week touch together with the vacuum argument's (Argument 15). Do the touches before the essay, not after.

**Cards.** No new cards. ## Capstone essay (800–1,200 words)

Write in Week 7, after the checkpoints, book closed for the first draft. **The gate relationship, stated exactly:** Stage 3's gate essay is Hume's problem of induction, with at least two replies, one of them Kant's. It is not about Descartes, and no prompt below substitutes for it. **Prompt 1 is built to pass the path's first Stage 3 exit checkpoint**, the cogito and the circle, at essay length. **Prompt 5 prepares the stage's last checkpoint**, which asks for Descartes and Hume as the foils Kant answers; if you run the [Kant dossier](../immanuel-kant/README.md), keep it for then. Each prompt draws on at least three of the seven weeks.

1. **The cogito and the circle (the exit-checkpoint prompt).** Reconstruct the cogito of *Meditation* 2 and say what it secures, using one reading from live debate (2) of [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md). Then state why the circle is charged against the route from the cogito to God and back, crediting Mersenne and Arnauld. Defend one reading of Descartes' reply (memory, consistency or rule-free) against the strongest objection the others raise, and say whether the circle, if real, also threatens the cogito.
2. **Was Elisabeth right?** Reconstruct the real distinction (Argument 10) and Elisabeth's objection with both replies (Argument 13). Decide whether the primitive notion of the union is an answer, a refusal to answer, or a third category (Cottingham's trialism). Then test one heir's solution from Week 7: does it show that the problem lies in the distinction itself, as Elisabeth suggested?
3. **Can an idea prove that God exists?** Set the trademark argument (Argument 5) beside the ontological argument (Argument 9): one argues from what could cause the idea, the other from what the idea contains. Take the strongest objection to each (Hume or Hobbes; Caterus or Gassendi) and judge which survives better, then say what losing it would cost a system whose external world (Argument 12) and answer to the circle depend on God.
4. **Machines, animals and minds.** Reconstruct the language and action tests (Argument 14) inside the mechanism of *Principles* II and *Discourse* V. Do they show that animals lack reason, that they lack all feeling, or only that we cannot tell? Use the letter to More and live debate (5), and finish with Turing: if a machine passed the language test, what would Descartes have to say?
5. **What Kant will have to answer.** Lay out three Cartesian claims Kant attacks: that the "I think" reveals a simple thinking substance (Arguments 3 and 11), that existence can belong to a concept (Argument 9), and that the existence of bodies needs proof from God's veracity (Argument 12). For each, give Descartes' best case and a contemporary objection from Hobbes, Gassendi or Arnauld, and say which claim is weakest.

- □ Run the dreaming argument and the deceiving God, say what each leaves standing, and say which one threatens arithmetic.

- □ State the circle in Mersenne's and Arnauld's forms, give Descartes' memory reply, and set out two modern readings of it.
