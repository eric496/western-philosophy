# Life and Times: La Flèche, the Stove-Heated Room, the Dutch Republic, and Stockholm
**English** | [简体中文](../../zh/focus-studies/rene-descartes/01-life-and-times.md)

Descartes told his own life once, as a story. Part I of the *Discourse on the Method* (1637) describes a boy schooled at a famous college who lost faith in his schooling, travelled, and resolved to rebuild his beliefs alone. The story is shaped to sell a method, and some scholars, Roger Ariew among them, read its narrator as a persona. Firmer evidence is the correspondence, edited with the works by Charles Adam and Paul Tannery (1897–1913, cited here as AT). The first full biography, Adrien Baillet's *La Vie de Monsieur Des-Cartes* (1691), is indispensable and unreliable at once; a section below weighs it. The modern lives are by Geneviève Rodis-Lewis (1995), Stephen Gaukroger (1995) and Desmond Clarke (2006). This file marks what the letters record, what only Baillet reports, and what is legend or dispute.

Descartes is quoted in English from Haldane and Ross (1911–12). The learning path recommends Cottingham or Cress for reading, but Haldane and Ross is in the public domain and can be checked word for word. Where the English hides the original, the French or Latin follows in brackets. Phrases from letters that Haldane and Ross did not translate are given in the original with my own translation.

## La Haye

René Descartes was born on 31 March 1596 at La Haye in Touraine, a small town on the Creuse south of Tours, renamed Descartes in 1967. His father, Joachim, was a councillor in the Parlement of Brittany at Rennes; the family belonged to the *noblesse de robe*, gentry who rose through legal office. His mother, Jeanne Brochard, died in May 1597 after another childbirth. Descartes later told Elisabeth that she had died a few days after his own birth (letter of May or June 1645, AT IV). He was wrong by a year, which shows how little of her he knew. He was raised at La Haye by his grandmother, and Baillet says he was a sickly child not expected to live. Inherited land, sold in the early 1620s, gave him a modest income for life. He never needed a post and never held one.

## La Flèche

Around 1606 or 1607 (Baillet says 1604, Rodis-Lewis argues for 1607) he entered the Jesuit Collège Henri IV at La Flèche, opened in 1604 under the king's patronage. After grammar and rhetoric came three years of philosophy, Aristotle taught through commentaries such as those of the Coimbra Jesuits and Francisco Suárez (see [Suárez and Grotius](../../eras/03-early-modern/suarez-and-grotius.md)), with mathematics in the physics year. He left in 1614 or 1615. The *Discourse* is hard on the learning and gentle on the place:

> "And yet I was studying at one of the most celebrated Schools in Europe, where I thought that there must be men of learning if they were to be found anywhere in the world."

— *Discourse* I, AT VI 5, trans. Haldane and Ross

Mathematics alone pleased him, "because of the certainty of its demonstrations" (AT VI 7). He kept good relations with the Jesuits all his life and hoped they would teach his physics.

## Poitiers, Breda and Beeckman

On 9 and 10 November 1616 he took the baccalaureate and licence in civil and canon law at Poitiers; the university registers record it. His family expected a legal career. Instead, as the *Discourse* puts it,

> "resolving to seek no other science than that which could be found in myself, or at least in the great book of the world, I employed the rest of my youth in travel, in seeing courts and armies"

— *Discourse* I, AT VI 9, trans. Haldane and Ross

In 1618 he joined the army of Maurice of Nassau at Breda as an unpaid gentleman volunteer, during the Dutch truce with Spain. What he did there is not recorded. What is recorded is a meeting. On 10 November 1618 he met Isaac Beeckman, a Dutch schoolmaster seven years older, and Beeckman's journal entry of the next day speaks of him as someone just met (AT X 22; the journal was rediscovered in 1905). For seven weeks they discussed falling bodies, water pressure and the mathematics of musical intervals, and Beeckman's way of treating physics mathematically set Descartes' direction. On 31 December Descartes gave him the *Compendium musicae*, his first surviving work. In April 1619 he wrote to Beeckman: *tu enim revera solus es, qui desidiosum excitasti* ("you alone truly roused me from idleness"; AT X 162, my translation). The tale that they met in front of a street placard posing a mathematical challenge comes from Daniel Lipstorp (1653), who had it second-hand, and the AT editors doubt it (AT X 47–48). In 1630 the friendship broke bitterly when Beeckman let it be thought that he had been Descartes' teacher.

## The stove-heated room and the three dreams

In 1619 Descartes went into the Empire, where the Thirty Years' War had begun, attended the coronation festivities of Ferdinand II at Frankfurt (late July to 9 September), and joined the army of Maximilian of Bavaria. The *Discourse* gives the next scene:

> "the setting in of winter detained me in a quarter where, since I found no society to divert me, while fortunately I had also no cares or passions to trouble me, I remained the whole day shut up alone in a stove-heated room, where I had complete leisure to occupy myself with my own thoughts [*pensées*]."

— *Discourse* II, AT VI 11, trans. Haldane and Ross

A *poêle* is a stove-heated room; Descartes did not sit inside a stove. The town is not named. Baillet says Neuburg on the Danube; others suggest Ulm.

What happened that night survives only through Baillet. Among Descartes' papers in Stockholm was a notebook containing a short Latin piece, *Olympica*. The notebook is lost. Baillet had it before him and paraphrased it; Leibniz copied a few lines in Paris in 1676. Baillet quotes its opening date:

> *X. Novembris 1619, cùm plenus forem Enthousiasmo, & mirabilis scientiae fundamenta reperirem*
> ("10 November 1619, when I was full of enthusiasm and was discovering the foundations of a marvellous science"; my translation)

— *Olympica*, as reported by Baillet, *Vie* I, 50–51 (AT X 179)

Baillet then narrates three dreams of that night (*Vie* I, 80–86; AT X 181–187). In the first, a wind drives Descartes, bent and staggering, towards a college church while others stand upright, and someone offers him a melon from a foreign country. In the second, a clap of thunder wakes him to a room full of sparks. In the third, he finds a dictionary and an anthology of Latin poets, opens it at Ausonius' line *Quod vitae sectabor iter?* ("What road in life shall I follow?"), and is handed a poem beginning *Est et Non*. Still asleep, Baillet says, he began to interpret: the dictionary was all the sciences together, the poets were philosophy joined with wisdom, and the thunder was the Spirit of Truth descending on him. He vowed a pilgrimage to Loreto.

Three cautions apply. This is a summary of a lost text by a biographer with a purpose, written seventy years later. Baillet himself reports a second marginal note, *XI. Novembris 1620, coepi intelligere fundamentum Inventi mirabilis* ("11 November 1620, I began to understand the foundation of a marvellous discovery"; AT X 179, my translation), so the discovery has two dates a year apart, and no one knows what it was; candidates include a universal mathematics and the method of the later *Rules*. And Baillet raises a deflating explanation, the drinking customary on St Martin's eve, only to report that Descartes said he had been sober (AT X 186). What the record supports is this: in November 1619 Descartes believed he had found the beginning of a new science and recorded the conviction as a religious experience.

## Wandering, and Paris

The early 1620s are thin. Baillet puts him at the battle of the White Mountain (1620); no letter confirms it. By 1622 he was selling land in France. From 1623 to 1625 he travelled in Italy, and from 1625 he lived in Paris. There he found the circle that made his career. Its centre was Marin Mersenne (1588–1648), a Minim friar whose convent was a clearing house for European science; for twenty years Mersenne was Descartes' chief correspondent, agent and defender. Baillet adds a scene from about 1627 at the papal nuncio's house: Descartes answered a speaker named Chandoux by showing how any plausible thesis could be argued either way, and Cardinal Bérulle urged him to develop a method that reached certainty. The outline rests on a letter of Descartes; the dialogue is Baillet's. In these years he wrote and abandoned the *Rules for the Direction of the Mind*, printed only in 1684 (Dutch) and 1701 (Latin).

## The Dutch Republic

Late in 1628 he moved to the Dutch Republic and stayed twenty years, apart from visits to France in 1644, 1647 and 1648. He wanted quiet and freedom from visitors; the Republic also had good printers and tolerated Catholic foreigners who kept to themselves. He moved often: Franeker (where he matriculated on 16 April 1629), Amsterdam, Leiden, Deventer, Utrecht, Santpoort near Haarlem, Endegeest near Leiden, and from 1643 Egmond in North Holland. To Mersenne in April 1634 he gave his motto, from Ovid: *bene vixit, bene qui latuit* ("he who has hidden well has lived well"; AT I 286, my translation).

## *Le Monde* and Galileo

His first large work was *Le Monde*, with its companion *L'Homme*: light, the heavens, the earth and the human body explained by matter in motion, with a moving earth. He had promised Mersenne a copy for New Year 1634. In late November 1633 he wrote instead that he had learned that Galileo's *Dialogue* had been burned at Rome and its author condemned; the Inquisition had sentenced Galileo on 22 June. The news so shook him that he had almost resolved to *brûler tous mes papiers* ("burn all my papers"; AT I 270–271, my translation). If the earth's motion was false, he added, so were all the foundations of his philosophy. He kept the book in his desk. Whether piety or prudence moved him is debated; he was beyond the Inquisition's reach, but his standing with French Catholics was not. *L'Homme* was printed in 1662 (Latin) and 1664, *Le Monde* in 1664. In the *Discourse* he told the story without Galileo's name:

> "It is three years since I arrived at the end of the Treatise which contained all these things"

— *Discourse* VI, AT VI 60, trans. Haldane and Ross

## Helena and Francine

In Amsterdam in 1634 he lodged in the household of Thomas Sergeant, where Helena Jans van der Strom was a servant. Their daughter Francine was born at Deventer on 19 July 1635 and baptized on 28 July; the register names the parents "Hijlena Jans" and "Reijner Jochems", that is, René son of Joachim (AT I 395 note; Gaukroger gives 7 August). They did not marry. In a letter of 1637 arranging lodgings for mother and child he called the girl *ma niepce*, "my niece" (AT I 393–394). Francine died at Amersfoort on 7 September 1640, aged five, of a fever with a rash (Baillet, *Vie* II, 90). His father died the next month. Writing to Alphonse Pollot in January 1641, Descartes said that he had recently felt the loss of two people very close to him, and that those who forbade him sadness had only made it worse (AT III 278–279). Baillet calls her death the greatest sorrow of his life. Helena married at Egmond in 1644; archival work by Jeroen van de Ven suggests that Descartes helped with her dowry.

## The *Discourse* and the *Meditations*

The *Discourse on the Method*, with the *Optics*, *Meteorology* and *Geometry* as samples of the method, was printed by Jan Maire at Leiden in June 1637, without the author's name and in French, so that readers using their natural reason could judge it. Part IV sketched the metaphysics. The full version, written in Latin from 1639, was *Meditationes de prima philosophia*. Mersenne gathered objections from Johannes Caterus, [Thomas Hobbes](../../eras/03-early-modern/thomas-hobbes.md), Antoine Arnauld, Pierre Gassendi and others, and Descartes replied to each. The first edition (Paris, Michel Soly, printing finished 28 August 1641) carried six sets of objections and replies, a dedication to the Sorbonne, and a subtitle promising to demonstrate *Dei existentia et animae immortalitas*. The second (Amsterdam, Louis Elzevier, 1642) changed that to *animae humanae a corpore distinctio*, the distinction of the soul from the body, which is what the book proves. It added Pierre Bourdin's Seventh Objections and the *Letter to Father Dinet* (AT VII, Avertissement).

## Utrecht: Regius and Voetius

Henricus Regius (Henry de Roy) became an ardent Cartesian and in 1638–39 professor of medicine at the new University of Utrecht (AT VII 583 note). In December 1641 a student of his defended the thesis that a human being is an *ens per accidens*, an accidental union of two substances, which theologians heard as a denial that the soul is the body's form. Gisbertus Voetius (1589–1676), theologian, preacher and rector, pressed the senate, which in March 1642 condemned the new philosophy and confined Regius to medicine. Descartes drafted much of Regius's reply and then fought in his own name. In the *Letter to Dinet* he described Voetius as one who brought "abuse rather than reasons" to disputes, and recalled how the magistrates had at first reasoned that, if the new opinions

> "were false, there was no need to prohibit them, because in a short while they would collapse of themselves."

— *Letter to Father Dinet*, AT VII 583, trans. Haldane and Ross

Voetius's student Martin Schoock replied with *Admiranda methodus* (1643), hinting that Descartes was an atheist; Descartes answered with an open *Letter to Voetius* (1643). The Utrecht council summoned him, and in 1645 it banned writing for or against him. Leiden theologians raised similar charges in 1647. Regius meanwhile went his own way: his *Fundamenta physices* (1646) allowed that the mind might be a mode of body, and Descartes disowned him in the preface to the French *Principles* (1647) and in the *Notes against a Programme* (1648). The Republic let him publish; its universities were not his.

## Elisabeth, the *Principles* and the *Passions*

In May 1643 Elisabeth of Bohemia, aged twenty-four, wrote to him from her family's exile at The Hague. She was the eldest daughter of Frederick V, whose one winter as King of Bohemia had opened the war Descartes had joined. Her first letter asked *comment l'ame de l'homme peut déterminer les esprits du corps* ("how the soul of man can determine the spirits of the body"; 6/16 May 1643, AT III 661, my translation), since moving a body seems to need contact or extension, which he denied the soul. The letters, about sixty, ran until his death. The library entry on [early modern women philosophers](../../eras/03-early-modern/early-modern-women-philosophers.md) treats her as a philosopher in her own right, and file 04 sets out the argument.

The *Principles of Philosophy* (Amsterdam, 1644), dedicated to Elisabeth, recast the metaphysics and the physics of *Le Monde* as a textbook meant to replace the scholastic manuals. Claude Picot's French version followed in 1647. The *Passions of the Soul* grew from the letters to Elisabeth, was drafted in 1645–46, and appeared in late 1649. On the French visit of September 1647 he met the young [Blaise Pascal](../../eras/03-early-modern/blaise-pascal.md) and discussed the vacuum, which his physics denied. The Fronde cut short the visit of 1648, and Mersenne died that September.

## Stockholm and death

Through Pierre Chanut, the French resident in Sweden, Descartes had corresponded since 1646 with Queen Christina, who in 1649 invited him to her court. He hesitated, writing that a man born in the gardens of Touraine would not easily leave them for the *pays des ours, entre des rochers & des glaces* ("land of bears, among rocks and ice"; to Brasset, 23 April 1649, AT V 349, my translation). He sailed in September, reached Stockholm in early October, and lodged with Chanut. From January 1650 the queen took lessons from him in her library at five in the morning. On 15 January he told the Comte de Brégy that he had seen her only four or five times since early December and that *les pensées des hommes se gèlent icy pendant l'hyuer* ("men's thoughts freeze here in winter", like the water; AT V 467, my translation).

Chanut fell ill in January, and Descartes fell ill about two weeks later. At first he refused to be bled. He died at Chanut's residence in the early hours of 11 February 1650, aged fifty-three, and was buried the next day in a Stockholm cemetery (Baillet). On 12 February Chanut wrote to the Comte de Brienne that the queen had seen him *deux ou trois fois la semaine* ("two or three times a week") at five in the morning, that he had fallen ill a fortnight after Chanut with the same symptoms, and that he did not survive the ninth day (AT V 470–471, my translation). Clerselier's preface of 1657 calls the illness an inflammation of the lungs with fever and has the lessons every day (AT V 482), and Baillet follows him; Chanut, who was there, says two or three times a week.

## The poisoning hypothesis (disputed)

Eike Pies, in *Der Mordfall Descartes* (1996), read a letter from the court physician Johan van Wullen to Willem Piso describing the illness as showing signs of arsenic poisoning. Theodor Ebert, in *Der rätselhafte Tod des René Descartes* (2009), named a suspect: François Viogué, a Catholic missionary attached to the French embassy, who gave Descartes communion shortly before he fell ill, and who on Ebert's theory poisoned the host because Descartes stood in the way of the queen's conversion. Most historians are not persuaded. Chanut had the same illness just before and recovered; the symptoms fit pneumonia and its seventeenth-century treatment; no one at the time suspected poison; and no document supports the motive. The library entry's "he contracted pneumonia" states the majority view.

## The remains and the skull

In 1666 the French ambassador, Hugues de Terlon, had the body exhumed for return to France. It was reburied in Sainte-Geneviève-du-Mont in Paris on 24 June 1667; a royal order forbade the funeral oration, since Rome had put his works on the Index in 1663. In 1819 the remains were buried again at Saint-Germain-des-Prés. The skull was not among them. In 1821 the Swedish chemist Jöns Jacob Berzelius bought a skull said to be Descartes' at a Stockholm auction and sent it to Georges Cuvier in Paris; it is now in the Muséum national d'Histoire naturelle. Inscriptions by Swedish owners claim that a guard officer took it at the exhumation. The identification rests on that chain and on portraits, and cannot be proved (Russell Shorto, *Descartes' Bones*, 2008).

## Baillet as a source

Adrien Baillet (1649–1706), a priest and librarian who never met Descartes, wrote at the request of Parisian Cartesians. He worked from documents now lost, including the *Olympica* notebook, Clerselier's notes and the papers of Chanut and of the editor Jean-Baptiste Legrand; he cites sources in his margins; and for the 1620s he is often the only source. But he wrote an apology for a pious Catholic and loyal Frenchman persecuted by Protestant theologians, at a time when the works were on the Index. He fills gaps with scenes and dialogue. He reports, on the hearsay of Chanut's nephew Porlier, a two-year retreat in Paris from 1614 to 1616, although the registers put Descartes at Poitiers in 1616; and he opens the dream story with a paragraph of mental torments that the AT editors call an introduction written "on his sole responsibility" (AT X 180 notes). Pierre-Daniel Huet mocked the dreams in 1692–93, asking how a melon could signify solitude (AT X 185 note). The rule follows. Where Baillet paraphrases a lost document, cite him as the best report we have. Where he narrates, check him against the letters. Where he supplies motives, treat him as a seventeenth-century biographer writing a saint's life.
